On the Sava, modern Sremska Mitrovica sits over Roman Sirmium. Most of the old city remains below a living town; excavations appear in separate patches when research or construction reaches them. The palace is one such patch; the baths, granary, cemeteries and street grid are others. Archaeologists assemble the city's outline from those separate excavations rather than view it in one sweep. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/)

People lived here before Rome arrived. Sirmium grew from an earlier settlement beside a river crossing and a network of roads linking Italy, the Danube and the Balkans. It became a Roman colony in the Flavian period, but its exact foundation date is less certain than the usual date of AD 89 suggests. The Institute of Archaeology gives 89 under Domitian; historian Miroslava Mirković argues that the grant probably came between 79 and 96, likely late in Domitian's reign, while the *Oxford Classical Dictionary* places the colony under Vespasian. The safe point is the Flavian period, not a year that the evidence cannot firmly settle. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/) [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0) [[5]](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.5958)

The location put Sirmium on major routes. It stood on the Sava, near roads toward the Danube and Italy, and served as an administrative centre first in Lower Pannonia and later in Pannonia Secunda. Soldiers, officials and traders moved through it. Its role as a regional hub outlasted its first century as a colony. [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0) [[5]](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.5958)

Archaeology gives that city a rough map. Excavations have covered 90 larger urban units, and the Institute estimates that the inner city covered about 66 hectares, based on ramparts identified so far. The northern part has the straight, crossing streets familiar from Roman colonies. In the south, the palace and hippodrome altered the plan. Marshes and streams constrained the city's growth elsewhere. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/)

By the late third century, emperors were spending time in Sirmium as they moved between frontier campaigns and the provinces they governed. Under the Tetrarchy, the empire did not have one fixed capital in the modern sense. Several cities became principal residences for different emperors and their courts; Sirmium was one of them, equipped with the palace, circus and baths that made an imperial stay possible. Calling it “a capital” is fair only if we remember that the court moved. [[5]](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.5958) [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780192865236.003.0004)

A few rulers came from the surrounding region, though their birth stories need sorting. The emperor Probus was born at Sirmium. Maximian's family came from nearby, but the evidence does not make him securely city-born. Aurelian is sometimes attached to Sirmium in older accounts; the modern Oxford reference describes his origin more broadly as Danubian. The three names do not make an equally certain hometown list. [[8]](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah19147) [[9]](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.001.0001/acrefore-9780199381135-e-4019) [[10]](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548657359)

Constantine had an official residence here, and the city was one of his bases during the struggle with Licinius. He spent extended periods here, and his administration issued gold coinage at Sirmium. The court's presence meant work for clerks and officers, but also for builders, suppliers and people maintaining the ordinary infrastructure a travelling government needed. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/) [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0)

Ammianus Marcellinus gives one brief view of the city in motion. In AD 361, when Julian arrived, a large crowd from Sirmium's far-reaching suburbs met him with lamps and flowers and escorted him to the palace. The account is political theatre, written by a historian describing Julian's seizure of power, but it also gives a precise glimpse of how the city's outskirts could fill the approach to an imperial residence. [[15]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Ammian/21*.html)

The palace itself came into view in 1957. Excavators found it while preparing a site for new housing. Work stopped when massive walls, a heating system and mosaic floors appeared. That first discovery did not reveal an intact royal compound. It opened a long sequence of investigations into one part of a much larger complex. [[3]](https://carskapalata.rs/)

The palace and hippodrome lay close together in the southeastern part of the city, near the Sava. The palace complex took shape around the end of the third or beginning of the fourth century; the hippodrome was built shortly after AD 315, according to the *Princeton Encyclopedia of Classical Sites*. The long track and its curved end gave the imperial precinct a public face. Chariot races were a familiar feature of Roman city life, but the surviving plan cannot tell us which race a particular emperor watched, or whether he watched one at all. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/) [[3]](https://carskapalata.rs/) [[17]](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0006%3Aentry%3Dsirmium)

The palace complex had several distinct buildings. The Institute has identified a richly decorated residence in the southeast, a polygonal building near the centre and southwestern rooms used for storage, workshops and military accommodation. The complex occupied more than six hectares, though only some of its buildings have been explored. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/)

The palace's fabric changed over time. Rooms were heated by hypocausts, channels that carried warm air beneath floors. Mosaics and painted plaster decorated its interiors; later floors sometimes covered earlier ones. The site operator notes that only part of the imperial complex is open to view today, mostly residential rooms. The rooms open to visitors are a sample of the residence. [[3]](https://carskapalata.rs/)

A separate villa stood at the eastern edge, opposite the hippodrome. Built at the beginning of the fourth century, it had a large peristyle decorated with porphyry, breccia and marble brought from the Mediterranean. The excavated rooms do not identify its owner. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/)

Sirmium's public buildings served more than its court. In the central district, excavators found a large *horreum*, or public granary, and town baths. An inscription links the baths to Emperor Licinius, though the Institute presents that identification as an assumption. Water reached the baths through an aqueduct from the Fruška Gora foothills. These were expensive works, but their users included city residents as well as imperial staff. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/) [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0)

Not every Roman office was housed in marble. A *beneficiarius* station stood near the northwestern rampart, with dedications by officials and military men from legions based at Aquincum, Singidunum and Vindobona. Those inscriptions record Sirmium's links to the wider military network; they do not show that every named legion was permanently based in the city. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/)

The city's Christian history is visible in its burial grounds and churches. By the beginning of the fourth century, the Institute describes Sirmium as an important Christian centre. The basilica of St Irenaeus stood beside a Christian necropolis and was excavated in campaigns that continued into the 1970s. A recent scholarly volume publishes the church and cemetery in detail, including work from 1976–77 and later investigations. Its adjacent necropolis ties worship and burial together; other basilicas and burial grounds appear in the city's archaeological record, alongside palaces, granaries and baths. [[1]](https://ai.ac.rs/en/7729/) [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0) [[13]](https://doi.org/10.1484/M.ROMA-EB.5.124465)

St Demetrius requires more caution. The Serbian heritage register lists remains associated with a church of that name, but a 2026 study re-examines whether the excavated church at Site 59 can actually be identified as the Sirmian church of St Demetrius. The register preserves the association; the identification remains disputed. [[2]](https://nasledje.gov.rs/index.cfm/spomenici/pregled_spomenika?spomenik_id=43874) [[14]](https://epublishing.efa.gr/haemus/en/article/view/38)

Sirmium also gave its name to several fourth-century church meetings held amid disputes over the relationship between the Father and the Son. The synod of 351 deposed Bishop Photinus and issued a profession of faith. A creed associated with a meeting in 357 became another flashpoint in the dispute. Historians do not all count the later gatherings in the same way, so lists of “the Councils of Sirmium” can imply more certainty than the evidence allows. [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0) [[11]](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108613200.011) [[12]](https://www.fulcrum.org/concern/monographs/9c67wm919)

Excavation has pieced the city together over decades. Systematic work began in 1957, after earlier limited investigations. Mirković records American academic cooperation beginning in 1968, involving Denison University and the City University of New York; a separate French-Yugoslav campaign followed from 1973, with work at Sirmium continuing through the decade. The St Irenaeus basilica was part of that later research. The series *Sirmium: Archaeological Investigations in Syrmian Pannonia*, edited by Vladislav Popović and other collaborators including Edward Ochsenschlager, published many of the results. [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0) [[7]](https://findit.library.nd.edu/Record/001822421) [[13]](https://doi.org/10.1484/M.ROMA-EB.5.124465)

After Byzantium regained Sirmium in the sixth century, the Avars besieged it for nearly three years. The city fell in 582, ending Roman control. Mirković separates the conquest from a destructive fire she dates to the following year, so “the Avars destroyed Sirmium in 582” compresses two events into one. [[4]](https://digitalna.ff.uns.ac.rs/sadrzaj/2017/978-86-6065-375-0) [[16]](https://doi.org/10.2298/zrvi2360669b)

Today the national heritage register lists Sirmium itself, not just its palace, as an archaeological site of exceptional importance. Its entry describes the palace, hippodrome, baths, granary and other remains within the footprint of the ancient city. The palace site has a roof and visitor centre, but much of Sirmium remains beneath streets and buildings. Most of the Roman city has to be read from excavation plans, reports and the pieces left in place. [[2]](https://nasledje.gov.rs/index.cfm/spomenici/pregled_spomenika?spomenik_id=43874) [[3]](https://carskapalata.rs/)
