Manasija Monastery sits in the Resava river gorge in eastern Serbia, behind stone walls built for a serious fight. Its church has five domes and surviving frescoes of saints and warriors. Around it is a defensive circuit of towers, with a great keep rising above the rest. The monastery is also known as Resava, after the river, and it remains a living religious community.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)

Its founder was Stefan Lazarević, ruler of Serbia from 1389 to 1427. He began building the monastery in 1406 or 1407, and the work lasted until 1418. Serbia had been shaken by the Battle of Kosovo in 1389, in which Stefan's father, Prince Lazar, died. Stefan inherited a difficult political situation, but his reign also gave scholars, builders and artists opportunities to work. His monastery brought several of those activities together behind one set of formidable walls.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)[[7]](https://bfspc.bg.ac.rs/wp-content/uploads/pdf/std/2012/std-2012-50.pdf)

The title “despot” can sound like a verdict on Stefan's character to a modern English speaker. In his time it was a rank: he was a Serbian prince until 1402 and thereafter a despot. His contemporary biographer, Constantine the Philosopher, described a ruler interested in books as well as government. Constantine himself came from Bulgaria and found a place in Stefan's Serbia, where he wrote on language and later composed the ruler's life.[[7]](https://bfspc.bg.ac.rs/wp-content/uploads/pdf/std/2012/std-2012-50.pdf)

## Counting the towers

How many towers does Manasija have? The monastery's own account gives twelve. The submission on UNESCO's Tentative List describes eleven towers and a twelfth in a separately defended section at the eastern end. Both descriptions get to twelve, though the submission makes a distinction between the main circuit and the outlying work.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)[[3]](https://manastirmanasija.rs/en/fortress)

Architect Gordana Simić's 1992 study calls the monastery fortification a structure with eleven towers, while separately describing a projecting northeastern enclosure with its own defensive tower. In the same paper she quotes an older description that counted twelve. The figures therefore need not describe different buildings: a count may depend on whether the separately defended tower is included. The sources do not present a neat counting reconciliation, however, so it is better to show their distinction than pretend the wording is uniform.[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-XXIV-1992/Saopstenje_XXIV_1992_Despotova_kula_u_Resavi.pdf)

The towers were linked by ramparts. Passages at the level of the fourth storey connected the wall walk and the ordinary towers, allowing defenders to move along the line. The outer face of the fortress also had a lower defensive wall, and part of the approach was protected by a ditch. An attacker who got past one obstacle would still have the main wall to contend with.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-XXIV-1992/Saopstenje_XXIV_1992_Despotova_kula_u_Resavi.pdf)

The stone projections on the towers and ramparts were part of that problem. Called machicolations, they were defensive galleries from which people above could attack an enemy at the foot of a wall. The UNESCO submission counts 104 across the fortification. These were unusual features in Serbian military architecture, and their presence at Manasija helps explain why a monastery looks so much like a castle.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)

The largest tower is known as the Despot's Tower, or donjon: a keep rather than an ordinary wall tower. It was enclosed, unlike the other towers, and had a raised entrance. Simić measured that entrance at about eleven metres above the modern courtyard. She suggested a wooden approach, perhaps with removable ladders, but treated its exact arrangement as a reconstruction rather than an observed fact. The tower itself survives; the medieval instructions for getting through its door do not.[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-XXIV-1992/Saopstenje_XXIV_1992_Despotova_kula_u_Resavi.pdf)

Simić also found signs that the tower had once been lower and was subsequently raised. In 1992 she argued that the changes could have occurred during construction, while noting that more systematic investigation was needed to settle the dating of the fortification's successive phases. A fortress that looks like a single plan may still contain a history of alterations.[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-XXIV-1992/Saopstenje_XXIV_1992_Despotova_kula_u_Resavi.pdf)

## Inside the walls

The church at the centre is dedicated to the Holy Trinity. Its architecture belongs to the Morava school of Serbian church building, with a cross-shaped plan, projecting rounded arms and five domes. Outside, sandstone and architectural decoration give it a different character from the military walls. Inside, roughly a third of the original frescoes survive.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)

Among the surviving paintings are holy warriors with gilded details against blue backgrounds. On the western wall of the nave, Stefan appears in a founder's portrait, offering a model of his monastery. It is an image of the building as its patron wanted it remembered, placed inside the building itself. The church and its fortification have both been repaired over the centuries; their survival does not mean every visible detail is untouched from Stefan's day.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)

A monastery was more than its church. UNESCO's submission identifies the remains of a large two-storey refectory, where the community ate, and smaller quarters. It describes Manasija as a centre of translating and copying. A fortified entrance might keep danger outside, but the books being made and used within were also part of the reason the place mattered.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)

## A school of books

The “Resava school” is not a school in the modern sense of a classroom with a published timetable. The name refers to a tradition of literary work, copying and spelling associated with Resava and Stefan's patronage. Texts had to be copied by hand; copying made it possible to circulate a work, but also gave errors and variations opportunities to travel with it.[[6]](https://manastirmanasija.rs/en/resava-school)[[7]](https://bfspc.bg.ac.rs/wp-content/uploads/pdf/std/2012/std-2012-50.pdf)

A copyist could compare a translation with a Greek text, correct an older version, or prepare another book for use elsewhere. Manasija's account places this work in a wider network of monasteries and scholars. It notes, for example, that the monk Grigorije of Hilandar corrected a Serbian translation of the chronicle of Zonaras on Stefan's order in 1407 or 1408. That work belongs to the ruler's literary world; it should not be mistaken for proof that every book commissioned by Stefan was produced inside Manasija.[[6]](https://manastirmanasija.rs/en/resava-school)

The monastery also names Constantine the Philosopher in connection with the Resava school. His *Story about Letters* discussed writing and the correction of religious books. The University of Belgrade scholar Gordana Jovanović explains that Constantine knew the reform associated with Patriarch Euthymius of Tarnovo and wanted to address differences in Serbian liturgical books. For Constantine, a problem in a written book could be a problem of religious teaching as well as spelling.[[7]](https://bfspc.bg.ac.rs/wp-content/uploads/pdf/std/2012/std-2012-50.pdf)

How much of this was a new reform, and how much an ordering of conventions that already existed, is a question scholars have discussed. Jovanović notes an interpretation that Constantine worked from an existing Resava orthography, bringing order to it rather than inventing the whole system. She also considers Belgrade the likely place where he wrote the treatise; the monastery's account notes that some scholars place it at Resava. The label “Resava school” reaches beyond one room and one pen.[[6]](https://manastirmanasija.rs/en/resava-school)[[7]](https://bfspc.bg.ac.rs/wp-content/uploads/pdf/std/2012/std-2012-50.pdf)

Its models continued to be used after Stefan's death. The monastery's account cites a monk who copied an Apostol, a book of readings from the apostles, from old Resava exemplars in 1638. Another copyist in 1644 referred readers to Tarnovo and Resava examples for correction. Such remarks tell us how later scribes valued the tradition, not that every copy was literally free of mistakes.[[6]](https://manastirmanasija.rs/en/resava-school)

## The grave in the church

Stefan died in 1427. Constantine's biography places his burial at Resava, the monastery he had founded. For a ruler who had prepared a church as an endowment, burial there would fit a familiar medieval pattern. Yet identifying a particular set of remains centuries later is a different task from reading a biographical account.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)[[5]](https://antropologija.com/index.php/an/article/download/277/273)

In 2006 archaeologists excavated in the church nave and found an adult skeleton in a grave in its southwestern area. Serbia's Institute for the Protection of Cultural Monuments identifies it as Stefan's and says the conclusion agrees with the archaeological and historical evidence as well as anthropological and DNA analyses. The institute's account does not provide the underlying genetic report or enough detail to independently evaluate the analysis.[[4]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/english/radovi_i_aktivnosti_manastir_resava_manasija.php)[[5]](https://antropologija.com/index.php/an/article/download/277/273)

There had also been an earlier identification of remains at Koporin monastery as Stefan's. Archaeologist Monika Milosavljević's 2011 study examines the competing interpretations and how the Manasija discovery was presented. She reports that the identification of the Manasija skeleton drew on multiple disciplines, while documenting disagreement over the remains and their interpretation. The finding at Manasija is therefore an identification backed by an institutional assessment, not an identity that a reader can establish from the institute's short summary alone.[[5]](https://antropologija.com/index.php/an/article/download/277/273)

There is even a smaller disagreement over the description of the location: the UNESCO submission says the southeastern corner of the nave, while the institute and Milosavljević locate the find in the southwest. Their accounts agree that the excavation was inside the nave in 2006; the article follows the more detailed excavation discussion for its location.[[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5536)[[4]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/english/radovi_i_aktivnosti_manastir_resava_manasija.php)[[5]](https://antropologija.com/index.php/an/article/download/277/273)

The grave was found in a church whose founder is still pictured on its wall. Outside, the towers can be counted in more than one way, and inside, an identification depends on evidence that has to be interpreted. Neither detail makes Manasija less tangible. It makes the difference between seeing a medieval building and understanding what its surviving stones and records can tell us.
