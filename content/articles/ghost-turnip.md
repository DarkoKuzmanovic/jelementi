In 1943, a schoolteacher sent a turnip lantern to the National Museum of Ireland. Rois Ní Braonáin taught near Fintown in County Donegal, and she told the museum that this was the kind of lantern always made in that locality around 1900.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9)

The object in the collection today is not the vegetable she sent. It is a painted plaster cast made by the museum artist Eileen Barnes. Its official name is the ghost turnip, and its museum number is F1943.350.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9) A turnip would not make a dependable long-term museum specimen; the cast keeps the face when the material it copied could not be expected to last.

The lantern belongs to an Irish Halloween in which a root vegetable, a candle and a willingness to frighten your neighbours could provide an evening's entertainment. It also belongs to a much longer and less tidy story about seasonal customs, wandering spirits and the American pumpkin.

## Making a ghost

A record from the Schools' Collection in Manorhamilton, County Leitrim, describes how to make one. Cut a turnip in two and scoop it out until little remains but the rind. Cut eyes and a mouth, leave room for a candle, and put the result on a stick. Add a sheet over the stick, lift the whole thing high on a dark road, and it could look, as the account puts it, “awe-inspiring”.[[2]](https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4602749/4600891)

It is a practical set of instructions for producing a ghost at very little expense. The sheet supplies the body, the raised turnip the head, and the candle lets the face appear out of the darkness. The same account also describes a cabbage-stump trick for filling a house with smoke, and a game in which players might bite a rotten potato instead of an apple. Its Halloween could be both frightening and rather hard on the neighbours.[[2]](https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4602749/4600891)

The Schools' Collection was a huge gathering of local knowledge, not a single, uniform account of Irish life. Between 1937 and 1939, pupils in thousands of schools collected stories and customs from parents, grandparents and neighbours. The children wrote down what people told them, including memories of practices from earlier years.[[3]](https://www.duchas.ie/en/info/cbe-collections)

That is useful evidence, but it has limits. One child's description of a turnip ghost tells us what was reported in one place; it cannot tell us that every Irish community made the same lantern, used the same name, or believed the same thing about it. Ní Braonáin's information is similarly local: she described the kind made near Fintown around 1900, not an ancient design used everywhere in Ireland.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9)[[3]](https://www.duchas.ie/en/info/cbe-collections)

The museum says candles were placed inside turnips to frighten people on the night of 31 October.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9) The Manorhamilton instructions show how effectively ordinary materials could be turned into an encounter with something apparently supernatural. In that example, at least, the people being frightened were the audience, not spirits being driven away.

## A night at the edge of winter

The Irish name Samhain is associated with the beginning of winter. The National Museum describes 1 November as the first day of that season, with celebrations often taking place on the previous evening, Oíche Shamhna. The English name Halloween points to another calendar: it is the eve of All Saints' Day, also called All Hallows.[[4]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Folklife-Collections/Folklife-Collections-List-%281%29/Religion-and-Calendar-Customs/Hallowe-en-Samhain)

In the customs recorded by the museum, the evening brought food and games as well as fears. Fruit and nuts appeared in the festivities; games could be used to guess whom someone might marry. People also told of the dead returning to their family homes and of dangerous spirits abroad. Masks and visiting groups could give the night a more playful face.[[4]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Folklife-Collections/Folklife-Collections-List-%281%29/Religion-and-Calendar-Customs/Hallowe-en-Samhain)

Those beliefs are part of the documented later traditions. It is harder to say exactly which practices were performed in pre-Christian Ireland, or to draw a straight line from them to a particular nineteenth- or twentieth-century lantern. Medieval Irish stories associate Samhain with assemblies, feasting and supernatural events, but those stories were written by Christian authors looking back at an earlier world.[[5]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/0015587X.2023.2282282)

Historian Ronald Hutton has examined two especially persistent claims: that Samhain was the ancient Celtic New Year and that it was a great pagan feast of the dead. He finds the evidence for the first speculative and inconclusive, and for the second extremely thin. Neither idea is disproved, he says, but neither should be treated as established fact.[[5]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/0015587X.2023.2282282)

That does not make the later Irish customs any less real. It simply keeps separate a winter festival described in medieval literature, the Halloween practices people recorded much later, and the modern explanations we like to give them. The ghost turnip has a documented home near Fintown; it does not need an invented journey through prehistory to earn its place in the museum.

## Jack and his lantern

The National Museum connects the name jack-o'-lantern with a folktale about Jack, who was welcome in neither heaven nor hell and had to wander with a lantern.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9) Often called Stingy Jack, he has more than one story and more than one way of getting into trouble.[[8]](https://sites.nd.edu/rbsc/halloween-2024)

In a version told by folklorist Jack Santino and preserved by the Library of Congress, Jack is a blacksmith who makes a bargain with the Devil. He later uses a series of magical advantages to trap his visitor: a chair from which he cannot rise, a tree from which he cannot descend and a purse from which he cannot escape. In the end the Devil agrees never to claim Jack's soul.[[6]](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2022/10/spooky-stories-for-halloween-2022-on-the-folklife-today-podcast)

The arrangement is less convenient after Jack dies. Heaven refuses him because of his life and his bargain. The Devil, having had quite enough of him, shuts the gates of hell. Jack scoops up a coal and puts it in a turnip to light his way as he wanders. Santino introduces this as one version of a traditional tale, not the definitive account of Jack's misadventures.[[6]](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2022/10/spooky-stories-for-halloween-2022-on-the-folklife-today-podcast)

An Irish Schools' Collection version from County Tipperary sends Jackie back to earth with a lantern and a job: lead other people astray, just as he led the Devil astray.[[7]](https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4922282/4867042/5052363) In that telling the lantern is part of the punishment, and the light that tempts a traveller might be a reason to distrust the guide.

Other accounts use Jack to explain strange lights over wet ground. A story printed in Dublin in 1836 places Jack by an Irish bog; the narrator and his uncle offer different explanations for the lights they see.[[8]](https://sites.nd.edu/rbsc/halloween-2024) A wandering light, a story about a troublesome soul and a candle inside a carved face are related ideas, but they are not interchangeable pieces of evidence. The surviving stories do not prove that everyone who carved a turnip did it to represent Jack, or that one known version of his tale created the custom.

## The pumpkin arrives

The pumpkin has its own American history. A nineteenth-century illustration held by the Library of Congress shows a boy and girl carving a pumpkin into a jack-o'-lantern; the library dates it to around 1872.[[9]](https://www.loc.gov/item/2004667296) By then, the carved pumpkin was already a subject an illustrator could expect an audience to recognise.

An earlier illustration in an American publication, reproduced by the University of Notre Dame's special collections, accompanied an 1867 story called “The Pumpkin Effigy”. The story described children using a jack-o'-lantern to frighten people, but did not present the incident as a Halloween celebration. Its author attributed the pastime to the English.[[8]](https://sites.nd.edu/rbsc/halloween-2024)

That detail complicates the tidy version in which one Irish custom travels unchanged to America, meets a pumpkin and becomes modern Halloween. The Irish contribution is important, and the National Museum calls today's pumpkin lantern an American development of the Irish tradition.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9) But the carving of vegetable lanterns was not confined to Ireland: Santino also describes examples in England and Scotland.[[6]](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2022/10/spooky-stories-for-halloween-2022-on-the-folklife-today-podcast) The records do not allow us to give one person, voyage or community sole credit for the change.

Pumpkins were available in North America, and their larger hollow interiors offered an appealing surface for a face. The old trick of making a light look like a living head could be adapted to a different crop. In time the orange version became the familiar Halloween decoration; the Donegal cast preserves another local answer to the same problem.[[1]](https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/Collections-Research/Collection/Top-things-to-see-in-the-Irish-Folklife-Collection/Artefact/Ghost-turnip/b202e8ea-0728-4b3d-b0f1-82660fe45ce9)[[6]](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2022/10/spooky-stories-for-halloween-2022-on-the-folklife-today-podcast)

Barnes's plaster copy holds still a face that was made for a much shorter life. It preserves the shape Ní Braonáin sent to the museum, along with her account of where such lanterns had been made. The candle, the dark road and the startled neighbours are left to the records.
