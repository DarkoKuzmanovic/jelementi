On 23 October 1862 the Greeks deposed King Otto. He was a Bavarian prince whom the great powers had placed on the throne thirty years earlier, and after failed wars, heavy taxes and years of absolutist rule his subjects had enough. He sailed back to Bavaria.[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Otto-king-of-Greece)

What happened next is the odd part. Instead of simply inviting another prince, the provisional government held a plebiscite. In mid-December 1862, Greeks went to the polls to choose their next sovereign, and the result was about as decisive as elections get. Prince Alfred, the second son of Queen Victoria, received 230,016 votes out of 244,202 cast.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) A second scholarly account preserves the same figure for Alfred with a slightly different total, 240,702, so the precise turnout is a little uncertain, but the proportions are not: roughly nineteen out of twenty voters picked the English prince.[[2]](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/byzantine-and-modern-greek-studies/article/abs/republicanism-of-eleftherios-venizelos-ideology-or-tactics/30251D5F7042FA2C32E58702638FEF06)

The Greeks even had a pet name for him. They called him Alfredakis, little Alfred.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

## Why an English prince

Alfred had visited Greece three years earlier as a teenage midshipman, and he had made a good impression.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) But fondness for a polite naval cadet does not explain 230,000 votes. The vote was a wager on what an English king would bring with him.

The biggest prize was visible on the map. The Ionian Islands, Corfu, Cephalonia, Zante and their neighbours, had been under British protection since 1815, and their parliament had repeatedly voted for union with Greece.[[5]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ionian-Islands) Lord Palmerston, the British prime minister, held those islands as what the historian Douglas Dakin calls a strong card: he had already offered them to Otto on condition that Otto stop menacing Turkey, and Otto had refused, being more interested in Greeks still under Turkish rule than in islanders he expected to be difficult.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

Ordinary Greeks did the arithmetic differently. They forgot, as Dakin puts it, that Alfredakis came from a pro-Turkish country, and convinced themselves that Britain was only pro-Turkish because it was anti-Russian. Once "our Alfred" sat in Athens, Britain would convert to the Great Idea, the dream of a restored Greek empire, just as it had converted to Italian unity. Along with the Ionian Islands would come loans and trade, then Epirus and Thessaly, and perhaps, in the fondest version, even Constantinople.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

There was also genuine admiration mixed into the calculation. Speaking in the House of Commons in February 1863, Frederick Calthorpe argued that the unanimous election of Prince Alfred could only be explained by Greek appreciation of Queen Victoria herself, a desire to compliment her gallant son, and a conviction that the constitutional system as it worked in Britain was the best guarantee of liberty and material progress.[[7]](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1863/feb/05/address-to-her-majesty-on-the-lords) The British press agreed. Haris Dajc, who studied the British coverage of 1862 and 1863, found newspapers and weeklies treating Alfred's overwhelming choice as unmistakable proof of pro-British feeling among the Greeks, and pressing the cabinet to back Greece.[[8]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1298931)

## The throne he was not allowed to take

None of this could survive the diplomacy. Greece owed its independence to three protecting powers, Britain, France and Russia, and the international agreements that created the Greek throne excluded members of their reigning families from occupying it, while requiring an Orthodox successor.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) When the Greeks elected Alfred anyway, the Russian government insisted that the Protocol of 1830 was still in force. Britain proposed that all three powers jointly declare Alfred, the Russian-backed Duke of Leuchtenberg and every member of the French reigning house ineligible. Russia declined. In the end the powers declared the protocol in force, and Alfred could not accept.[[6]](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1863/apr/16/question)

That bald summary hides a season of gamesmanship. Palmerston did favour Alfred, but Queen Victoria objected, so he could not press openly for him. He kept Alfred in the running anyway, because dropping him would have cleared the field for the Duke of Leuchtenberg, the Tsar's nephew, whom the Russians hoped to install on the theory that he was not really a member of the Russian imperial family.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) In Parliament the opposition accused the government of putting the prince forward as a dummy, a man of straw, and letting the Greeks believe in a candidature that was never going to be accepted.[[6]](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1863/apr/16/question) The government answered that Alfred's candidature had at least given the Greeks a point of unanimity that preserved them from anarchy while everything else was undecided.[[6]](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1863/apr/16/question)

The standoff ended with a trade. The Russians withdrew Leuchtenberg in exchange for the formal withdrawal of Alfred, on 4 December 1862.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) By then the Greek government had already decided to hold the plebiscite in response to popular pressure, so the Greeks voted for a candidate who had been removed from the board two weeks earlier, and elected him almost unanimously.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

## Shopping with the Almanach de Gotha

Finding a replacement tested everyone's patience. In the Lords debate of April 1863, Lord Malmesbury listed the refusals: the King of Portugal had been invited first, then the head of the House of Coburg, and both said no.[[6]](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1863/apr/16/question) The objections were easy to understand. The Greeks had expelled their king without having anyone ready to replace him, which Malmesbury considered close to insanity, and no prince wanted to walk into a revolution that might next devour him.[[6]](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1863/apr/16/question)

Palmerston worked down the list. Ernst, Duke of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha, a nephew of the King of the Belgians, looked suitable but made demands that were too exacting, as his uncle Leopold had done in 1830 when he too turned the Greek crown down.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) The French backed the Duke of Aumale, the fourth son of Louis-Philippe, who was engaged to a Leuchtenberg, and Palmerston let it be known that he found the hen bird of that house just as objectionable as the cock.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) Dakin's footnote of also-rans runs to sixteen more names: the dukes of Oldenburg and Aosta, a Prince Napoleon, princes of Sweden, Portugal, the Netherlands, Hesse-Darmstadt, Baden and Carignano, a Bavarian Ludwig, two Coburgs, the future emperor of Mexico, a Hohenzollern and a Holsteiner.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

At last, nearly at the end of his tether, Palmerston produced Prince Christian William Ferdinand Adolphus George, the second son of Prince Christian of Denmark, the heir to the Danish throne, and the brother of Princess Alexandra, who was about to marry the Prince of Wales.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) The young man was seventeen. Queen Victoria had no sympathy for his family and underrated his intelligence, but she did not positively oppose him. Napoleon III favoured him, and the Tsar, deserted by France over Poland, could offer no firm resistance.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

Prince Christian drove a hard bargain before he would lend his son: a formal abdication by Otto, a bigger Greek frontier, a civil list of at least fifty thousand pounds a year, protection of William's Danish succession rights and Lutheran faith, and even a British naval squadron at the boy's disposal.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) A protocol of 5 June between Denmark and the three powers recognised William's election, and the Treaty of London of 13 July 1863 settled the terms. William would reign as George the First, King of the Greeks, a title amended by an August protocol to King of the Hellenes. The Ionian Islands would join Greece once their parliament and the powers agreed. The Greek and Danish crowns were never to sit on the same head, and George's successors would have to be Orthodox.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

## Fifty years of the substitute

The National Assembly had accepted William back in March 1863, and he ascended the throne on 31 October.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-I-king-of-Greece) He was nominated, as Britannica drily records, by Britain, France and Russia, the same powers that had barred the Greeks' own choice.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-I-king-of-Greece)

The substitute outperformed the job description. George steered clear of trampling on the Assembly, and became what Britannica calls one of the most successful constitutional monarchs in Europe.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-I-king-of-Greece) The Ionian Islands were ceded to Greece in 1864, explicitly as a gesture marking his accession.[[5]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ionian-Islands) He survived the feuds of his politicians and reigned for almost exactly fifty years, from March 1863 to March 1913.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-I-king-of-Greece)

He died in the city he had just won. In March 1913, during the Balkan Wars, George was walking in Thessaloniki, refusing to leave while his armies were still fighting in Macedonia, when an assassin killed him. He was succeeded by his son Constantine, who lacked, in Dakin's judgement, his father's experience and flexibility.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse)

There is a coda that the Greeks of 1862 could not have imagined. Twenty-three years after the plebiscite, Greece slid toward another war with Turkey, and the powers imposed a naval blockade to force it to demobilise. The officer commanding the British squadron was the Duke of Edinburgh. His name had once been Prince Alfred, the same prince the Greeks had elected king.[[1]](https://archive.org/details/unificationofgre0000unse) The king they voted for came back at last, with warships, to tell them to stand down.
