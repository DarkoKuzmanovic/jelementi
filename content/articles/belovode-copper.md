Belovode lies near Veliko Laole and Petrovac na Mlavi, about 140 kilometres southeast of Belgrade, on a windy plateau crossed by a spring.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[2]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/prehistory/late-neolithic-and-eneolithic-collection/?lang=en)[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)
The National Museum of Serbia lists it among the Vinča-culture settlements, alongside Vinča-Belo Brdo near Belgrade and Pločnik near Prokuplje.[[2]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/prehistory/late-neolithic-and-eneolithic-collection/?lang=en)
Vinča-Belo Brdo is the culture’s eponymous settlement; Belovode sits farther east.[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

Occupation at Belovode ran from roughly 5350 to 4650 BC.[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)
By 2009, excavations had opened about 400 square metres of a site estimated at roughly 100 hectares, with cultural deposits up to three metres deep.[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)
The metallurgical evidence comes from a small corner of a much larger settlement.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)

The green minerals here did not all have the same job.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
The 2010 study identifies perforated beads made from visually pure malachite.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
The 2021 review describes smelting ores at Vinča-culture sites as strongly coloured, from green or blue to violet, and notes black-and-green, manganese-rich malachite at Belovode and Vinča.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
Native copper, when found in metallic form, could be shaped cold, without smelting.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
The authors reserve the word “ore” for minerals found in secure smelting contexts.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
A green colour alone cannot tell us whether a stone became an ornament or a source of metal.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

Belovode preserves evidence for both kinds of work.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)
Malachite beads and mineral fragments appear alongside copper slag, slagged pottery and small droplets of copper metal.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
Chemical and lead-isotope analyses place the bead minerals and the materials used for smelting in different source groups.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
The researchers interpret that separation as a deliberate choice of different minerals for ornament and metal production.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

The evidence that separates smelting from hammering is in the slag.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
Smelting heats copper-bearing minerals in a reducing environment so metal separates from the surrounding material; cold-hammering native copper skips that step.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
Slag is the residue left as unwanted minerals react with ash and material from the working surface or vessel.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
At Belovode, analyses found copper prills, cuprite, spinels and delafossite in a mostly glassy, siliceous matrix.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
The copper droplets contain branching crystals formed as molten metal cooled.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
Together, the chemistry and microstructure show that copper-bearing ore went through high-temperature extraction, not merely a hammering session.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)

A green fragment on its own would not settle the question.[[8]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1571679)
At Çatalhöyük in modern Turkey, a much earlier piece once described as possible copper slag was re-examined and identified as green mineral pigment accidentally baked during a destructive house fire.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)[[8]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1571679)
Belovode’s case rests on more than colour: the slag’s internal structure, its copper metal, the associated minerals and its archaeological context all point to smelting.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

The first Belovode smelting evidence published in 2010 was dated to about 5000 BC.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
Later excavations added more production debris.[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)
Two copper droplets from Feature 21 were found in spits 12 and 13.[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)
Associated dates place the metal-production evidence in the 49th century BC, around 4900 BC.[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
The droplets themselves were not radiocarbon-dated; the date belongs to their archaeological context.[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)
The same study reports that archaeologists could not identify a solid furnace or smelting installation.[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)
The authors suggest that a vessel bottom surrounded by ash and burnt soil may have been involved, but the working arrangement remains uncertain.[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

The prehistoric mine at Rudna Glava in eastern Serbia lies about 50 kilometres from Belovode.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
Archaeologist Borislav Jovanović excavated its prehistoric shafts and tools.[[7]](https://balcanica.rs/index.php/journal/article/download/216/199/189)
The mine was in use during the late sixth and first half of the fifth millennium BC, with activity intensifying around 5000 BC.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)[[7]](https://balcanica.rs/index.php/journal/article/download/216/199/189)
The provenance analysis does not identify Rudna Glava as the source of Belovode’s slag.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
Where the smelted ore came from remains unresolved.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

A long-standing model treated the Near East as the single birthplace of extractive metallurgy, with the technology spreading outward.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
Belovode placed established copper smelting far from that proposed centre and revived the case for more than one invention.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
Later research makes the story less tidy: copper-mineral use and other practices may have reached the Balkans through contact with Anatolia, while Balkan copper smelting followed a distinctive, potentially independent route.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
Contact and independent invention are not opposites; the evidence requires neither a sealed Balkan world nor a single workshop exporting metallurgy everywhere.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

A later find at Pločnik helps mark what came next, but it is not evidence for Belovode’s smelting.[[2]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/prehistory/late-neolithic-and-eneolithic-collection/?lang=en)[[6]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X0004984X)
An occupation layer there, dated to the middle of the fifth millennium BC, contained a piece of tin-bronze foil.[[6]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X0004984X)
Pločnik is a different site, and the foil belongs to later alloying; Belovode’s evidence concerns the earlier extraction of copper from ore.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[6]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X0004984X)

“Earliest” needs a date attached to it.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
The 2010 authors called Belovode the earliest securely dated copper smelting then known.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
A 2021 review still treated it as the earliest secure example in the current record.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
The closest comparison discussed there was Tal-i Iblis in Iran, whose early fifth-millennium material came from spoil heaps and is only relatively dated.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
The supposed c. 6500 BC smelting at Çatalhöyük no longer counts after reanalysis.[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)[[8]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1571679)
New excavation or better dating elsewhere could change the ranking; these sources support a strong claim for Belovode, not a world record guaranteed for all time.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)

A bead shows that someone shaped a green mineral; a copper tool shows that someone worked metal.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)
The slag at Belovode records a further step, one harder to infer from finished objects alone: ore was being transformed into copper here around 5000 BC.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[3]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10059796/1/Radivojevic%202013%20Archaeometallurgy%20Belovode%20HMS%2047%20sm.pdf)[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)
The exact working setup and the source of the smelted ore remain open questions.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.012)[[4]](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10134145/7/Radivojevic_Ch11_The%20Rise%20of%20Metallurgy%20in%20Eurasia.pdf)[[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10963-021-09155-7)
