The Fitzwilliam Museum's clay house measures about thirty-eight centimetres deep and thirty-four wide. It has a courtyard, two levels and stairs pinched from the clay. The museum catalogues it as a "soul house", a model from an Egyptian tomb, made during the Middle Kingdom. The object record assigns it to the Twelfth Dynasty and gives a broad date range of 2055 to 1650 BCE. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)[[8]](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/Fitzwilliam-Museum-discovers-4000-year-old-handprint)

Turn it over and the story changes scale. A handprint runs across the underside. The museum says the house was carried after it had been completed, and Cambridge researchers have suggested that the impression may have been left as someone moved it out to dry before firing. The mark is unusually direct: a person handled wet clay, and the clay kept the evidence. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)[[8]](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/Fitzwilliam-Museum-discovers-4000-year-old-handprint)

The house is open to view rather than a closed miniature. Its rooms and open spaces are represented in a compact, raised arrangement: a lower courtyard, offerings on its floor, pillars and stairs leading to the upper level. On one side, a hole at first-storey height lets the eye pass through. The Fitzwilliam's record says a wooden or plant-fibre frame was built first, then coated with clay; the frame burned away in firing. The hand-shaped stairs and attached details were added to the same shell. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)[[8]](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/Fitzwilliam-Museum-discovers-4000-year-old-handprint)

## A house, a tray, or both?

The label "soul house" sounds settled, but it carries an old interpretation inside it. In 1907, the Egyptologist William Matthew Flinders Petrie published his excavations at Rifeh in *Gizeh and Rifeh*. He described roughly 150 models of dwellings, found near graves, alongside simpler pottery offering trays. He thought the models had stood above burials and called them soul houses because he believed they had become dwellings for the deceased. [[2]](https://archive.org/details/gizeh-and-rifeh)

Petrie's chapter is still useful because it records where the objects lay, what forms they took and what their surfaces showed. He also makes clear that his explanation was an inference, not a label left by the ancient makers. He noted uncertainty over whether the ka or ba, different aspects of a person in Egyptian belief, was meant to use these models. His practical solution was to keep the term "soul house" rather than claim more certainty than the evidence allowed. [[2]](https://archive.org/details/gizeh-and-rifeh)

The objects themselves complicate the name. Some have the shape of a house or shelter; others are shallow trays with modelled bread, meat, vegetables, vessels or water basins. A channel or spout could carry liquid poured over the surface. The British Museum's Twelfth-Dynasty example EA32610 has a two-storey building at the back of an enclosure and food laid out in the courtyard: fish, vegetables, bread, meat and jars. The museum describes it as a fired-clay offering tray in the form of a house. [[3]](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA32610)

That wording points to what is easiest to see: the models were connected with food and drink for the dead. A liquid libation could be poured over an offering surface, with a channel or spout directing it away. In Egyptian funerary practice, an image or model could stand in for provisions; it did not have to be an edible miniature to be useful. The Met's record for another Rifeh house model notes a front spout and interprets it as an offering table placed on a grave. [[3]](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA32610)[[4]](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544249)[[9]](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA32612)

But it is harder to say whether a house-shaped model was primarily a dwelling for a spirit, an offering device, or both. Museum descriptions preserve both possibilities. The British Museum notes that the old idea of a house for the deceased's ka is now considered less likely than the importance of the food offerings, while leaving the model's relation to actual houses open. The Fitzwilliam, meanwhile, describes its example as both a dwelling space for the deceased and a place where offerings were set out. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)[[3]](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA32610)[[9]](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA32612)

Recent scholarship has questioned whether shallow offering trays and architectural models should be treated as stages in one tidy sequence. Cristina Lechuga-Ibáñez reviews that traditional scheme and argues that trays and house models may have developed independently, even though they share features and can appear in the same funerary settings. Her proposal is one interpretation of a mixed archaeological record, not a reason to treat every object alike. [[5]](https://aedeweb.com/wp-content/uploads/6-CRISTINA-LECHUGA-IBANEZ-POTTERY-OFFERING-TRAYS-AND-SOUL-HOUSES_STABLISHING-THE-DIFFERENCE.pdf)

So the most careful description is not that every "soul house" was literally a miniature home for a wandering soul. These are clay funerary models that combine architecture, offerings and sometimes a means for libation. Their use belongs to the relationship between the living and the dead, but the exact emphasis could differ by object and context. The label is convenient; the uncertainty is real. [[2]](https://archive.org/details/gizeh-and-rifeh)[[3]](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA32610)[[5]](https://aedeweb.com/wp-content/uploads/6-CRISTINA-LECHUGA-IBANEZ-POTTERY-OFFERING-TRAYS-AND-SOUL-HOUSES_STABLISHING-THE-DIFFERENCE.pdf)

## Made around a disappearing frame

The Fitzwilliam house is a lesson in working backwards from a finished object. Its empty spaces are part of the evidence. The builders did not need to leave a permanent wooden skeleton inside the clay. They made a light framework from sticks or plant fibre, covered it with clay, and fired the result. When the frame burned away, the object retained its architectural shape around a hollow interior. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)

The method made sense for a model with walls, pillars and more than one level. The temporary frame supported the structure while damp clay was formed around it. Once the clay had dried enough to hold its shape, firing hardened the covering and consumed the support inside it. The museum's record identifies the frame and the firing process; the precise workshop sequence for this particular object is not documented. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)

Other details were made by hand rather than assembled from a standard kit. The Fitzwilliam describes the object as probably slab-built, with stairs, offerings and pillars shaped separately and attached. The exterior staircases rise to the upper floor. Their edges are uneven; that is not evidence of carelessness by itself. Clay had to be joined while workable, and each attachment left its own surface and seam. The small building was a construction job, not a single press into a mould. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)

A related model at the Metropolitan Museum, found at Deir Rifa in Tomb 72, shows how architecture and offering function could occupy the same form. It has a courtyard, a columned porch, a stair to the roof and a spout for libation. Its catalogue date is around 1750–1700 BCE, within the Middle Kingdom. The object resembles a house, but the opening at the front also lets liquid pass toward the grave. [[4]](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544249)

Petrie compared the models with Egyptian houses, noting courtyards, columns, stairs, rooftop spaces and features that resemble practical architectural details. He argued that some aspects could be copied from real buildings even when the model's offerings were ritual representations. His account is a valuable early excavation report, but it should not be treated as the last word: its claims about origins and function were made before modern archaeological analysis of these objects. [[2]](https://archive.org/details/gizeh-and-rifeh)

## What the handprint can tell us

The underside was not part of the display the ancient object was designed to offer. That is why a handprint there feels like a small interruption in the neatness of a museum object. It is not a portrait, though. The record does not identify a maker, give a name, or report an age estimate. The suggestion that the potter carried it is plausible, but the hand could belong to someone else who moved the finished, unfired model. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)

A print does not automatically tell us a person's age or identity. Researchers can sometimes study the ridge patterns on suitable impressions, comparing ridge measurements across multiple prints and with reference populations. In a study of clay figurines from the Middle Kingdom village of Lahun, Vanessa Forte and Gianluca Miniaci combined fingerprint analysis with traces of how the figurines were shaped. They identified a range of makers and found that children contributed to some animal figurines, while adolescents and adults made most of the sample. Those results come from a studied group of objects, not from a single unanalysed print on a house. [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaa.2023.101543)

Fingerprints are only one kind of trace. On a small Egyptian coffin at the Fitzwilliam, conservator Flavia Ravaioli recorded a fingerprint in black resin, probably left when the coffin was closed after resin had been poured inside. Nearby chisel marks may have roughened the wood so a preparation layer would adhere. The prints and tool marks document different moments of work: handling wet or sticky material, shaping a surface, preparing it for paint. [[7]](https://conservation.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/study-and-conservation-of-a-miniature-egyptian-coffin)

Clay figurines from Lahun preserve still other gestures. The researchers distinguish pinching and pressing from sliding movements used to smooth a surface, and they trace the joins where clay parts were attached. A seam may show where one piece met another; parallel ridges can record a smoothing movement. These details can help reconstruct a sequence of actions. They do not tell us what a maker thought, or whether every mark was deliberate. [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaa.2023.101543)

The hand beneath the Fitzwilliam house is less a signature than a moment in the production process. Someone had to lift or shift a large, wet model without crushing its walls. If the museum's proposed explanation is right, the handprint came from that move toward a drying place, before the frame vanished in the kiln. The clay kept a record of contact that was never meant to be displayed. [[1]](https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/50994)
