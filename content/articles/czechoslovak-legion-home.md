In the spring of 1920, Czechoslovak Legion units reached Vladivostok, where the Trans-Siberian Railway ended beside the Pacific. The European war had been over for a year and a half, and Czechoslovakia had declared independence. Yet the new state lay thousands of kilometres to the west; its soldiers were at Asia’s eastern edge, in a country still torn by civil war. A railway had taken them there. Now they needed ships to carry them home. [[1]](https://www.vhu.cz/en/legionari-vystava-o-tezke-ceste-domu-i-zpet-do-zivota/)

The evacuation carried more than soldiers. The returning groups included officers, wounded and disabled men, military staff, civilians, former prisoners of war, women and children. It was organized as a military operation, but families were travelling too. Many had spent years away from home. [[1]](https://www.vhu.cz/en/legionari-vystava-o-tezke-ceste-domu-i-zpet-do-zivota/)[[3]](https://www.csol.cz/images/Projekty/Legionarsky_smer/LS_transporty_spec_2020.pdf)[[4]](https://journals.muni.cz/dejinyadejepis/article/download/36447/31178/58817)

## A route that was supposed to be temporary

Czech and Slovak volunteers in Russia fought Austria-Hungary and Germany before a Czechoslovak state existed. The first force was small. The Russian Provisional Government expanded it with Czech and Slovak prisoners of war; by 1917 it had grown to a corps, and France hoped to send it to the Western Front. Their military presence abroad helped the independence movement, while Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk and other leaders worked to secure diplomatic support. The Czechoslovak provisional government’s declaration of 18 October 1918 carried the names of Masaryk, Milan Rastislav Štefánik and Edvard Beneš, but only Masaryk signed in person: Beneš was in Paris and Štefánik in Siberia. In Prague, independence was proclaimed ten days later, on 28 October. The Legion mattered to this story, but it did not create a country by itself: military action, organized politics and diplomacy all mattered. [[5]](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918Supp01v01/d846)[[6]](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/cey/article/view/1691)[[11]](https://history.state.gov/countries/czechoslovakia/summary)[[12]](https://mzv.gov.cz/jnp/cz/zahranicni_vztahy/verejna_diplomacie/vyroci_2018/rok_1918/washingtonska_deklarace_pariz_18_rijna.html)[[15]](https://kids.britannica.com/scholars/article/Czechoslovakia/28462)

After the Bolshevik government signed peace with the Central Powers, the Czechoslovak command sought passage east to Vladivostok, hoping to sail from there to France and return to the European front. The agreement, signed in March, promised free passage across Russia. By May, that promise had begun to unravel. [[6]](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/cey/article/view/1691)

The route east ran through Chelyabinsk, where westbound trains carried former prisoners of war while Legion trains moved east. On 14 May 1918, a piece of iron thrown from a passing train struck a legionnaire. Czech soldiers stopped the train and killed the passenger they identified as responsible. The local Soviet detained members of a Czechoslovak delegation sent to explain the incident; the Legion then took control of the city and freed them. The clash widened into fighting with Bolshevik forces along the railway. [[6]](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/cey/article/view/1691)[[7]](https://doi.org/10.20310/1810-0201-2022-27-6-1446-1457)

The Chelyabinsk incident is often named as the trigger for the revolt, but it does not explain the whole conflict. Mironov and Salnikov describe the incident and the organized mutiny that followed on 25 May, while noting that its causes remain disputed. The troops were already dealing with a stalled evacuation, competing orders and suspicion on both sides. [[7]](https://doi.org/10.20310/1810-0201-2022-27-6-1446-1457)

The Trans-Siberian Railway became the Legion’s route east and the ground over which it fought. Scattered units seized or defended stations and stretches of track, eventually holding a long corridor across the Urals and Siberia. Trains, isolated garrisons and supply depots shaped the campaign as much as the advance across the map. In the summer of 1918, Legion victories helped anti-Bolshevik forces take towns on the line, while the planned transfer to France fell away. [[6]](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/cey/article/view/1691)

After the armistice of 11 November 1918, the Legion’s original plan to join the European front no longer made sense. Czechoslovakia was independent, and the men gradually withdrew from the fighting. During 1919 they guarded railway sections, including the line between Novo-Nikolaevsk and Irkutsk, while White armies and the Bolsheviks fought over Siberia. The same track carried the supplies on which armies in the region depended. [[1]](https://www.vhu.cz/en/legionari-vystava-o-tezke-ceste-domu-i-zpet-do-zivota/)[[6]](https://pubs.lib.umn.edu/index.php/cey/article/view/1691)[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1080/09668139408412232)

## The railway, the admiral and the gold train

By late 1919, the Red Army had driven Kolchak’s anti-Bolshevik government east. Czech units were still keeping trains moving, and Kolchak and his entourage travelled under their protection as political power in Irkutsk changed hands. For the Legion’s command, safe passage to Vladivostok had become the priority. [[2]](https://www.vhu.cz/dlouha-cesta-slozite-zaclenovani-do-spolecnosti-navrat-ruskych-legii-do-vlasti/)[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1080/09668139408412232)

A train carrying Russia’s gold reserve also moved east with Kolchak’s retreating government. The reserve had been taken from Kazan to Omsk during the civil war; it financed Kolchak’s government and became a symbol of the struggle for control of Russia. In early January 1920, Czech troops took over the gold train’s guard near Nizhneudinsk. Kolchak had relinquished his title of “Supreme Ruler.” The SR-led Irkutsk Political Centre first held him; after Bolshevik forces took power, they transferred him to their custody and executed him on 7 February. The Legion’s agreement to hand him over was tied to its effort to secure passage east. [[8]](https://doi.org/10.1080/09668139408412232)[[9]](https://doi.org/10.69264/jsssll.28.0_85)

A U.S. diplomatic telegram sent on 30 January reported that the Czechs had delivered both Kolchak and the gold to the Bolsheviks. It was an early report, not a receipt for the bullion. The records separate the handovers: Kolchak was transferred to Bolshevik custody; the gold remained under Czech and Russian guard after the 7 February ceasefire. Chila’s report says he handed the reserve to the Irkutsk Revolutionary Committee at Irkutsk station at 1 p.m. on 1 March. The archival act is dated that day; Chila’s report itself is dated 12 March. [[10]](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v03/d602)[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1080/09668139408412232)[[9]](https://doi.org/10.69264/jsssll.28.0_85)[[13]](https://docs.historyrussia.org/ru/nodes/93328-akt-komissii-po-peregruzke-zolota-ot-01-03-1920-g)[[14]](https://docs.historyrussia.org/ru/nodes/84623-raport-komandira-10-go-chehoslovatskogo-strelkovogo-polku-im-yana-sladkogo-koziny-podpolkovnika-a-chily-komandiru-3-y-chehoslovatskoy-strelkovoy-divizii-polkovniku-l-irhale-o-peredache-zolotogo-zapasa-rossii-predstavitelyam-irkutskogo-vrk-12-marta-1920)

On the night of 11–12 January, thirteen boxes of gold, worth about 780,000 gold roubles, disappeared from a carriage on the railway near Tyret. The thief has not been identified. A 2025 study comparing Czech and Russian archival accounts finds competing explanations: a Czech account records suspicion of a Russian group; a separate reconstruction based on Russian-bank records places the theft earlier, at Zima, and implicates the Czechoslovak escort. Nagayo concludes that neither account offers decisive proof. The much larger reserve was later handed over, and historian Jonathan Smele writes that virtually all of Kolchak’s gold can be accounted for, though a small portion remains untraced. The record does not justify saying that the Legion stole the reserve. [[8]](https://doi.org/10.1080/09668139408412232)[[9]](https://doi.org/10.69264/jsssll.28.0_85)

## A ship was only the next connection

Getting the army to Vladivostok did not finish the evacuation. The Czechoslovak government could not supply enough ships or pay for a global operation on its own. The question reached the Paris Peace Conference in 1919. The eventual arrangement relied chiefly on American vessels, with British ships carrying much of the rest; Japanese, French and Italian ships also took part, alongside the Czechoslovak liner *Legie*. [[1]](https://www.vhu.cz/en/legionari-vystava-o-tezke-ceste-domu-i-zpet-do-zivota/)[[2]](https://www.vhu.cz/dlouha-cesta-slozite-zaclenovani-do-spolecnosti-navrat-ruskych-legii-do-vlasti/)[[4]](https://journals.muni.cz/dejinyadejepis/article/download/36447/31178/58817)

The first transport of combat units left Vladivostok on 9 December 1919. Some ships followed the coast of China and India, crossed the Indian Ocean, entered the Red Sea and passed through the Suez Canal toward Trieste. Others crossed the Pacific to the United States or Canada. From Vancouver, passengers could travel by rail across Canada and sail from the Atlantic coast; another route crossed the United States by train before the Atlantic crossing. A few went through the Panama Canal. These routes offered a way west after a rail journey through Russia was no longer practical. [[2]](https://www.vhu.cz/dlouha-cesta-slozite-zaclenovani-do-spolecnosti-navrat-ruskych-legii-do-vlasti/)[[3]](https://www.csol.cz/images/Projekty/Legionarsky_smer/LS_transporty_spec_2020.pdf)[[4]](https://journals.muni.cz/dejinyadejepis/article/download/36447/31178/58817)

Life aboard the transports had its own routines. The Legion kept libraries, held language lessons and lectures, and published transport newspapers. Some ships also hosted concerts and amateur theatre. At ports, soldiers sometimes went ashore for visits or public performances. The army’s internal life continued aboard ship after the front disappeared. [[2]](https://www.vhu.cz/dlouha-cesta-slozite-zaclenovani-do-spolecnosti-navrat-ruskych-legii-do-vlasti/)

The numbers depend on which part of the operation a source counts. The Military History Institute Prague’s exhibition says 38 transports reached Trieste between December 1919 and November 1920, carrying 72,644 people, including 1,726 women and 717 children. It gives 11 November 1920 as the arrival date of the last of those transports. The Czech Legionnaires’ Association’s transport history counts 36 official departures from Vladivostok, distinguishes 42 ships used in the evacuation, and reports 67,730 people, including 56,459 military personnel and 11,271 civilians. Jaroslav Vaculík’s archival study also gives 67,730 people returned from Siberia. The difference between 72,644 and 67,730 is 4,914; the sources do not reconcile their totals, so the figures should stay attributed rather than being merged into one unquestioned headcount. [[1]](https://www.vhu.cz/en/legionari-vystava-o-tezke-ceste-domu-i-zpet-do-zivota/)[[3]](https://www.csol.cz/images/Projekty/Legionarsky_smer/LS_transporty_spec_2020.pdf)[[4]](https://journals.muni.cz/dejinyadejepis/article/download/36447/31178/58817)

The last official transport left Vladivostok on 2 September 1920. Later transports still carried supplies, rear units, guards, officials, prisoners and civilians. The last official departure and the final arrival at Trieste mark different ends of the operation. VHÚ dates the arrival of the last of its 38 transports at Trieste to 11 November, just over two months later. The ships’ work was not the end of the story. Back in Czechoslovakia, the army had to demobilize and regiments were sent to garrisons; most soldiers had to find civilian work. Some had been away for more than six years. The VHÚ exhibition describes mutual distrust: veterans did not understand the society they returned to, and the society did not always know what to do with them. They came back to a republic that had been independent for two years, but that was not the same as coming back to ordinary life.
