The Danube passes Lepenski Vir where the Iron Gates gorge narrows between steep hills. Archaeologists led by Dragoslav Srejović excavated here from 1965 to 1970, finding a prehistoric settlement on ground due to be flooded by the Đerdap I reservoir. By 1971, the houses and other archaeological remains had been moved to a new terrace about one hundred metres northwest and 29.5 metres higher. Serbia’s Central Register lists Lepenski Vir as an archaeological locality of exceptional importance, entry AN 45.[[1]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en)[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-VIII-1969/Saopstenje_VIII_1969_Projekat_spasavanja_Lepenskog_Vira.pdf)[[19]](https://nasledje.gov.rs/index.cfm/spomenici/pregled_spomenika?spomenik_id=44034)

“Village” is useful, with two reservations. The familiar trapezoidal structures may not all have been ordinary homes, and the site was not one village occupied without interruption for four thousand years. It rests on a small horseshoe-shaped inlet on the Danube’s right bank at Boljetin, near Donji Milanovac. The first inspection found pits with Starčevo pottery and suggested a small Neolithic settlement. As excavation continued, the scale and age of the site changed that picture. The Institute of Archaeology’s 2016 volume records eight campaigns, roughly 2,500 square metres excavated in about 230 working days. The approaching dam shaped the pace and method of that work; the trenches exposed several distinct occupation phases, not a single continuous settlement.[[1]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en)[[5]](https://rai.ai.ac.rs/handle/123456789/1699)

The best-known remains are about seventy partly sunken structures, laid out with their broad ends toward the river. Each had a hard reddish floor made from limestone and a rectangular hearth edged with stone slabs. Their outlines are trapezoidal, a shape rare in the region. People built them into the slope, close to the Danube. The 2018 dating study calls them likely dwelling features; that is a useful description, not proof that every structure served only as a home. Across the river, the outline of Treskavac Mountain is also trapezoidal. The same study suggests the builders may have linked the mountain and the floor plans, an interpretation rather than a demonstrated design rule.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)

The age of Lepenski Vir depends on which part of it you mean. A formal radiocarbon model places the earliest known activity around 9,900–9,280 cal BC, with early and middle Mesolithic traces continuing, though perhaps discontinuously, until roughly 7,480–6,860 cal BC. The model then finds a gap of at least seven centuries before the phase associated with the trapezoidal buildings and carved boulders. “Ten-thousand-year-old site” is therefore a fair shorthand for its oldest evidence, but it is not the date of the famous houses.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)

For the main building-and-sculpture phase, the 2018 Bayesian model gives a 95 per cent probability that it began between 6,170 and 6,070 cal BC and ended between 6,010 and 5,940 cal BC. Dušan Borić’s later review of the chronology also puts the start of the iconic phase in the last two centuries of the seventh millennium cal BC. These are modeled ranges, not exact years; new dates, different samples and different definitions of a phase can shift the boundaries. The figures put the trapezoidal settlement around the Mesolithic–Neolithic transition, when farming communities had already reached neighbouring parts of the Balkans. The 2018 model places the earliest direct dates for domestic animals in the later phase III, around 6,000–5,900 cal BC, and reports no domesticates associated with the earlier trapezoidal floors. Borić’s 2002 reassessment had argued that Neolithic pottery was closely tied to the buildings, which is one reason the labels remain debated.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.2298/STA1969009B)[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0003598x00091833)

Fish remains from several occupation phases include carp, catfish and sturgeon, among them beluga. A 2022 study of the preserved hand-collected assemblage identified 1,347 fish-bone specimens from Lepenski Vir; a combined set from the transitional and early Neolithic contexts contained 937 specimens. Those numbers describe what survived and was collected, not the contents of every meal. The authors infer fishing through much of the year, with heavier effort in spring and autumn when sturgeon and other migratory fish ran. Its estimates for beluga based on surviving bones range from about one metre to 5.66 metres in total length, with a mean near 2.91 metres. These are reconstructions from bones, not a record of typical catch size.[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033822200039606)[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1515/opar-2022-0258)

How much fishing those remains represent is harder to count than the bones imply. The 2022 study linked only two of 393 chipped stone tools specifically to fish processing, and found clear cut marks on eleven of the 1,347 bones. Scaling, gutting and filleting often leave little mark on the bones; fish bones are fragile, and tool traces from processing fish can resemble traces left by other meat. Those small counts say more about what archaeologists can identify than about whether people ate fish regularly.[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1515/opar-2022-0258)

Stable-isotope studies help explain why “fishing village” can mislead. They show that aquatic food made a substantial contribution for some people, at some times, but the proportions vary between individuals and phases. The bones of people who ate freshwater fish can also carry a radiocarbon reservoir offset: the river’s old carbon can make a human bone appear older than the person’s death unless the date is corrected. Researchers now compare isotopes, animal remains, burial context and calibrated dates rather than treating one dietary signal as a complete menu.[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033822200039606)[[9]](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461957104056500)

The most distinctive objects are sandstone boulders, some weighing more than fifty kilograms, brought from the Boljetinska River, about ten kilometres away. Not every stone was a face: the 2018 catalogue distinguishes hybrid human-fish faces, geometric designs, plain boulders and mortars. Some carved faces have heavy brows, wide mouths or incised lines. The museum displays the sculptures and other finds, and the study places many boulder artworks alongside the trapezoidal floors and hearths.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)[[7]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/prehistory/lepenski-vir-collection?lang=en)

Srejović’s 1972 English-language book, *Europe’s First Monumental Sculpture: New Discoveries at Lepenski Vir* (Thames & Hudson), set out his account of this art.[[6]](https://archive.org/details/europesfirstmonu0000srej)[[20]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0079497x00012275)

“Fish-human” describes a modern reading of the forms; it does not tell us what the carvers called them or what they meant. Borić’s study follows how human and animal features seem to shift into one another. Other interpretations connect the imagery with the river, but no inscription records a name or meaning. The stones are carefully carved, yet their purpose remains unclear. Gods, portraits and fish spirits are interpretations, not facts the stones can confirm.[[7]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/prehistory/lepenski-vir-collection?lang=en)[[12]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095977430500003X)

Some buildings later acquired a funerary role. Researchers found burials within or around the built area, including neonates placed beneath house floors; some structures were later used as tombs. The combination of hearths, boulders and burials makes “home” a rough label, not a full account of a building’s uses. The 2025 study of four individuals suggests that at least some houses had social or ritual functions as well as domestic ones.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)[[14]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2025.106214)

Who built the houses is still debated. Srejović’s original sequence treated the trapezoidal architecture as part of a Mesolithic community that was later joined by Neolithic groups. Borić’s 2002 reassessment argued that pottery and other Neolithic materials were closely tied to the buildings, and that the old division between “local foragers” and “incoming farmers” oversimplified the evidence. The later radiocarbon work confirmed that the house-and-art phase overlapped the spread of farming in the region, while genetic studies found both local hunter-gatherer and Aegean/Anatolian ancestry among people buried at Lepenski Vir.[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.2298/STA1969009B)[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0003598x00091833)

A 2022 team led by Maxime Brami argued that Aegean/Anatolian farmers were central to founding the house-based community, rather than arriving only after local fishers had created it. The study re-examined genetic data from 34 Lepenski Vir individuals. That sample can show ancestry patterns, but it cannot represent every generation; the authors caution that burial place does not necessarily identify where a person lived. The argument challenges a picture of local fishing families simply adopting farming, but it remains an interpretation. Genetic ancestry cannot tell us a person’s language or how they understood the place.[[13]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10963-022-09169-9)

A 2025 study connects genetic findings to specific burials. Its authors examined four individuals: two neonates buried beneath house floors, and an adult and infant found together in a pit. The neonates had mixed Aegean/Anatolian and Iron Gates hunter-gatherer ancestry; the pair in the pit had Aegean/Anatolian ancestry. Because no biological relationship was detected among the four, the authors suggest that at least some houses might have served social or ritual purposes as well as domestic ones. In a separate 2025 paper, Brami and colleagues argue that a fully Aegean-ancestry infant from the final phase supports the presence of immigrant families. These four burials show how migration and mixed ancestry appear in individual lives; they cannot tell us how every household took shape.[[14]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2025.106214)[[15]](https://journals.uni-lj.si/DocumentaPraehistorica/article/view/22781)

The dam project required relocation of the excavated site. The Đerdap I reservoir would rise over the original terrace, so a project was drawn up in 1969. It called for an access road, retaining walls, a prepared replacement plateau and a sloping transfer ramp. Designer Milka Čanak-Medić chose nearby natural ground and kept the settlement’s compass orientation. The plan specified a lift of 29.50 metres; the National Museum gives the horizontal move as about one hundred metres northwest. In 1970–71, the Republic Institute for the Protection of Cultural Monuments cut the archaeological remains into blocks and transferred them to the higher site.[[1]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en)[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-VIII-1969/Saopstenje_VIII_1969_Projekat_spasavanja_Lepenskog_Vira.pdf)

The rescue plan included a protective roof, but its proposed suspended cable-and-tent design differs from the structure visitors see today. The present Visitor Centre opened in June 2011, designed by architects Siniša Temerinski and Marija Jovin. VMS describes a roof of about 3,000 square metres, spanning the excavated remains without interior columns. It shelters the exposed floors and leaves their layout visible.[[1]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en)[[2]](https://www.heritage.gov.rs/cirilica/Download/Saopstenja/Saopstenje-VIII-1969/Saopstenje_VIII_1969_Projekat_spasavanja_Lepenskog_Vira.pdf)[[16]](https://www.vms.rs/project-item/lepenski-vir)

A visit from Belgrade takes planning. The National Museum lists access by road on M25.1, by bicycle on EuroVelo 6, or by boat, and places the Visitor Centre in Boljetin near Donji Milanovac. An OpenStreetMap/OSRM driving estimate from central Belgrade is about 189 kilometres and two hours fifty-four minutes each way, before stops or traffic. In practice, set aside a full day. The museum’s visitor page gives group-booking contacts but does not list Lepenski Vir’s opening hours or an individual ticket price in that section, so check directly before setting out.[[17]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/visit-us/?lang=en)[[18]](https://www.openstreetmap.org/directions?engine=fossgis_osrm_car&route=44.8153318%2C20.4456588%3B44.4629164%2C22.1555518)

At the visitor centre, the old floor plans are visible beneath a roof on the plateau set aside for them. The original terrace now lies under the reservoir. The move preserved the settlement’s layout, not a definitive answer to who built the houses or what the carved stones meant.[[1]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en)[[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-31884-7)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.2298/STA1969009B)
