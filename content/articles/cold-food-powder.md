The prescription came with a way of life attached. Swallow the powder, and afterwards eat only cold food. Wash your hands and feet in cold water. Undress, bathe in more cold water, and wait for the heat to rise and break. Then get dressed in loose robes, put on wooden clogs, and walk. Keep walking until you sweat the heat out. Get any of this wrong, the physicians warned, and the powder would repay you with ulcers, madness or death.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

The powder was called Five-Stone Powder, after its clutch of mineral ingredients. It was also known as the Great Powder, and as Cold-Food Powder, after the diet its users were stuck with. From the third century until the late Tang period, it was the fashionable drug of the Chinese literati: not just a cure for illness but a tonic that promised to invigorate the body and illuminate the mind. A fifth-century formulary summed up its reputation in a single line: "Since the beginning, the Great Powder has been a medicine hard to manage."[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

## The beautiful man who started it

The craze is usually blamed on He Yan, a scholar-official of the third century. The fifth-century anecdote collection Tales of the World preserves his review: "Ingestion of Five-Stone Powder not only cures sickness but also makes one feel that the spirit is open and bright." A physician's commentary adds the backstory: the formula went back to the Han dynasty but was rarely used, until He Yan discovered its marvelous effects and inaugurated the fashion.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)[[5]](https://press.umich.edu/Books/S/Shih-shuo-Hsin-yue)

He Yan lived as the Han dynasty fell apart and three rival kingdoms split China between them, the Wei, Shu-Han and Wu, from 220 to 280.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/event/Three-Kingdoms-ancient-kingdoms-China) Raised in the Wei palace, he became a leading mind of Mysterious Learning, a philosophy that revisited old Daoist texts and traced everything in the cosmos back to a spontaneous, inscrutable nothingness. When a court rivalry got him executed in the middle of the third century, his followers drew the practical conclusion and withdrew from politics into lives of cultivated spontaneity.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

That is the milieu the powder belonged to. The Seven Worthies of the Bamboo Grove, a group of third-century scholars, essayists and poets, made a performance of defying convention: wild drinking, loud singing, mocking funeral rituals, walking around naked. One of them, Ruan Ji, steamed a fat boar and drank immoderately at his own mother's funeral, against every Confucian rule of mourning. Another, Liu Ling, was found naked in his room after heavy drinking and told his reproachful visitors: "I take heaven and earth as my house, and the rooms of that house as my clothes and pants. Why do you people enter my pants?"[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

There is no direct evidence that these particular iconoclasts took the powder. But in a celebrated lecture in the early twentieth century, the writer Lu Xun argued that much of the era's famous eccentricity was the powder talking: users walked around naked to vent the heat, wore loose clothes and wooden sandals so as not to chafe the ulcers the drug raised, and suffered short tempers, agitation and worse. Lu Xun may have exaggerated the dose of weirdness he attributed to the drug, but he put his finger on its social life: taking the powder was part of an uninhibited style that the elite found irresistible.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

## A medicine people swore by

It is easy to file the powder under decadence and move on. The sources will not let you. Most of the writing about it is about healing, and some of it is touchingly specific. The third-century scholar Ji Han composed a rhapsodic poem about how the powder had cured his infant son of violent vomiting and diarrhea when every other formula failed: it "saved him from the severe trouble and restored his spirit afresh."[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

The great calligrapher Wang Xizhi, who lived from 303 to 361, wrote about the powder the way people write about a difficult colleague: with feeling, and at length. In letters to relatives and friends he reported that sometimes it markedly improved his chronic ailments and sometimes the released heat left him in pain and distress. The letters also show that his family members and acquaintances were taking it too, comparing experiences by post as they tried to work out the best way to handle the stuff.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

The professionals were enthusiastic. The fifth-century physician Qin Chengzu called it "the best of medicinal preparations and the leader of all formulas," second to none for the enhancement of life. A Buddhist monk from the north named Huiyi went further: "Five-Stone Powder is among the superior drugs. It can prolong life and harmonize one's inner nature. How could it be that it only cures sickness?" Quite a few monks of the period took it or wrote instructions for its use, drawn perhaps by the promise of a purified mind in a nourished body. One friend's gift of a related preparation, a Five-Color Stone Paste Powder, reportedly left Wang Xizhi feeling so light he moved as though flying.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

## Rocks, ground up

What exactly were they swallowing? There was no single recipe; multiple formulas circulated under the name across the centuries. A typical one named a subset of the following minerals: amethyst, quartz, red clay, stalactite, limonite, sulfur, and either kalinite or arsenolite. The medical literature classified most of these as warming drugs. Arsenolite, an arsenic ore, was considered a mineral of great heat: properly prepared, it could dissolve frigid stagnation in the body, or so the theory went.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

The arsenic probably was not there from the start. The two earliest candidate precursors, preserved in a Han medical text, contain no poisonous minerals at all, only ordinary heating ingredients plus a few strong herbs. An eighth-century variant of one of them adds two more minerals, stalactite and arsenolite, which looks like the moment the recipe turned dangerous. The physician Tao Hongjing had warned back in the fifth century that arsenolite had to be refined by fire for a hundred days and taken in doses of about two-tenths of a gram, and that taken raw "it kills people and the hundred beasts." To make matters worse, the character for harmless kalinite was sometimes written interchangeably with the character for arsenolite, so arsenic may have slipped into batches more often than anyone intended. Several powder symptoms read like arsenic poisoning: gut pain, limb pain, failing vision, skin eruptions, convulsions, mental disorder. Arsenic could also temporarily improve the complexion, revitalize the body and act as a potent aphrodisiac, which fits the devotees' reputation, He Yan's included, for sexual indulgence. One seventh-century medical text describes habitual users developing a condition of prolonged erections and seminal leakage that caused "all kinds of trouble" as they aged, a case of temporary pleasure laying down lasting pain.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

Two origin stories competed. One historian reconstructed He Yan as the inventor, combining the two Han precursor formulas into a tonic for exhaustion brought on by sexual indulgence. The dynastic History of the Jin instead credits a talented physician named Jin Shao, whose formula delighted the scholar-officials of his day. The first story explains a fashion; the second advertises a cure. Both may say more about their tellers than about the laboratory, if there ever was one.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

## How to walk off a fire

The strange thing about this drug is that swallowing it was only the beginning. What followed was a regime, and the regime was the treatment. The heat had to be activated, released and channeled out, in that order, under close attention to what the body was feeling. The word the writers use for the activation is *fa*, whose root meaning is to shoot an arrow. Unshot heat, left sitting inside, was what caused the damage.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

A fifth-century physician named Chen Yanzhi explained why minerals were so tricky. Herbs manifest their force quickly and are easy to subdue; minerals manifest slowly and are hard to subdue. A sick person, depleted in blood and vital energy, cannot potentiate the minerals at all, so they sit there cold as ice. The user feels nothing, takes more, and concludes the drug has no force. Then the accumulated doses all go off at once. In Chen's neat formulation, the minerals' material is cold but their nature is warming, and mistaking the first for the second was how people got hurt.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

This is where the walking came in, and where the physicians started arguing. Huangfu Mi, writing in the third century, prescribed the cooling method that gave the drug its Cold-Food name: after three doses, wash the hands and feet in cold water to stimulate the drug's energy, then strip, bathe cold, and let the cooling open the heart and brighten the spirit until every illness is cured. He adjusted the method by age and constitution, cut the dose for the young and the old, and told the already hale to leave the powder alone.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

His contemporary Cao Xi, an aristocrat of Wei, swore by the opposite. In his Formulas That Disperse Cold-Food Powder he told users to put on more clothes and walk hard enough to sweat, carrying the heat out through the skin, and warned that weak constitutions dosed with cold water would come down with cold-damage disorders. He had forty years of personal use behind the advice.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

Later physicians mostly concluded that both men had killed people by being too sure. Tao Hongjing noted the body count of rigid method: men who poured two hundred jugs of cold water over themselves and dropped lifeless, and men who shut themselves in a narrow room with fires at four corners to sweat it out and died on the spot. Tend the body according to the situation, he advised; copying past examples blindly was worse than doing nothing. A Buddhist monk named Daohong worked out an elaborate scheme of mineral-and-herb pairs, each aimed at an organ with its own alarming sensations and its own rescue decoction, which Chen Yanzhi attacked as contradicted by the pharmacological books. The Sui and Tang imperial bibliographies list some two dozen treatises on the powder and the injuries it caused. Nobody in this literature proposes the obvious modern option of not taking it. The entire debate is about how.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

The margin for error was real in both directions. Pei Xiu, a statesman of the Western Jin, was killed by having so much cold water poured over his dosed body that it extinguished his vital heat. General Fang Boyu of the Southern Qi survived the same ordeal and came out the other side: after scores of useless doses left him shivering in thick summer clothes, the physician Xu Sibo waited for winter, had him held down naked on a stone, and poured on cold water until he passed out. Under family protests, Xu ordered more water, until warm energy rose from the patient's back and he sat up demanding a cold drink. From then on he radiated vital heat, and grew plump and strong. Same brutal therapy, opposite endings. Dispersing the powder was a delicate matter.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

## Ulcers, madness and excuses

The casualty reports accumulate across five centuries. Huangfu Mi listed his contemporaries' fates the way a village remembers a bad winter: Wang Liangfu of the east grew ulcers on his back, Xin Changxu of the west was tormented by festers along his spine, six relatives of Zhao Gonglie of the southwest died, and Huangfu's own cousin suffered the shrinking of his tongue into his throat.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

Power did not protect anyone. In 409 the emperor Daowu of the Northern Wei fell ill and took the powder. He turned erratic, swinging between rage and joy, talking to himself without pause, going days without food, and executing officials on whim until his frightened son murdered him. Three centuries later the rebel general An Lushan, who tore the Tang empire apart in his rebellion of 755 and was murdered in 757, suffered mental instability and physical agonies from the powder on his way to a premature death.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/An-Lushan)

Huangfu Mi knew the drug from the inside. At thirty-five a wind-induced malady paralyzed half his body. The powder did not cure it; it added choking, sudden fevers and chills, insomnia, terror and confusion. At one point he tried to stab himself and was stopped by his family. He steadied himself on cold food and cool water and lived, but never fully recovered. Then he put the suffering to use: summoned repeatedly to serve at the emperor's court, a duty a committed recluse dreaded, he submitted a memorial describing nineteen years of the original illness compounded by seven years of powder damage, and the emperor gave up on him. The detail is too precise to be invention and too convenient to be innocent.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

Others skipped the suffering and went straight to the performance. Summoned by a rebel leader, the scholar-official He Xun dosed himself, disheveled his hair and bared his body in public, raving convincingly enough to be dismissed as unfit for service. An unruly body, visibly triggered by the drug, could no longer occupy its political slot. The powder opened a space where misbehavior counted as chemistry.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

## The long comedown

By the Tang dynasty, which ruled China from 618 to 907, the argument had moved on from how to take the powder to what it was for.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tang-dynasty) The great physician Sun Simiao embodies the shift, and contradicts himself in ways that puzzled scholars until someone read him carefully. In his earlier Essential Formulas he denounces the powder outright: he would rather swallow gelsemium, a known deadly poison, and urges readers to burn any copy of the recipe on sight. In his later Supplement he includes a near-identical formula under the sunny name of Life-Restoring Five-Stone Powder.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

The contradiction dissolves on inspection. The denunciation sits in a chapter on countering mineral poisons, where Sun cheerfully admits that some minerals, stalactite in particular, are superb life-nourishers; he once ate some two hundred grams of the stuff himself in his late thirties and felt the benefit for years. The Life-Restoring formula is reserved for the desperate: the depleted, the bedridden, the paralyzed, the nearly dead. Healthy people cultivating longevity are told, in effect, to put it down and step away. Sun's fury was never about the powder being poison. It was about healthy enthusiasts taking a last-resort medicine as a lifestyle tonic, and paying for the confusion with their lives.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

Something larger was happening underneath. Between the Era of Division and the early Tang, plain stalactite had quietly replaced Five-Stone Powder as the fashionable life-cultivation tonic. The pharmacologists classed it as free of the dangerous potency, though Sun still fenced it with rules: nobody already plump and replete, nobody under thirty, and the older you were the more often you could dose, up to once a year past seventy. The hard drug was being demoted to the emergency shelf while a milder mineral took over daily use. Difficulty may have done what denunciation could not: a medicine that demanded days of supervised heat management, with death as the penalty for misjudgment, was always going to lose to simpler alternatives. By the late Tang, the five-century fashion was over.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)

It is tempting to call the powder China's opium, and some modern scholars have. The historian of the drug urges us not to. Opium sedates; the powder stimulated. Opium enslaves the body; no ingredient of the powder was physically addictive and no source shows habitual dosing, though the social hunger to follow the fashion was addictive enough. Opium flooded every layer of nineteenth-century society; the powder, complex and demanding and costly in time and attention, stayed an elite affair. Above all, opium was condemned without remainder, while most medieval physicians valued the powder even as they catalogued its horrors. They blamed the hand, not the tool, and argued about technique for five hundred years, until the fashion faded and the walking stopped.[[1]](https://uw.manifoldapp.org/read/healing-with-poisons/section/2cbf855f-df45-49e1-b494-e3e39999ef2a)
