Kalemegdan has a monument to a set of keys. The Memorial for the Delivery of Keys (*Spomen-obeležje predaje ključeva*) is the work of the sculptor Mihailo Paunović. It was put up in 1967, a hundred years after the event it marks. The spot where the keys actually changed hands is a few metres behind it: a small earthen mound with thujas planted around it.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD-%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B6%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BA%D1%99%D1%83%D1%87%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The handover was symbolic, and it involved more than one city. In the spring of 1867 the commander of Belgrade Fortress handed the keys of the towns to Prince Mihailo Obrenović.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD-%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B6%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BA%D1%99%D1%83%D1%87%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B0/?lang=en)[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf) The six cities whose keys were returned, Šabac, Belgrade, Smederevo, Kladovo, Užice and Soko, are named on the pedestal of the Prince Mihailo Monument (*Spomenik knezu Mihailu*) in Republic Square (*Trg republike*).[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD-%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B6%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BA%D1%99%D1%83%D1%87%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B0/?lang=en)

Getting there took two uprisings, half a century in which a Serbian town lived beneath an Ottoman fortress, and a quarrel at a fountain that ended up in front of the great powers of Europe.

## Janissaries in charge

By the late eighteenth century, Ottoman rule in Serbia was coming apart. In the north, the local Janissaries, the elite corps of the Ottoman army, were virtually beyond the control of the Porte, the sultan's government in Constantinople. What they took from the population had passed from taxes to open plunder.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje)

[Part 3](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-3-the-field-before-the-fortress) closed with an attempt to deal with them. Under the Treaty of Sistova of 1791, which returned Belgrade to the Ottomans, the Janissaries were forbidden to enter the Belgrade Pashaluk, the province governed from the city.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) That treaty and the Treaty of Jassy of 1792 also promised the Serb population other rights. None of it was fully respected, and the region sank steadily into disorder.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule) At the beginning of the nineteenth century the commander of Belgrade, Hadji Mustafa Pasha, was assassinated, and the Janissaries took over the city and the surrounding villages.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The fortress's official history names two causes of what came next. One was the Janissaries' reign of terror. The other was the killing of Serbian local leaders, the knezes, remembered as the slaughter of the knezes (*seča knezova*).[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/) Together, in the history's words, they "led to the awakening of a national consciousness and the First Serbian Uprising in 1804".[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

## The First Serbian Uprising

The uprising broke out in Šumadija, the region south of Belgrade. Its leader was Đorđe Petrović, known as Karađorđe, "Black George", a successful pig trader who had served with the Austrians in their war against the Turks.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje)

At first the rebels and the sultan had the same enemy. The Janissaries were defeated quickly, with the tacit approval of Sultan Selim III, who regarded them as rebels. The Serbs then asked for local autonomy, and when Selim refused, Karađorđe launched a war of independence in 1805.[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje)

At the end of 1806 the insurgents took the town, and at the beginning of 1807 the fortress.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) From 1807 until 1813 Belgrade was the Serbian capital.[[7]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belgrade) In 1808 the State Council gave Serbia its first constitution and declared Karađorđe the "first and supreme Serbian hereditary leader".[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje)

That year, too, Dositej Obradović, one of the leading intellectuals of his time, founded the Great School (*Velika škola*) in Belgrade. It grew into the University of Belgrade.[[8]](https://arhiva.rect.bg.ac.rs/en/study-in-belgrade/about-ub.php)

The rest depended on the great powers. When Russia went to war with Turkey, the Serbs gained a powerful ally, but the Russians sent only a token force.[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje) Then, threatened by Napoleon's invasion in 1812, Tsar Alexander I made a hasty peace with the Turks at Bucharest. It left Serbia with little more than paper guarantees of autonomy.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje)

The sultan's forces were now free, and they invaded Serbia from three sides. By 1813 all opposition had been crushed. Karađorđe, sick with typhus and broken in spirit, fled to Austria.[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje) The Turks ruled the fortress again,[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) and their return brought a widespread reign of terror.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)

## The dungeon by the river

The fortress had its own prison for these years: Nebojša Tower (*Kula Nebojša*), the octagonal cannon tower on the Danube bank that appeared in part two. Until the end of the eighteenth century it guarded the entrance to the Lower Town's port. After that it became a notorious dungeon.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%98%D1%88%D0%B0/?lang=en)

Its best-known prisoners came before the uprising. In the spring of 1798 Rigas Feraios, a Greek patriot who fought for the liberation of the Balkan peoples from the Turks, was imprisoned in the tower and killed there. Two years later Metodije, the Metropolitan of Belgrade and the city's senior Orthodox bishop, was executed in the same tower.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%98%D1%88%D0%B0/?lang=en)

After Karađorđe's uprising collapsed, many Serbs were held there as slaves. In the summer of 1815 one of them was Jevrem Obrenović, brother of Miloš Obrenović, who led the next uprising.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%98%D1%88%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The tower was renovated in 2010 with a large donation from the Greek government. Inside, on a ground floor and three upper floors, there is now a small cultural centre. Its exhibition covers the history of the tower and the life and work of Rigas Feraios, and one section is given to the First and Second Serbian Uprisings.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%98%D1%88%D0%B0/?lang=en)

## A Serbian town under an Ottoman fortress

The second uprising began in April 1815, when the Christian population rose again in self-defence.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule) It was led by Miloš Obrenović, a knez, who gathered his followers at Takovo.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[10]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milos) The rebels drove the Turks out of a wide area of northern Serbia. Faced with renewed Russian intervention after Napoleon's defeat, the Porte made several important concessions. The Serbs could keep their arms, run much of their local administration and hold their own assembly.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)

In December 1815 the Ottomans recognised Miloš as prince of Serbia, and in 1817 the national assembly, the Skupština, declared him hereditary prince.[[10]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milos) This was still autonomy, not independence: Serbia remained an Ottoman principality, with a resident pasha and Turkish garrisons in its principal towns.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule) Belgrade was one of those towns.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The two leaders of the uprisings did not share the country for long. When Karađorđe returned from exile in 1817, he was murdered, and Miloš was suspected of a part in it. The feud between their families, the Karađorđević and Obrenović dynasties, divided Serbian society for the rest of the century.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Karadjordje)

In 1830 the Ottoman government granted the Serbian principality full autonomy and gave the Serbian church independent status, and now the Ottomans, too, recognised Miloš as hereditary prince.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule) The document was a decree of the sultan, known in Serbian as the *hatišerif*.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

The same decree ordered Muslim civilians to leave Serbia, except for a few towns with fortresses. Belgrade was one of them, so its Muslims stayed on in the town below the fortress for another three decades.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

Miloš himself was forced to abdicate in 1839.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule) His son Mihailo became prince that year, after the death of his elder brother, Milan,[[11]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-III-prince-of-Serbia) and in 1841, during this first reign, the capital moved from Kragujevac to Belgrade.[[12]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Kragujevac)[[13]](https://arhiva.rect.bg.ac.rs/en/university/history.php) The reign did not last. After a revolt in 1842 Mihailo fled into exile, and the assembly elected Alexander, Karađorđe's son, as prince. Alexander was deposed in turn, and the aged Miloš was called back.[[11]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-III-prince-of-Serbia)[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)

Whoever was prince, the arrangement on the hill stayed the same: an Ottoman pasha and his garrison held the fortress above the town.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

## The Čukur Fountain

Mihailo returned to the throne in 1860, after his father's death, and continued the work of consolidating the state and modernising its administration.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule)[[11]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-III-prince-of-Serbia) He reformed the courts, revised the electoral laws and, in 1861, introduced a regular conscript army, for which Russia furnished supplies.[[11]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-III-prince-of-Serbia)

The crisis began in the street. On 15 June 1862 a quarrel at the Čukur Fountain (*Čukur česma*), a public fountain in the town, ended in the killing of a Serb.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

Accounts differed from the start. According to one, recorded by the German historian Leopold von Ranke, it began as an argument between Muslims and Orthodox Christians over who would be first to take water. Serbian police arrested two Ottoman soldiers suspected of the killing. Local Muslims then started shooting, which drew in Serbian and Ottoman officials and the foreign consuls. Pro-Serbian newspapers blamed the Ottoman side, and Ottoman newspapers blamed the Serbs.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

Later that June the Ottoman army in the fortress bombarded the town.[[14]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332291)[[15]](https://www.balkaninstitut.com/eng/news/59/122/great-britain-and-serbia-18561862.html)

The Serbian government saw an opportunity. According to the historian Amir Krpić, who cites Serbian historians, it meant to use the incident "as an excuse for peaceful expulsion of the Muslims" from its territory, and gave the task to the prime minister, Ilija Garašanin.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

## A conference about a fountain

By then Serbia was no longer a matter between Belgrade and Constantinople alone. Under the Paris peace treaty of 1856 it was an Ottoman vassal principality, but under the collective protection of the European powers that had signed the treaty.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)[[15]](https://www.balkaninstitut.com/eng/news/59/122/great-britain-and-serbia-18561862.html) On 8 July 1862 the powers asked the Ottoman government to call a conference, and it agreed. The conference met at Kanlıca, in Serbian Kanlidža, near Constantinople, and sat from 22 July to 4 September.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)[[14]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332291)

Britain and Austria took the Ottoman side, and France and Russia the Serbian one. The fate of Serbia's Muslims was not the main subject. The delegates argued over whether the bombardment had been legal at all, how large the Serbian army could be, and whether the fortresses in the towns should be kept or destroyed.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

The protocol they signed had twelve articles, and three of them concerned the Muslims. Their conclusion was simple. The Muslims still living in the towns would be expelled from them, with financial compensation, and the towns would pass to Serbian authority.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)

Two fortified towns, Užice and Soko, were destroyed. The Ottoman garrisons stayed in the four fortresses on the Sava and the Danube: Šabac, Belgrade, Smederevo and Fetislam at Kladovo.[[14]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332291)[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD-%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B6%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BA%D1%99%D1%83%D1%87%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B0/?lang=en)

In Belgrade, the Muslim population left before the army did. With them went the Turkish part of the city and its oriental look, and Belgrade became, in the words of Rodoljub Vasiljević's 2013 study of its last Ottoman commander, "a city with European character".[[14]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332291)

## The keys

The garrisons lasted five more years. The Serbs kept pointing to them as a cause of trouble and demanding their withdrawal. In the end Sultan Abdulaziz issued a ferman, a decree, handing over Belgrade and the remaining towns to the Serbian authorities.[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf)[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD-%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B6%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BA%D1%99%D1%83%D1%87%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B0/?lang=en) Prince Mihailo received it from the sultan in Constantinople, and it was officially announced at a special ceremony in Belgrade.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD-%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B6%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%98%D0%B5-%D0%BA%D1%99%D1%83%D1%87%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The man on the other side of the ceremony was Ali Riza Pasha, the last Ottoman vizier of Belgrade. Like those before him, he was both the vizier, or governor, of Belgrade and the commander of its fortress, the *muhafiz*. According to Vasiljević, he was very fond of the Serbs and of Prince Mihailo, and relations between the principality and the empire had improved a great deal since the bombardment.[[14]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332291)

At Kalemegdan, Ali Riza Pasha handed the prince the keys.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[2]](https://praguepapers.ff.cuni.cz/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2022/01/Amir_Krpic_27-42.pdf) The fortress's history dates this 6 April 1867.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) That is an Old Style date, from the Julian calendar, which in the nineteenth century ran twelve days behind the Gregorian calendar used today; by today's calendar it was 18 April.[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/)[[16]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Gregorian-calendar)

Serbian soldiers replaced the Turkish guards, and the Serbian flag was raised beside the Turkish one.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) After this the fortress lost its importance as a military stronghold.[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/) That year, all Ottoman soldiers left Serbia.[[11]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michael-III-prince-of-Serbia)

## The last flag

The Turkish flag stayed up for nine more years, at the Inner Stambol Gate (*Unutrašnja Stambol kapija*), the Ottoman gate from part three. After 1867 the two flags flew there side by side, and a Serbian guard replaced the Turkish one. Serbia declared war on Turkey in 1876, and the pillar with the Turkish flag was then removed from the gate, as the last symbol of Serbia's status as a vassal of the Ottoman Empire.[[17]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en) Full independence came two years later. It was recognised at the Congress of Berlin in 1878.[[18]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milan-IV)

That year Serbia also gained a Military Museum (*Vojni muzej*). Prince Milan Obrenović IV founded it by decree on 10 August 1878, 22 August by today's calendar, on the proposal of the war minister, Colonel Sava Grujić. Its first exhibition opened in 1904, for the centenary of the First Serbian Uprising, in a modest Ottoman-era building next to the Roman Well, inside the fortress.[[19]](https://www.muzej.mod.gov.rs/eng/230/o-vojnom-muzeju-230)

Prince Mihailo saw none of this. He was assassinated in 1868, probably by supporters of the Karađorđević dynasty.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Serbia/The-disintegration-of-Ottoman-rule) Part five follows the fortress after the Ottoman garrison left.

## Dates to remember

- 1804: the First Serbian Uprising begins, led by Karađorđe
- 1806–1807: the insurgents take the town of Belgrade, then the fortress
- 1813: the uprising collapses, and the Ottomans retake the fortress
- April 1815: the Second Serbian Uprising, led by Miloš Obrenović
- 1830: the sultan's hatišerif gives Serbia full autonomy
- 1841: Belgrade becomes the capital of the Principality of Serbia
- 1862: the Čukur Fountain incident on 15 June, then the bombardment of the town and the conference at Kanlıca
- 6 April 1867 (18 April in today's calendar): the keys are handed to Prince Mihailo at Kalemegdan
- 1876: the Turkish flag comes down at the Inner Stambol Gate
- 1878: the Congress of Berlin recognises Serbia's independence, and the Military Museum is founded
