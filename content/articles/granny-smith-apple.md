The Granny Smith is easy to recognize: a hard green apple, tart enough to make a pie taste awake. Its name belongs to a real woman, Maria Ann Smith, who spent the last decades of her life on a farm outside Sydney. The neat origin story, however, is not a note she left behind. It comes to us through a grower’s memory recorded more than half a century after the event it describes.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)

## From Sussex to New South Wales

Maria Ann Sherwood was born in 1799 in Peasmarsh, a village in Sussex. Her father worked as an agricultural labourer, and Maria went into farm service. In 1819 she married Thomas Smith, a farm labourer from nearby Beckley. Both signed the marriage register with a mark.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

The couple had eight children while living in Sussex. Three died in infancy; the five surviving children travelled with their parents to New South Wales. The Australian Dictionary of Biography describes their passage as part of a government bounty scheme. It belonged to a wider migration program: by 1836, New South Wales was paying migrants’ fares without requiring repayment.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)[[2]](https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/assisted-migration)

The scale was large: the National Museum of Australia estimates that 127,000 assisted migrants came to Australia between 1832 and 1850, about 70 percent of all migrants in those years. The Smiths were one family within that government-led movement.[[2]](https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/assisted-migration)

The ADB’s family links include her grandsons, so “Granny” was a grandmother as well as the name later attached to the apple.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

The family sailed on the *Lady Nugent* and reached Sydney on 27 November 1838. The ADB says Thomas found work for a settler in the established fruit-growing district of Kissing Point, now part of Ryde. Maria and Thomas remained in the district for the rest of their lives.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

Their youngest child, William, was the only one born in Australia; he was baptized at St Anne’s in Ryde in May 1842.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

In the mid-1850s Thomas bought two blocks of land for an orchard, about 24 acres on the edge of the Field of Mars Common at Eastwood. The ADB describes Kissing Point as an established fruit-growing district; their orchard sat in the same agricultural community.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

An unusual seedling can appear in any orchard. Most are not worth keeping. A grower who finds a tree with good fruit can preserve it by taking wood from it and grafting that onto rootstock, instead of raising more trees from seed.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)[[3]](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/can-i-grow-apple-tree-seed)

## The story told years later

The familiar account begins beside a creek. In the 1868 story, Maria and Thomas’s orchard held a seedling said to have grown from the remains of French crab apples grown in Tasmania. A local fruit-grower and his father were invited to inspect it; Maria reportedly explained its origin and began raising a few trees.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

That account first appeared in print in the *Farmer and Settler* in June 1924, in a piece by Dundas orchardist and local historian Herbert Rumsey. Rumsey interviewed two growers who had known Maria. The Australian Dictionary of Biography says one remembered the visit, but it does not name that witness. The story reached print roughly 56 years after the supposed meeting, not in a contemporary report. Her descendants have also disputed parts of the discovery story.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

The ADB does not name the witness. It identifies Edward Gallard as the local orchardist who later planted many of the trees and marketed a crop each year until his death in 1914. The source does not establish whether Gallard also heard the 1868 explanation, so it would be too strong to call this his recollection. The creek-side seedling may well have been the Granny Smith’s beginning, but the details remain a recollection, not a proven chain of events.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

If the account is right, the tree by the creek was a chance seedling: a tree grown from seed, with its own genetic combination rather than a copy of its parent. Iowa State University Extension notes that apple seedlings usually do not reproduce a named cultivar, so growers use grafting. They attach a piece of selected tree wood, called a scion, to a rootstock to reproduce that variety consistently. The first seedling may have been an accident; the Granny Smith that followed was a choice.[[3]](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/can-i-grow-apple-tree-seed)

## After Maria

Maria Smith died at Ryde on 9 March 1870. The apple was not a commercial variety during her lifetime. Its early survival depended on local growers, including Gallard, who bought part of the Smith farm after Thomas died in 1876 and continued planting and marketing the fruit.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

The Castle Hill Agricultural and Horticultural Show grew out of local sports and ploughing contests. Its own history says fruit and vegetables were already on display in 1886; the 1890 show had a competitive fruit section. The permanent showground opened in 1891.[[7]](https://www.castlehillshow.com.au/society-history) By 1891–92, “Granny Smith’s seedlings” were winning prizes in cooking-apple classes and several local growers were exhibiting them, according to the ADB.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)

In 1895 the New South Wales Department of Agriculture included “Granny Smith’s seedlings” on a list of fruit varieties suitable for export. The same year, the government’s experimental station at Bathurst planted the apple on a larger scale. That was a practical endorsement, not evidence that a worldwide industry appeared overnight. The State Library of New South Wales holds William John Allen’s 1906 booklet *Commercial fruits of New South Wales – Apples*, a small sign of how the colony’s fruit trade was being treated as a commercial subject by then.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)[[6]](https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/collection-items/commercial-fruits-new-south-wales-apples-1906)

The early show records called them “Granny Smith’s seedlings.” The familiar cultivar name took shape as growers preserved the selected fruit, and grafting let each new orchard grow that same apple without relying on fresh seed.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)[[3]](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/can-i-grow-apple-tree-seed)

## The long journey into world markets

Export suitability did not immediately make Granny Smith a global apple. It was planted commercially after 1900, but it did not become important in world trade until the 1950s. The variety’s long growing season limited where it could be raised, while its firm, crisp flesh and excellent storage qualities helped make it useful to growers and merchants handling fruit over long distances.[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)

A 1982 article in *HortScience* traced the next stage. By 1960, Granny Smith accounted for more than a quarter of Australian apple production. It was already important in Argentina, New Zealand and South Africa; during the late 1960s and 1970s, plantings spread through France, Italy, Chile and Spain. It reached U.S. orchards in the mid-1970s, especially in central Washington.[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)

The same article gives a snapshot of the American market in 1982. Granny Smiths were selling at a premium over major U.S. varieties. Until a few years earlier, nearly all the fruit sold in American supermarkets had been imported from the Southern Hemisphere; Washington and France were just beginning to supply local-grown apples. The green variety had become both an export and a crop for new growing regions.[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)

Larsen’s production table estimated about 705,000 metric tonnes of Granny Smith in 1978, or 3.7 percent of world apple production in the 32 countries surveyed. That placed it fifth among the named varieties in the table. The survey left out some major producers, including the Soviet Union and China, so it was not a complete count; even so, the figures show that by the late 1970s this was a substantial international crop, not simply an Australian export novelty.[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)

Storage did not make the apples invulnerable. Granny Smith can develop storage scald and bitter pit, and it needs a long season to mature. Australian researchers tested fruit from different growing districts under ordinary and controlled-atmosphere storage; in the study, some lots were held for six months in air or nine months in a controlled atmosphere.[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1080/00221589.1981.11515008) A 1982 horticultural review also called it an excellent storage cultivar, while warning about scald and bitter pit.[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)

That long season narrowed the places where it could be grown. The 1982 review gave a typical Southern Hemisphere harvest requirement of 180 to 190 days from bloom for fruit suitable for long storage. In warmer regions, the same cultivar could be picked earlier as a tart cooking apple or left longer to ripen for the dessert market, making it useful for more than one kind of buyer.[[4]](https://doi.org/10.21273/hortsci.17.6.875)[[5]](https://www.pubhort.org/aps/48/v48_n2_a1.htm)

By 1994, a review in the *Fruit Varieties Journal* described Granny Smith as widely grown around the world and especially important in the Southern Hemisphere. It estimated that Southern Hemisphere production in 1991 exceeded 50 million cartons, with a carton defined as about 18.5 kilograms.[[5]](https://www.pubhort.org/aps/48/v48_n2_a1.htm) The green apple that began as a local selection had become a standard variety across multiple national industries. It was no longer just a story about a tree beside a creek; it was a crop that could be reproduced, stored and sold at scale.[[5]](https://www.pubhort.org/aps/48/v48_n2_a1.htm)

The best-known version of Maria Smith’s story is still the least certain part: a meeting remembered long after it happened, a seedling said to have come from discarded apples, and a woman whose own voice is absent from the account. What followed is better documented. Local growers kept the fruit alive, the department put it forward for export, and grafting let new orchards preserve the same chosen apple. The first tree may have appeared by chance; the trees that carried its name around the world were deliberately grafted from selected wood.[[1]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/smith-maria-ann-13199/text23897)[[3]](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/can-i-grow-apple-tree-seed)
