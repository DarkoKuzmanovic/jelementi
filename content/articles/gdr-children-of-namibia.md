Roughly 430 children from camps run by SWAPO, Namibia’s independence movement, spent part of their childhood in the German Democratic Republic. Some arrived very young. Some stayed there for most of their school years. They lived first at Schloss Bellin in Mecklenburg and, as they grew older, some went to the School of Friendship in Staßfurt. In August 1990, as the political arrangements that had shaped both their exile and their schooling collapsed, they were sent to Namibia, often before completing the education for which the project had been designed. The term “GDR children of Namibia” now gathers these adults under one name, though their biographies vary.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

Even the number deserves a little care. Yvonne Niekrenz, Christian Armbrüster and Matthias D. Witte use “some 430”; Matthias Witte and colleagues give 420; Jason Owens gives 428. These are estimates of a project whose participants arrived over several years, not a single roll call that every source counted in the same way. “About 430” is a useful shorthand, not a claim of an exact total.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1353/hcy.0.0014)

## From camps to a northern German manor

The children’s journey began in a war. Namibia, then under South African rule, was the setting for SWAPO’s independence struggle. Many Namibians had fled across borders, where SWAPO organised refugee camps and schools. In May 1978 South African forces attacked the camp at Cassinga in Angola. That attack formed part of the immediate background to SWAPO’s request that the GDR take children from the camps into its care. The children sent to Germany came from the wider refugee network in Angola and Zambia; it would be wrong to describe every child as a survivor of Cassinga or to imply that all came from one camp.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

The first group, about eighty children, arrived in the GDR in December 1979 with Namibian caregivers. They were placed at Schloss Bellin, a manor house in Mecklenburg. The children’s home was initially imagined as temporary: Namibia’s independence was expected sooner than it came. Meanwhile more children arrived, and the temporary arrangement stretched across the decade. In the GDR, the project was kept out of public view for years. Niekrenz and her co-authors document an agreement by SWAPO and the ruling Socialist Unity Party to avoid publications about the children’s presence until the two sides agreed otherwise.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

Protection was one part of the arrangement. SWAPO asked for care and schooling for children exposed to the dangers of the camps; GDR and SWAPO leaders also expected them to return as educated members of an independent Namibia. Witte, Klein-Zimmer and Schmitt describe the project as a joint education and care scheme, with the stated aim of preparing a future Namibian elite. The GDR’s socialist educational methods and SWAPO’s political goals both shaped it. The same programme combined everyday caregiving and classroom instruction with political preparation.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

At Bellin, the children’s daily life was arranged by adults from both countries. Namibian caregivers helped maintain Oshiwambo language and cultural instruction; German staff taught German and used the GDR curriculum. As the children began school in nearby Zehna, lessons followed that curriculum but included material about Namibia. Geography, local studies and other subjects could include the country’s history and culture. Their education combined the GDR curriculum with Namibian history and cultural instruction, rather than reproducing either system intact.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[5]](https://www.landesbeauftragter.de/publikationen/1996-bis-2006/wir-hatten-noch-nie-einen-schwarzen-gesehen)

The children also made their own ways of living across the two settings. Witte and colleagues describe “Oshi-Deutsch”, a blend of Oshiwambo and German that young people used among themselves. It was not a language devised by officials for a syllabus. It belonged to the children’s own social world, as did the friendships and routines they built around one another. The home could be deliberately separated from ordinary GDR life, but it was not sealed off completely: the children had contact with nearby residents, trips beyond the home and other encounters. Their experience included constraints set by institutions and room for their own practices within them.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

When the oldest children reached secondary-school age, they moved in 1985 to the School of Friendship in Staßfurt, near Magdeburg. That institution was already a large boarding-school project for young people from Mozambique. The Namibian students joined that setting; the move did not mean that every child took the same route at the same time. Some remained connected to Bellin and other schools while others spent their older school years in Staßfurt. The GDR project’s institutional reach was broader than a single home, and its Namibian group was one part of a larger education experiment involving African students.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[6]](https://gepris.dfg.de/project/5302874)

## Education for a future state

One stated aim was to educate a future Namibian elite. Witte and colleagues describe political and ideological upbringing shaped by socialist ideas alongside care and schooling. A 2016 German Federal Cultural Foundation page about the theatre project *Oshi-Deutsch* says the children “primarily received an education in German-style socialist ideology.” The Foundation’s text describes a theatre production, not a full curriculum; it records a different emphasis without showing what every pupil learned.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[11]](https://www.kulturstiftung-des-bundes.de/en/programmes_projects/detail/oshi_deutsch_the_gdr_children_of_namibia.html)

The GDR and SWAPO shared responsibility for the project, but brought different aims. The GDR offered care, schools and resources through its international solidarity policy; SWAPO planned for a future state and a population displaced by war. The children’s own lives did not fit neatly inside either government’s plan. They learned German, retained or developed Namibian knowledge, and formed a peer community whose reference points stretched across countries. The education project intended to prepare them for Namibia, while the years spent in Germany also became part of their biographies.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.3224/diskurs.v9i4.17294)

That complexity mattered before the return, too. During the late 1980s and 1990, the question of how the children had come to the GDR became politically contentious. Some Namibian and German publications repeated allegations that children had been taken against their parents’ wishes or were children of SWAPO prisoners. Other voices rejected the “abduction” account and described an agreed solidarity project. Niekrenz and her co-authors analyse these claims as part of a changing media debate; the evidence they review leaves individual questions about parental consent open. The project’s official purpose does not settle them, and media allegations cannot be treated as established fact for every child.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[4]](https://www.namibiadigitalrepository.com/items/show/149)

Constance Kenna’s 1999 edited volume offers a different kind of record. Alongside her historical overview, it includes pieces by former pupils and people who cared for or taught them. The contents move from Cassinga and Bellin to Staßfurt and Namibia, reflecting places where these lives unfolded. Kenna describes the book as documentation rather than a definitive account, leaving room for former pupils to add their own versions.[[4]](https://www.namibiadigitalrepository.com/items/show/149)

## The return, in a year that moved at different speeds

Namibia became independent on 21 March 1990. The GDR’s end came later: the East German parliament decided in August that it would join the Federal Republic on 3 October. In between, in August, the Namibian children began returning. The events belong to the same year, but they were not simultaneous. That difference matters: the children’s return took place while governments and institutions were changing around them, rather than after a settled transition had made the next step clear.[[7]](https://namibia.un.org/en/175155-uns-role-namibian-independence)[[8]](https://www.bundestag.de/parlament/geschichte/parlamentarismus/10_volkskammer)

The return was organised quickly and without the preparation the young people needed, according to Witte and colleagues. The GDR government would no longer fund the children’s home, and Namibia’s education minister decided they should return immediately. Their schooling was interrupted. Some had spent more than a decade in Germany; others had spent less. Those who arrived in Windhoek in August 1990 were not all the same age, and they did not have the same family situation or the same knowledge of the place they were being told was home.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

Lucia Engombe, author of *Kind Nr. 95*, is one of the former pupils to have published her own account. In an excerpt quoted by Witte and colleagues, she describes returning to Namibia at seventeen, where her mother was as foreign to her as the country she loved.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[9]](https://www.ullstein.de/werke/kind-nr-95/taschenbuch/9783548258928)

Witte and colleagues stress that return experiences varied with family situation and time spent in Germany; some young people met relatives they barely knew, while others had lost close family members or had no family to receive them. The lead media study records reports that German had become the stronger language for some, but treats such descriptions as part of a changing public narrative, not as a language test for every person.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

“Repatriation” names the administrative act of returning people to their country of origin. It does not tell us whether the place was familiar to them. Many children had left when very young; some had never lived in Namibia. Officials could choose a destination and arrange transport, but neither action restored the years they had spent away or guaranteed that family relationships would pick up where they had stopped.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

The return brought immediate practical questions, too. Children returned before finishing school, and arrangements had to be made for their registration, schooling and support. Niekrenz and colleagues report that returnees were initially taken to the People’s Primary School in Katutura to be registered; public debate focused on responsibility and what should happen next. The young people themselves could be treated as a political issue even as they tried to continue school, find relatives and work out daily life.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)

Niekrenz and colleagues trace how coverage moved between victim stories and former pupils who objected to one-sided portrayals. Kenna’s volume preserves accounts that include affection for the GDR years, education and relationships. The study also records later stories of former pupils taking part in Namibian public life. Affection for those years could coexist with a painful return.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[4]](https://www.namibiadigitalrepository.com/items/show/149)

## Who gets to call somewhere home?

The question of belonging has followed the group partly because other people keep asking it. Niekrenz, Armbrüster and Witte studied newspaper and magazine coverage from 1979 through 2013. They found that the media repeatedly assigned the former children roles: political symbols, war victims, curiosities, bridge-builders, experts on their own lives and, at times, objects of suspicion. The frames changed with the political moment. What stayed constant was the habit of discussing the group as a problem of identity and home.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)

Niekrenz, Armbrüster and Witte’s media analysis follows newspaper and magazine frames; it cannot tell us what every adult believes today. The authors warn that the group label can flatten biographies and obscure that the children are now adults. Kenna’s collection preserves first-person accounts, while Witte and colleagues’ biographical work follows how people negotiated borders and belonging. A shared childhood does not determine a shared answer.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)[[4]](https://www.namibiadigitalrepository.com/items/show/149)

The project expected the children to return to Namibia, but many spent formative years in East Germany, where they learned German and formed relationships. Witte and colleagues’ biographical work follows how people negotiated life across both places. Their shared childhood is part of these histories; it cannot assign every former pupil a single present-day identity.[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)

SWAPO and the GDR intended to protect and educate the children for an independent Namibia. Their joint project also carried Cold War politics into schooling and daily life. Many formed attachments in East Germany before the abrupt return in August 1990. The years that followed belong to each person’s own account.[[1]](https://www.namibian-studies.com/index.php/JNS/article/view/85)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1080/21931674.2013.10820755)
