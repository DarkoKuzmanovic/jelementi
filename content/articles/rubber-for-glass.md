In August 1915, with the war on the Western Front just over a year old, a representative of the British Ministry of Munitions travelled to Switzerland on an unusual mission. His job was to find out whether Britain could obtain optical instruments from German sources.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) The instruments in question were binoculars, telescopes and gunsights. The country that could supply them in bulk was Germany, the country Britain was fighting.

The official history of the Ministry of Munitions puts it plainly. Preliminary investigations had found that only Germany could supply optical instruments in quantity, and the representative was sent to Switzerland to see whether instruments could be had from German sources.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) It is hard to think of a stranger shopping trip. Britain wanted to buy from its enemy the lenses its soldiers would use to aim at that enemy's troops.

## Why lenses decided battles

This was the first great war fought at long distance. Advances in artillery power and precision had pushed target ranges as high as 40,000 to 60,000 yards, and guns firing over such distances needed optical rangefinders, panoramic sights, field glasses and other fire control devices.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) The side with the better optics had a clear advantage on the battlefield.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)

There was no doubt who held that advantage. Germany dominated world production of precision optics through the firms of Carl Zeiss, which made lenses, and Schott, which made the glass that went into them.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) The industry was concentrated at Jena, in Thuringia, where Otto Schott had developed new glass mixtures, including borosilicate glass and clearer, more stable glasses using lithium, and Zeiss had turned them into remarkable lenses.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)

The Jena glasses had set off a wave of innovation in optical design. Their effect on peaceful work such as microscopy is well known and celebrated, but they left just as deep a mark on military equipment.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) Mid nineteenth century conflicts such as the American Civil War had been fought mostly at close quarters, with artillery firing over short distances and little need for fine optics.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) Half a century of advances in artillery power and precision changed that, and by 1914 there was no going back: guns needed glass, and the best glass was German.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)

Britain, by contrast, bought nearly all its optical glass abroad. In 1913 some 60 percent of British optical glass was made at Jena, another 30 percent came from Parra Mantois in Paris, and only about 10 percent came from the British firm of Chance Brothers in Birmingham.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) When war cut off German imports, France was already overwhelmed by its own mobilisation and had little to spare for Britain.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) Britain had to replace the German share and equip an army growing from a peacetime strength of 250,000 men to several million, all needing binoculars, periscopes, rangefinders, aerial camera lenses and telescopic rifle sights.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

## The famine

At first Chance Brothers insisted its existing works would meet all military needs, in a war everyone expected to be short, and resisted government pressure to expand.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) Some progress was made with telescopic sights, which were small and easier to make, but British firms lacked the machinery and skill for larger lenses in quantity, and by the spring of 1915 domestic production was falling far short of what the army needed.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

The Glasgow firm of Barr and Stroud managed to expand its production, but it was not enough.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) By the spring of 1915 it was clear that home production was falling far short of the quantities required.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

Trench warfare made the shortage acute. Once the front lines froze in place, binoculars became the daily tools of infantry officers trying to pick out enemy snipers and machine gun positions, and every officer or non commissioned officer needed a reliable pair hanging from his neck.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php)[[5]](https://greatwarproject.org/2015/07/20/a-clear-case-of-trading-with-the-enemy/) Britain was meanwhile planning new offensives on the stalemated Western Front, with the attack at Loos being prepared in mid 1915, and the demand for optics kept climbing.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php)

The government scrambled. It appealed to the public to donate field glasses and gunsights, and collected only some 2,000 instruments, including four pairs each from the king and queen.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) It tried importing from the United States, but quantities were small and an offer of binoculars from Bausch and Lomb was rejected as an unsatisfactory design.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) By November 1915 the government had requisitioned every unsold instrument in shops across the country.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) Meanwhile British troops were being killed by German snipers fitted with Zeiss telescopic sights while British soldiers went into the trenches without binoculars at all.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)

Glass was not the only forgotten dependence the war exposed. Britain found itself short of other products it had long relied on Germany to supply, with little or no domestic manufacture: engine starters, uniform dyes and laboratory apparatus among them.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) Optics was simply the most urgent case, because guns without sights cannot hit anything.

## Rubber for glass

Germany had a shortage of its own. Under the Allied naval blockade it could import almost no natural rubber, and its early synthetic rubber was produced in small quantities and poor quality.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) Britain and France, with colonies in Africa and south east Asia, had abundant natural rubber, while Germany needed it for tyres, fan belts, communications cables and other military uses.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) By early 1915 the German army was so short of tyres that it considered running supply trucks on steel tyres, which chewed roads to pieces.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php)

So Britain proposed an exchange: British rubber for German optical instruments, arranged through Swiss intermediaries.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) The German answer was prompt and positive.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) The German War Office agreed to supply 8,000 to 10,000 each of two types of binoculars, one pattern for infantry officers and one for artillery officers.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) For the future, the official British record says, the Germans were prepared to deliver 10,000 to 15,000 of each type within six weeks of a contract being signed, and were even willing to release skilled workmen from the army back to Zeiss to get the orders through quickly.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) Lower grade binoculars for non commissioned officers, 10,000 to 12,000 of them, could be supplied at once and then at 5,000 pairs a month, with 5,000 to 10,000 telescopic sights a month and as many rangefinders as Britain wanted.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) Since samples could hardly be posted through the front lines, the British were invited to judge German quality by inspecting the equipment of captured German officers.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) In return, British rubber from Africa and south east Asia would travel to Switzerland and cross the German border.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php)

## Did anything change hands

Here the story splits, and the split matters. The official British account says that despite German willingness, Britain never took up the offer.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) The historian Stewart Wills, writing in the journal Optics and Photonics News, likewise says the agreement never actually went into effect.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) John Heilbron, in his biography of Ernest Rutherford, also concludes that no exchange was made.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

But other historians read the archives differently. Adam Hochschild, in his 2011 history To End All Wars, writes that during August 1915, the first month of the arrangement, the Germans delivered even more than first agreed: some 32,000 pairs of binoculars, 20,000 of them the higher quality types for officers.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) He adds that records showing how long the trade continued, or how much rubber Germany received in return, have disappeared.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) Alan Simmonds, in his history Britain and World War One, puts the figure at 30,000 pairs supplied by the end of 1915, a conclusion the historian Guy Hartcup reached independently from British archive material.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

These accounts cannot all be right, and none can be checked against a complete paper trail, because the decisive records are missing. What can be said is this: the negotiation certainly happened, the terms were certainly generous, and whether any binoculars or any rubber actually moved is genuinely disputed. Readers should treat any confident statement either way with suspicion, including mine. The one piece of context that weakens the case for a large delivery is what happened next. Britain soon stopped needing German glass at all.

So what were the negotiators thinking? Hochschild admits there is no written trace of what was in the minds of the men who arranged this exchange.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) Perhaps each side believed it was getting the better deal. Perhaps businessmen on both sides were simply eager enough for profit that nothing else mattered. Or perhaps the war had gathered such momentum that anything seemed justified, even trading with the enemy, so long as it helped fight the war.[[4]](https://erenow.org/ww/loyaltyandrebellion1914-1918/13.php) The archives answer none of these questions. They record the quantities and the prices, and then they go silent.

## The glass feast

In the summer of 1915 Britain set out to build its own optical industry. Production was brought under the Optical Munitions and Glass Department of the newly formed Ministry of Munitions, working with the Royal Society's advisory council for research.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) In June 1915 the department offered Chance Brothers money for plant expansion, scientific expertise and guaranteed exclusivity in military contracts for ten years, in return for specified increases in output.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) Chance accepted at once, expanded its works, bought new machinery and set about reproducing the high grade glasses of the Jena catalogue, something it had never attempted at scale.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

The project succeeded astonishingly fast. Output rose to 70,000 pounds (about 32,000 kilograms) of glass a year by November 1916 and to 92,000 pounds (about 42,000 kilograms) by 1918, though potash for the furnaces remained hard to find.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) British makers improved on German patents as they went, and by 1916 the supply deficit had almost been made good.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange) Complete self sufficiency was never quite reached, and some raw materials stayed hard to find throughout the war, but by 1918, in the words of the economic historian Stephen Sambrook, the Chance catalogue included virtually everything obtainable from Schott in 1913, plus some glasses Schott had never made.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) A first class, high volume optical industry had been created almost from nothing.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) By the end of the war Britain was producing more than it needed, enough to supply its allies.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)

There is an irony on the German side too. Zeiss expanded its workforce from 5,200 to 9,800 during 1914 and 1915, yet even that was not enough: by the late war the German army's demand for glass instruments was outrunning supply, the government had to let other firms into the market, and shortages of optical instruments began to show in Germany as well.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War_glass%E2%80%93rubber_exchange)

How had Britain fallen so far behind in the first place? Ed Conway, who tells this story in his book Material World, traces part of the answer to a long fiscal assault on British glassmaking, beginning with the window tax of the eighteenth century and continuing through duties on glass producers and consumers assessed by weight.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) The window tax itself was mostly a tax on housing, since houses with more windows paid more, which is why people bricked windows up, but it opened a long period in which glass was taxed at production and at sale.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)

A century or so earlier, Conway notes, Britain had held a world leading optical industry. British lenses were by most accounts clearer and more precise than their Bohemian rivals, glass was among the most profitable sectors of the economy, and foreign powers sent spies to steal its secrets.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) Glass in those days was the advanced technology of its age, something like semiconductors today: lenses extended working lives, enabled scientific research, and, Conway suggests, may even help explain the Renaissance.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) British firms stopped investing in new kinds of glass just as the great breakthroughs in lens glass were being made, while German states supported their glassmakers with the forerunner of industrial strategy and Jena pulled ahead.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine) Whether taxes deserve quite this much blame is debatable, but the dependence they describe is a matter of record: in 1914 Britain relied on Jena for most of its lenses, and bought the cheapest available without a second thought.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)

The ending carries its own warning. After the war, military demand collapsed, the new industry slumped badly in 1919, and though Chance Brothers survived into the 1980s, Britain let much of its rebuilt optical industry waste away again.[[2]](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_27/january_2016/features/how_the_great_war_changed_the_optics_industry/the_british_glass_scramble/) The glass famine was followed by a glass feast, and the feast, in Conway's phrase, was short lived.[[1]](https://edconway.substack.com/p/the-glass-famine)
