On 11 November 2013, a user of the Mathematics Stack Exchange forum asked for help with an integral. It was a forbidding object: a fraction with a square root inside, multiplied by the logarithm of another fraction, integrated from negative one to one. The asker had tried the standard computer algebra programs, and those had managed only an approximate numerical value, not an exact closed form answer.[[8]](https://www.spektrum.de/kolumne/wer-ist-cleo-internet-detektive-vermuten-vladimir-reshetnikov/2255256)[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

About four and a half hours later, the answer arrived. A user named Cleo, whose account was only weeks old, wrote a single line: the integral equals four pi times the inverse cotangent of the square root of the golden ratio. The only decoration was a link on the golden ratio symbol, pointing to its definition. No derivation, no explanation, no comment.[[3]](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/562694/integral-int-11-frac1x-sqrt-frac1x1-x-ln-left-frac2-x22-x1/563063#563063)[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

The site's regulars did not know whether to be grateful or annoyed. One of them asked within minutes whether there was any reason to believe a closed form existed at all for such a horrid looking thing.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) Another, a patent agent and former physicist named Ron Gordon, objected on principle. Quoting the engineer Richard Hamming's line that the purpose of computing is insight, not numbers, he argued that a bare result with nothing illuminating about it hardly counted as an answer.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) Then he sat down, spent the better part of a weekend filling about half a legal pad, and proved that Cleo was right.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

His proof became one of the most celebrated answers in the site's history. It is the accepted answer on that thread, it has collected more than a thousand upvotes, and it was passed around elsewhere under the title "Master of Integration."[[4]](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/562694/integral-int-11-frac1x-sqrt-frac1x1-x-ln-left-frac2-x22-x1/565626#565626)[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) The thread itself has since drawn more than 470,000 views and nineteen separate answers.[[2]](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/562694/integral-int-11-frac1x-sqrt-frac1x1-x-ln-left-frac2-x22-x1) And Cleo, having supplied the spark, said nothing at all.

## Thirty nine answers, zero explanations

That was Cleo's second answer. The first had come a couple of days earlier, after a wait of roughly nineteen hours that must have felt luxurious by later standards. Over the next two years, thirty nine answers followed in the same style: brutally hard integrals, mostly, answered with complete closed forms and no working whatsoever. The run stretches from November 2013 to the last day of 2015.[[1]](https://math.stackexchange.com/users/97378/cleo)[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html) The account has not been active since early 2016.[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html)

Cleo broke the pattern only twice, to ask two questions of her own, both about exotic integrals involving special functions. They attracted high scores and little suspicion.[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html) Everything else about the account deepened the mystery. The early profile quoted the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan describing a dream in which a hand wrote elliptic integrals on a screen of flowing blood, and added an invitation of its own: remember that you are not locked into a single system of axioms, since you may invent your own whenever you wish, using intuition and imagination.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) In late 2014 a bitcoin address appeared, so that grateful readers could send donations. In early 2015 the profile changed one final time, to the text it still carries: "My real name is Cleo, I'm female. I have a medical condition that makes it very difficult for me to engage in conversations, or post long answers, sorry for that."[[1]](https://math.stackexchange.com/users/97378/cleo)[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/)

That explanation settled nothing. Mathematicians are not generally told to accept results on trust, and a forum built on showing your work found itself hosting a user who never did. Some readers were delighted. Many were furious. The arguments filled comment sections that have mostly since been deleted.[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html)

## Why the missing steps mattered

The row was not really about one user's manners. It was about what an answer is for.

Nine days after the famous integral appeared, Gordon opened a thread on the site's discussion board asking outright whether answers with no explanation were useful.[[5]](https://math.meta.stackexchange.com/questions/11723/are-answers-that-have-no-explanations-useful) The responses split along a fault line that still runs through every technical forum. One side held that unexplained results are barely answers at all: a bare number teaches nothing, transfers no method, and leaves every reader exactly as helpless as before.[[5]](https://math.meta.stackexchange.com/questions/11723/are-answers-that-have-no-explanations-useful) The other side, in a highly voted reply, argued that a bare correct result can be worth a great deal: it tells you a closed form exists, its shape hints at where a proof might lie, and it can challenge others to find a route they would never have attempted.[[5]](https://math.meta.stackexchange.com/questions/11723/are-answers-that-have-no-explanations-useful)

That reply was written by Vladimir Reshetnikov, a long standing contributor to the site with a formidable reputation score.[[6]](https://math.meta.stackexchange.com/questions/11723/are-answers-that-have-no-explanations-useful/11741#11741)[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html) Nobody knew at the time how personally interested he was in the question.

There was a precedent for admiring answerless brilliance, and Cleo's fans reached for it constantly. Ramanujan, the self taught Indian genius of the early twentieth century, had sent pages of theorems to Cambridge without proofs, writing that they were simply right. "I just do," Dev Patel says as Ramanujan in the film about his life, when pressed for a derivation.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) The parallel was irresistible: another intuitive prodigy, appearing from nowhere, handing down results from private visions. Cleo's own profile invited the comparison by quoting Ramanujan's dream.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) Some readers even repeated a rumor that Cleo had once explained the silence differently, as a matter of religion, calling herself a priest of Namagiri, the goddess Ramanujan worshipped.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) Whether that was ever really said is now impossible to check.

## A decade of detective work

Naturally, everybody tried to unmask her.

The guesses ranged across the whole spectrum of mathematical celebrity. Perhaps she was Terence Tao, relaxing in the evening. Perhaps she was Maryam Mirzakhani, the Iranian Fields Medal winner. Perhaps she was the reclusive Grigori Perelman. The most extravagant theory nominated Stephen Hawking, noting with dark logic that the profile's medical condition would fit.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) Tao, contacted by journalists, confirmed by email that he was not Cleo.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

Others suspected a machine. The answers arrived fast, never tired, and never erred, and at least one professional mathematician wondered publicly whether some unreleased theorem prover was doing the work.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) Against that stood the awkward fact that the best commercial software of 2013 could not touch these integrals, and remains limited with them today.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

Then came the spreadsheets. A Reddit user writing as evilscientist311 analyzed Cleo's activity periods and mapped the accounts that kept showing up on the same threads. Two names recurred: Vladimir Reshetnikov, and Laila Podlesny, the asker of the famous November integral. The investigator's conclusion was reasonable and wrong: probably a group of university friends, one posting questions from some accounts while another answered from Cleo, keeping the game alive until graduation scattered them.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/)

Meanwhile the legend grew its own folklore. One rumor put Cleo at fifteen years old, though the profile never said anything of the kind. Every so often somebody stepped forward to claim the account was theirs, and none of those claims ever stuck.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/)[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

The story might have frozen there, another internet legend, had short video not revived it. A viral clip about Cleo restarted the speculation, copycat claimants popped up on social media offering no proof, and in late 2023 the digging resumed in earnest.[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/) This time the detectives got lucky.

## The base64 confession

In February 2025 a YouTube creator named Joe McCann published an investigation into the mystery.[[9]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7gQ9DnSYsXg) Working from the Reddit analysis, he noticed odd coincidences in when the suspect accounts were active, though he judged the evidence too thin to name anyone.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) One of his viewers went further. Podlesny's profile had once shown a contact address, and by probing the account recovery flow the viewer found that the first letters of its backup address matched the address on Reshetnikov's profile.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) McCann then contacted Reshetnikov directly and put the evidence to him.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/)

Reshetnikov did not wait for the video to appear. On 8 February 2025, scrambled text materialized on his Stack Exchange profile. Decoded from base64, it read "Creator of Cleo." Matching scrambled strings appeared on the Podlesny profile and on a third account under the name Oksana Gimmel, decoding to "Alter-ego of Cleo, created by Vladimir Reshetnikov."[[8]](https://www.spektrum.de/kolumne/wer-ist-cleo-internet-detektive-vermuten-vladimir-reshetnikov/2255256)[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) McCann laid out the complete chain in a follow up video the next day.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) Shortly afterward Reshetnikov confirmed it himself on social media with the Latin phrase "mea culpa," my fault.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/)

So the oracle was a 45 year old software developer from Tashkent: trained in theoretical physics at the National University of Uzbekistan, employed for more than eight years at Microsoft, and a heavyweight contributor to the very forum he had pranked.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/)[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html)

His motive, as he explained it, was attention, or rather the lack of it. The fiendish integrals he cared about drew shrugs when he posted them under his own name. Presented as challenges from a phantom, they electrified the site. In McCann's account of the affair, Reshetnikov builds his monsters by reverse engineering: start from a known result, tweak the integral step by step while tracking what happens to its value, and keep going until even Mathematica cannot follow. Then post the question from one account, answer it from another, and watch the mathematicians scramble.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) He had, in a sense, already told the forum what he believed years earlier, in that much upvoted discussion reply: a bare answer can goad people into finding a proof they would otherwise never attempt.[[6]](https://math.meta.stackexchange.com/questions/11723/are-answers-that-have-no-explanations-useful/11741#11741)

## What the oracle proved

There is an irony here that survives the unmasking. The people most irritated by Cleo were right that mathematics runs on reasoning, not revelation. A result without justification cannot be checked, taught, or built upon, and a forum full of oracles would be useless. Gordon's weekend with the legal pad is the whole discipline in miniature: the answer was a curiosity until someone derived it, and the derivation, with its symmetry spotting and its collapsed eighth degree polynomial, is what readers still admire.[[4]](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/562694/integral-int-11-frac1x-sqrt-frac1x1-x-ln-left-frac2-x22-x1/565626#565626)[[7]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/cleo-the-mysterious-math-menace1/)

But the defenders of the bare answer were also right, and the record backs them. Knowing the shape of the destination genuinely helped travelers get there. The famous thread accumulated proof after proof, each using different machinery, precisely because someone had planted a flag on the summit and said it could be climbed.[[2]](https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/562694/integral-int-11-frac1x-sqrt-frac1x1-x-ln-left-frac2-x22-x1) Reshetnikov's stunt demonstrated his thesis the hard way: withholding the working did not destroy mathematical interest, it detonated it.

Cleo is gone, in the sense that she never existed. The account fell silent at the end of 2015, and the woman in the profile was fiction from the start.[[1]](https://math.stackexchange.com/users/97378/cleo)[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20251205065321/http://xahlee.info/math/cleo_math_stackexchange.html) What remains is the mathematics, which checked out, and a small parable about how the subject actually advances: not by trusting mysterious strangers, but by refusing to leave their claims unproven. One forum user, quoted approvingly after the reveal, put the moral in grander terms: the idea of Cleo is what matters, not whether she exists.[[10]](https://talimtelekanali.uz/en/shaxsi-nomalum-daho-matematik-kleo-ozbekistonlik-dasturchi-bolib-chiqdi-25-02-2025/) That may oversell a prank. But for two years, a phantom handed the internet impossible integrals, and the internet, on the record, solved them.
