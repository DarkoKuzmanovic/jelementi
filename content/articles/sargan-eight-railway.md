At Mokra Gora, the railway climbs toward Šargan Vitasi by doubling back through tunnels and looping across the same mountain slopes at different levels. Rather than attack the grade in a straight line, the track takes a longer route around the terrain, gaining height in stages. The name, Šarganska osmica, means “Šargan Eight,” after the figure-eight layout on the ascent.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)[[2]](https://srbijavoz.rs/nostalgija/)

Two different distances are in circulation. Srbijavoz gives the present museum section, Mokra Gora to Šargan Vitasi, as 15.5 kilometres one way, with an elevation difference of 300 metres, five bridges and 22 tunnels. The park operator’s 22.8-kilometre figure covers the longer historic section from Šargan Vitasi through Jatare and Mokra Gora to Vardište, at the border. It is not the length of today’s ride.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)[[2]](https://srbijavoz.rs/nostalgija/)

The 354-metre figure is less exact. The park operator says the historic line overcame a “high gradient” of 354 metres but gives no endpoints. Srbijavoz gives 300 metres for the current route. Without endpoints for the 354-metre figure, the measurements cannot be compared, and it would be wrong to call the present excursion a 354-metre climb.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)[[2]](https://srbijavoz.rs/nostalgija/)

## A missing link between railways

The line began as a missing piece in a route planned from Stalać through Užice and Šargan to Vardište from 1907. The Stalać–Užice stretch opened in 1912. The final mountain section, from Užice across Mokra Gora to the border with Bosnia, remained unfinished when war interrupted the work.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)

By 1918, Bosnia and Herzegovina had an extensive narrow-gauge network on the 760-millimetre Bosnian gauge, while parts of Serbia also used narrow gauge. A normal-gauge north–south railway divided the Serbian narrow-gauge lines into two sections. The new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes inherited five distinct railway systems using four different gauges. Railway managers had to make those inherited pieces work together.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

At a railway conference in Belgrade in 1920, experts proposed joining the Bosnian and Serbian 760-millimetre lines into one narrow-gauge system alongside the normal-gauge network. The alternative was to build standard-gauge routes and replace more of the inherited track. The wider plan to use normal gauge for a new Adriatic route would have required dismantling the Bosnian network and cost more; linking the existing narrow-gauge lines preserved what was already there, but left the country with separate systems. Šargan would close one of the gaps between them.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

The choice of 760 millimetres had begun with a contingency. In 1879, the contractor Hügel & Sager used narrow-gauge track it already had in storage in Romania for the Bosanski Brod–Doboj–Zenica military railway. Austria-Hungary later made that width standard for the Bosnian network, in part because normal-gauge construction cost more. Kežić describes the network as a product of military and political interests. After 1918, the Šargan crossing carried that inherited system into a new national project.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

Work on the mountain route began in 1916, while Austria-Hungary occupied part of Serbia. The park operator says about nine kilometres of track had been laid to the foothills when a rockslide during tunnel work stopped construction. Its account says the collapse killed around 200 Russian and Italian prisoners of war. I found no independent contemporary or scholarly confirmation for that casualty total, so it should be read as the operator’s reported estimate, not a verified count.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)

Construction resumed in 1921 under the new kingdom. The line from Užice to Vardište, about 56 kilometres, used old Serbian and Austrian plans and was completed in 1925. The 22.8-kilometre section from Šargan Vitasi through Jatare and Mokra Gora to Vardište was the mountain crossing within that longer link. It was a late piece in a route planned before the war, not a line conceived from scratch after it.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

The engineering trick was to lengthen the climb. The line runs through 22 tunnels and over five bridges; the Šargan tunnel alone measures 1,666 metres. On the figure-eight section, the railway doubles back and crosses its own path at a different level. The loops add distance where the mountain offers little room, letting the train gain height over a longer route instead of forcing a steep direct ascent. From the train, the crossings can be hard to read; the tunnels hide the bends that make them possible.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)[[2]](https://srbijavoz.rs/nostalgija/)

Completed in 1925, the line connected two narrow-gauge networks. The Belgrade–Obrenovac section, which opened in 1928, brought the capital onto the route toward the Adriatic. Kežić gives the link greater ideological than economic value: the railway connected those places on the map, but the operating details still made through-travel awkward.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

“Belgrade to Dubrovnik by one direct train” is too simple a description. Kežić notes that in winter, different carriage-heating systems on the Serbian and Bosnian lines forced railway staff to change locomotives and carriages in Sarajevo. He concludes that the direct train from Belgrade ran to Sarajevo, not Dubrovnik. The full journey to Dubrovnik took about 31 hours, only an hour less than an alternative route. On a map, the new line pointed toward the coast; in operation, that did not make the journey easy.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

The attempt to run the narrow-gauge lines as one system also faltered administratively. In 1929, the Sarajevo railway directorate took control of six narrow-gauge lines in Serbia, including the Užice–Vardište section. Eleven months later, Belgrade took back control of three. Kežić describes the episode as a failed attempt to integrate the Bosnian-Serbian railway space. The line crossed the mountain; the institutions on either side did not settle into one arrangement.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)

## From Ćira to Nostalgija

After 1945, railway planners wanted a network able to carry heavy industry and saw much of the inherited narrow gauge as a limit. The system the Šargan line had been built to join was now judged against national plans for standardized, higher-capacity traffic. The tourist railway preserves a route that was useful in one transport system and later dropped from another.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)[[5]](https://www.zeleznicesrbije.com/novi-zivot-osmice/)

In 1962, plans for the new Belgrade–Bar line considered normalizing or decommissioning 390 kilometres of Serbia’s 700-kilometre narrow-gauge network. During construction of Belgrade–Bar from 1966 to 1976, hundreds of kilometres of narrow-gauge track in western Serbia were rebuilt or closed. The old passenger train was popularly called Ćira; it made its last run over Šargan on 28 February 1974, in the middle of that wider shift. Kežić describes this process as ending in the 1970s, but does not give a separate local reason for this line’s closure.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.29362/2023.2794.kez.19-44)[[4]](https://www.zeleznicesrbije.com/%D0%BE%D0%B4%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B7%D0%B0%D0%BA-%D1%9B%D0%B8%D1%80%D0%B5/)

The line’s second life began with local effort. In the late 1990s, residents of Mokra Gora cleared the abandoned route in volunteer work, and the railway company ŽTP Beograd decided in 1999 to restore it for tourism. Restoration took several years. On 1 September 2003, the first museum train began carrying visitors over the revived section.[[5]](https://www.zeleznicesrbije.com/novi-zivot-osmice/)

The museum route is not the full historic line. Today’s excursion begins at Mokra Gora and runs to Šargan Vitasi and back. Srbijavoz also describes a shorter winter service to Jatare, but the timetable on its page is labelled 2022–23, so it is not a current schedule. Departure dates, prices and locomotive arrangements can change; check the operator before travelling. In 2011, a museum train also made its first run across Vardište and Dobrun to Višegrad, a separate excursion beyond the regular mountain route.[[2]](https://srbijavoz.rs/nostalgija/)[[5]](https://www.zeleznicesrbije.com/novi-zivot-osmice/)

From Belgrade, an OpenStreetMap/OSRM road-route estimate to Mokra Gora is about 242 kilometres and three hours twenty-six minutes each way, before stops or live traffic. That makes the train a substantial outing, not a quick hop from the capital; plan the drive around the railway’s actual departure times. Nearby on Mećavnik, the cultural complex Drvengrad was created by Emir Kusturica, who drew inspiration for it while filming *Life Is a Miracle* in Mokra Gora. It is a separate stop, not part of the railway museum.[[6]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/drvengrad-on-mecavnik)[[7]](https://www.openstreetmap.org/directions?engine=fossgis_osrm_car&route=44.8153318%2C20.4456588%3B43.7897531%2C19.5031247)

On the map, the detour is obvious. From the train, tunnels and crossings break it into pieces. The present excursion covers only the restored section, but the route still shows why railway builders bent the line around Šargan: it had to gain height and connect tracks that ended at the same border.[[1]](https://www.parkprirodemokragora.org/index.php/en/tourism/the-sarganska-osmica)[[2]](https://srbijavoz.rs/nostalgija/)
