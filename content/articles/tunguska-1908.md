A fireball crossed the sky above central Siberia in the morning of 30 June 1908, then burst in the air over the basin of the Podkamennaya Tunguska River. The site lies in remote taiga now within Evenkiysky District, Krasnoyarsk Krai. NASA gives the incoming object's estimated diameter as 50 to 100 metres and places the burst roughly 6 to 10 kilometres above the ground. The energy flattened and scorched forest; it did not leave a conventional impact crater. [[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-cosmic-explosion-over-siberia-154488/)

Longo's review estimates that 2,150 ± 50 square kilometres of taiga were devastated and about 80 million trees were flattened. The damage formed a broad, roughly butterfly-shaped zone. Trees far from the centre lay in directions that pointed away from it. The region was sparsely inhabited, and the human record is thin; NASA's workshop report mentions anecdotal accounts of several deaths in nomadic camps about 25 kilometres from the centre, but the exact toll is unknown. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-32711-0_18)[[9]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20190002302)

One of the best-known accounts came from Semyon Borisovich Semenov, who lived at the Vanavara trading post, about 70 kilometres from the centre of the destruction. His recollection reached Leonid Kulik in a letter dated 26 March 1927. Semenov described sitting on his porch when a fiery flare appeared to the north-west, then feeling sudden heat and a blast that threw him from the porch and shook the buildings hard enough to break windows. The account was recorded almost nineteen years after the event. It is testimony to what he experienced, not a thermometer reading or a timed observation. [[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-cosmic-explosion-over-siberia-154488/)[[3]](https://tunguska.tsc.ru/ru/science/1/0/7/)

Evenki brothers Chuchancha and Chekaren were closer to the blast, camped by the Avarkitta River. Ethnographer Innokenty Suslov recorded their account in 1926. They described being woken by wind and falling trees, then seeing what they called a second sun above the forest. Their viewpoint differed from Semenov's at Vanavara: the brothers were in the taiga itself. Both accounts are approximate memories, but they give investigators independent perspectives to compare with the instrument records. [[4]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-76574-7_1)

Some of that evidence was already waiting in instrument records. Seismographs at Irkutsk, Tashkent, Tiflis and Jena registered ground waves; barographs and microbarographs recorded pressure changes in the air. The records were not immediately identified with a meteor. The Russian seismic traces appeared in print in 1910, Jena's followed later, and a 1925 paper first linked them to Tunguska. Whipple made the connection to British pressure records in 1930. The two kinds of wave travelled by different routes and at different speeds, so comparisons across distant observatories helped constrain the explosion's timing. None could identify what the object was made of. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-32711-0_18)[[5]](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1477-870x.1930.tb00133.x)

The nights after the explosion were strange over a much larger area than the forest itself. Observers across Eurasia reported unusually bright skies and lingering twilight. Longo's review places the reports across roughly 12 million square kilometres, from Ireland to Krasnoyarsk; one observer wrote that the midnight sky was bright enough to read a newspaper without artificial light. The reports extend the event's footprint beyond the taiga, but their cause is less certain. Proposed explanations include material lofted into the upper atmosphere; researchers still debate the plume's composition and effects. The glow is evidence of atmospheric disturbance, not a sample of the object. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-32711-0_18)[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-earth-060115-012218)

The site was hard to reach. Kulik's 1921 expedition gathered reports but did not reach the damaged forest. His successful attempt began in Leningrad in February 1927. He travelled east by train, continued overland by sled and horse-drawn transport, and reached the Vanavara trading post on 25 March. With an Evenki guide, the party pushed into the taiga; by 13 April they had reached the beginning of the flattened forest. Kulik established the radial pattern by 30 May and returned from the field in June. It was a long expedition just to see the damage the witnesses had described. [[6]](https://www.nasa.gov/history/115-years-ago-the-tunguska-asteroid-impact-event/)[[17]](https://tunguska.tsc.ru/ru/science/1/2/6/1/index.html)

Kulik expected to find meteorites and a crater. Instead, he found a pattern in the forest. Trees around the centre had been thrown outward; many trunks nearer it remained upright, stripped of branches and bark. The radial arrangement made the blast's centre visible even where the forest had grown back. No large crater appeared. Later aerial photographs and surveys extended the map from Kulik's ground observations. The damage pointed to an explosion above the forest, but left no hole to mark its source. [[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-cosmic-explosion-over-siberia-154488/)[[6]](https://www.nasa.gov/history/115-years-ago-the-tunguska-asteroid-impact-event/)

At the centre, the standing trunks came to be described as a “telegraph-pole forest.” The name applies to the stripped trees at the core, not the whole destruction zone: surrounding trees fell in different directions, and the map is not a neat circle. Aerial surveys show a broader, butterfly-shaped pattern. Longo's estimate of 2,150 ± 50 square kilometres and 80 million flattened trees combines field measurements and later mapping, not a count of each trunk. Treefall directions locate the blast's centre more firmly than they determine its yield; energy estimates require a model to translate the damage into pressure and heat. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-32711-0_18)

Kulik's team called it a meteorite, and that name remains common. In current usage, a meteor is the luminous event in the atmosphere; a meteorite is material that survives to the ground. Tunguska left abundant evidence of the first, but no large recovered fragment that settles the body's identity. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-32711-0_18)[[9]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20190002302)

## An explosion without a crater

The lack of a crater fits an atmospheric airburst: most of the energy was released in the air, rather than by a solid impact at ground level. An object moving at cosmic speed carries kinetic energy. As it plunges into denser air, pressure and heating build; aerodynamic stresses can deform and fragment it. The fragments expose more area to the air, lose energy rapidly, and produce a blast wave. If the body breaks up high enough, the atmosphere receives most of the energy before a large mass can strike the ground. That is an airburst. [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1038/361040a0)[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-earth-060115-012218)

Most of the damage came from the shock wave and the hot plume. In one family of models, the initial fireball and vapor rise above the entry path, cool, then collapse back through the atmosphere. The descending plume can carry heat and momentum toward the ground even when no large fragment survives. Farther out, the direction of fallen trunks helps trace the blast. Models differ on how much energy that collapse delivers to the forest. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-32711-0_18)[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijimpeng.2008.07.053)

One influential model came from Chyba, Thomas and Zahnle's 1993 Nature paper, which treated Tunguska as the atmospheric disruption of a stony asteroid. Their calculations put the energy release at 10 to 20 megatons of TNT equivalent, around 10 kilometres above the surface. The paper argued that a stony body tens of metres in radius could break up in the atmosphere; comets and carbon-rich asteroids of comparable energy, under their assumptions, would disrupt higher, while typical iron bodies would be more likely to reach the ground and form a crater. No recovered sample confirms the body's composition. [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1038/361040a0)

Later simulations challenged some assumptions used to estimate blast damage. Boslough and Crawford argued that the hot plume and descending flow from an airburst could carry energy closer to the ground than a simple point-source explosion model suggests. That changes the yield needed to match the observed treefall. In their 2008 paper, they reviewed estimates as low as 3–5 megatons and described 10–15 megatons as the then-common range, while making the case that an event of 5 megatons or less could produce the effects. The disagreement is not a rounding error: it comes from how researchers interpret old seismograms, pressure records, forest health, topography and the way an airburst couples energy to the ground. [[8]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijimpeng.2008.07.053)

NASA's 2018 Tunguska workshop brought together several modern modelling approaches. Its report says the models converged on a most likely energy of 10–20 megatons, with a stony-asteroid diameter of roughly 50–80 metres under the assumed composition. It also stresses that quantitative measurements from 1908 are sparse. Treefall maps record where the blast damaged the forest, but not the object's speed or composition. [[9]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20190002302)

The 3–5 megaton low estimate, the 10–15 megaton range Boslough and Crawford called common in 2008, and NASA's 10–20 megaton workshop range are distinct model-based estimates, not one measured yield. A megaton is an energy unit equivalent to a million tonnes of TNT; it does not mean the object contained explosive material. [[8]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijimpeng.2008.07.053)[[9]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20190002302)

The nature of the object is unsettled too. Chyba and colleagues developed a stony-asteroid model; other researchers have considered a comet or a more fragile, carbon-rich body. Farinella and co-authors analysed 886 possible pre-atmospheric orbits and found that, within their method and assumptions, 83 percent were more consistent with an asteroidal source and 17 percent with a cometary one. The authors also note that the distinction is difficult: some possible source regions overlap, and the result depends on which trajectories and populations are included. No fragment large enough to settle the composition has been recovered. [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1038/361040a0)[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361:20011054)

## Chelyabinsk's better records

On 15 February 2013, an asteroid exploded over Chelyabinsk, Russia. Popova and an international team estimated its pre-atmospheric diameter at 19.8 ± 4.6 metres, far smaller than Tunguska's estimated body. It burst above a populated region: the flash came first, then the shock wave shattered windows, and flying glass and debris injured residents. Cameras, satellites, seismic and infrasound instruments, and recovered meteorites let the team reconstruct the object's path, energy release, damage and fragments. Chelyabinsk became a modern case against which researchers could test airburst models. [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-earth-060115-012218)[[12]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1242642)

Tunguska's evidence consists of witness recollections, scattered instrument records and a damaged forest. Chelyabinsk offered a measured path and recovered fragments, but its asteroid was smaller and it burst above different terrain. Researchers cannot scale its yield directly to the Siberian event; its measurements still help test airburst models. [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-earth-060115-012218)[[12]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1242642)

A 50–100-metre body poses a regional rather than global hazard; an airburst over a city could still cause severe damage. NASA's Near-Earth Object Observations Program focuses its formal discovery goal on finding, tracking and characterizing at least 90 percent of the predicted population of near-Earth objects 140 metres and larger. Tunguska's estimated object was below that size target. The 140-metre goal is a survey target, not a boundary between harmless and dangerous objects. [[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-cosmic-explosion-over-siberia-154488/)[[13]](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/near-earth-object-observations-program/)

Finding an object early matters because a deflection mission needs time to change its course. NASA's mission report says a kinetic impactor needs an appropriately sized spacecraft and typically many years to decades of warning, so a small orbital change has time to alter a future encounter. DART provided one practical demonstration: in 2022, a spacecraft deliberately struck Dimorphos, the roughly 150-metre moon of asteroid Didymos. A Nature analysis measured a 33.0 ± 1.0 minute reduction in Dimorphos's orbital period. The binary system made that change measurable from Earth. The mission tested a kinetic impactor against a known target; it did not test every asteroid type, or an object discovered only as it entered the atmosphere. [[14]](https://science.nasa.gov/planetary-defense-dart/)[[15]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05805-2)[[16]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230015804)

Tunguska's cause is understood as an atmospheric explosion, but the body itself remains unidentified. Planetary defense now has systematic searches and a demonstrated way to change an asteroid's orbit; both are useful only if an object is found with enough warning to act. [[13]](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/near-earth-object-observations-program/)[[16]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230015804)
