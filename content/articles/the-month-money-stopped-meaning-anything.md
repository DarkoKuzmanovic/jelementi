In the second week of July 1946, the Hungarian National Bank issued a banknote with a face value of one hundred quintillion pengo: 100,000,000,000,000,000,000 of them.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) It was the highest denomination any country had ever released into circulation, and the last pengo banknote ever released.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) Within weeks it was demonetized along with every other note in the currency. The entire money supply of Hungary, all the notes in every till and wallet in the country, converted into a fraction of a single forint.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)

Economists conventionally define hyperinflation as starting in any month when prices rise by more than fifty percent.[[5]](https://centerforfinancialstability.org/books/Makinen_excerpt.pdf) Hungary cleared that bar beyond all recognition. Between August 1945 and July 1946 the country suffered the most extreme inflation ever recorded.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations)

## The worst month in monetary history

The peak month was July 1946. That month prices rose by 41,900,000,000,000,000 percent: 41.9 quadrillion percent, or 4.19 times ten to the sixteenth.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations) The two sources that matter most agree on the figure. It appears in the American Numismatic Society's survey of hyperinflation and in the Hanke and Krus table of every known hyperinflation, compiled at Johns Hopkins and published by the Cato Institute.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations)

Numbers that large stop working as numbers, so here is the translation the economists use. Spread across the month, the rate equals about 207 percent per day, which means prices doubled every fifteen hours.[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations) A price fixed on Monday morning had doubled by Monday night and doubled again by Tuesday afternoon. Four doublings fit inside two and a half days. By the end of the episode, the overall price level had risen by a factor of two times ten to the twenty-fifth.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)

Nothing else in the record comes close. Zimbabwe's November 2008 peak doubled prices roughly every twenty-five hours, and Weimar Germany's October 1923 peak needed days, not hours.[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations)[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) Hungary's July stands alone at the top of every ranking, and the underlying price data behind the modern table goes back to a 1948 study in the American Economic Review.[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations)

The daily figures deserve one caution. The Numismatic Society reports that inflation culminated at 350 percent a day in the summer of 1946.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) That is higher than the 207 percent derived from the monthly total.[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations) The two numbers describe different things: the worst days ran hotter than the month's average pace. Either way, money did not survive the week, let alone the month.

## A respectable currency with terrible luck

The pengo had not started as a joke. It was introduced on 1 January 1927 as Hungary's national currency after the korona was ruined by First World War inflation, defined at 3,800 pengo to a kilogram of gold.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[3]](https://www.mnb.hu/en/the-central-bank/organisation/history) The National Bank of Hungary, founded in 1924, stabilized the old crown first and then issued the new money.[[3]](https://www.mnb.hu/en/the-central-bank/organisation/history) Hungary was no stranger to the genre: the country appears twice in the hyperinflation table, with a smaller postwar episode peaking in July 1923 before the pengo existed.[[2]](https://www.cato.org/working-paper/world-hyperinflations) For a decade and a half the new currency behaved like ordinary money.

Then came the Second World War, and after it the settlement. About forty percent of Hungary's wealth was destroyed in the fighting of 1944 and 1945.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) An interim government took over in December 1944 while German and Soviet armies were still contesting the country, and elections followed in November 1945.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) The new state inherited wrecked industry, collapsed tax revenue, and three kinds of bills it could not pay: the cost of supporting Soviet occupation troops, war indemnities owed to the Soviet Union, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, and the ordinary expense of reconstruction.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) By the autumn of 1945, by one press account, the state was spending eleven times what it collected.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/)

With no tax base and no credit, the government chose the printing press. As elsewhere in wartime, the money supply had already grown steeply during the war itself.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) After the war, Soviet troops used their own currency, which left Budapest to finance its depleted budget alone, and printing ever more paper money became the working source of finance.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) Each new issue bought the government a little less than the last one, so the presses ran faster.

## Renaming the zeros

The first response was typographical. When denominations grew too long to fit comfortably on a note, Hungary redefined the unit instead of the number. The milpengo was one million pengo, so a note marked 10,000 milpengo was ten billion pengo wearing a shorter name.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) When even that overflowed, the b.-pengo arrived: one trillion pengo per b.-pengo, twelve zeros absorbed into a single letter.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)

None of this changed any value. It changed how many digits the printers had to set, which mattered because the denominations were now moving too fast for normal banknote production. Late in the collapse, high-denomination notes were produced with simplified offset printing, and in such quantities that some were no longer even numbered.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) Several Budapest printing houses were drafted to help the overstretched national operation keep up.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/)

A Swedish reader may feel a small tremor of recognition here. Where seventeenth-century Stockholm answered heavy money with the first European banknotes, Budapest answered weightless money with words standing in for zeros. Both were printing conveniences. Neither, on its own, fixed anything.

## The tax pengo that could not save itself

In January 1946 the finance ministry tried something more sophisticated: a parallel unit called the adopengo, the tax pengo. It began life as an accounting unit worth exactly one pengo, and its value was adjusted daily so that it would hold steady while the ordinary pengo depreciated.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) The point was to protect one specific thing: the real value of government tax revenue.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) Indexed deposits and indexed units were the novelty of the Hungarian episode, the feature economists still write about.[[5]](https://centerforfinancialstability.org/books/Makinen_excerpt.pdf)

It worked in the narrow sense and failed in every wider one. Measured in pengo, the adopengo climbed from 630 on 1 May to 160,000 on 1 June and to two times ten to the twenty-first by 31 July.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) Since the adopengo's own purchasing power had depreciated as well, the true compound inflation was even higher than that exchange rate implies.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) Indexing changes the unit of account, not the money supply, so the indexed unit tracked the collapse it was built to escape.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)

In the final weeks the experiment ate itself. Adopengo tax bills, first printed in late May, were made legal tender around 8 July.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The parallel unit became ordinary money, subject to the same flight as the currency it had been built to escape. Economists had reason to stare: indexed bank deposits, used by Hungarians as shelter from inflation, made this episode the great outlier in the early studies of hyperinflation.[[5]](https://centerforfinancialstability.org/books/Makinen_excerpt.pdf) By the end it took two hundred million adopengo to buy one forint. The stable unit had outrun the pengo by twenty-one orders of magnitude and still ended worthless.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)

## Shopping with a wheelbarrow

Statistics describe the episode. Prices describe the day. In August 1945 a kilogram of bread cost six pengo. By November it was ninety, in December three hundred and seventy, and by January 1946 eight hundred.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) The figures then leave the realm of groceries: nine and a half million pengo by May, seven point two billion by June.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) Shops changed their price tags several times a day.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/)

Wages were paid daily, and the rational response was to spend them at once.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) Anyone who held money past the afternoon paid a visible penalty for the delay. Hungarian press retrospectives describe workers carrying earnings home in suitcases, and even the wheelbarrow, the classic prop of Weimar photography, makes its appearance in Hungarian accounts too.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) Savings held in pengo simply evaporated, which is why so many surviving notes from the final weeks look crisp and uncirculated: nobody hoarded what was melting, and notes printed days before the reform barely circulated at all.[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/)

Barter, durable goods and the indexed adopengo carried much of real economic life by mid-1946.[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) People kept accepting pengo largely because wages were paid in it and the law required it, not because anyone wanted to hold it for an hour longer than necessary.[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/)

## The biggest numbers ever printed

The banknote series tells the story in compressed form. Between 1 July and 1 August 1946, Hungary issued five new denominations, made the parallel currency legal tender, printed its two largest notes ever, and then retired the entire currency.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The final circulating note, the one hundred million b.-pengo, equaled one hundred million times one trillion: ten to the twentieth pengo, one hundred quintillion.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) It went out on 11 July, by the catalog-based account, and it was the last pengo banknote ever released.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) One Hungarian account dates it a day later, on 12 July.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) Either way it was legal tender for about three weeks and practically worthless on arrival.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/)

There is one larger note, and it never circulated. A one billion b.-pengo note, worth ten to the twenty-first pengo, was printed but never released, overtaken by the currency reform before it could go out.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) An example survives through the numismatic trade, including a specimen associated with the economist François Velde.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) It is the highest face value ever printed on a government banknote.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The distinction is worth keeping straight: the hundred quintillion note is the largest ever circulated, the sextillion note the largest ever printed.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/) For scale, Zimbabwe's famous hundred trillion dollar note is a million times smaller than the circulated Hungarian record.[[7]](https://planetbanknote.com/banknote-blog/hungary-1946-the-worst-hyperinflation-ever/)

## One forint for four hundred octillion

On 31 July 1946 Hungary withdrew the pengo and the adopengo. On 1 August it introduced the forint.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The conversion rate was four times ten to the twenty-ninth pengo to one forint: four hundred octillion to one, twenty-nine zeros retired in a single step.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) It is the largest currency conversion ratio in monetary history.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The adopengo, indexed since January, converted at two hundred million to one.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) (The Numismatic Society gives two billion adopengo to the forint instead, ten times higher and inconsistent with its own pengo figures; the two hundred million figure is the standard one.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)) At the new rate, 11.74 forints equaled one American dollar.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)

The reform worked because it changed the fiscal position, not just the numbers on the notes.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The money supply was restricted, the banking system placed under strict state control, and the budget rebuilt so the state no longer needed the presses.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) The first forint notes were produced under tight monitoring to prevent counterfeiting and public panic.[[8]](https://dailynewshungary.com/hungarys-nightmare-hyperinflation-of-1946/) The Numismatic Society's summary is blunt: controlled paper money supply and fiscal reform eradicated the inflation.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/) Bomberger and Makinen, the economists who made the episode their subject, put it more formally: the inflation was the most intense on record, and the reforms of August 1946 were immediately and entirely successful in stabilizing prices.[[4]](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/261182)

The success held. The forint is still Hungary's currency, through the communist decades, the transition of 1989 and European Union membership.[[1]](https://numismatics.org/times-of-hyperinflation/)[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) Leftover adopengo bills lingered until the end of September, then lapsed.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/) The worst hyperinflation in history ended in a single step, and it never came back.[[6]](https://learn.planetbanknote.com/pengo-to-forint-1946/)
