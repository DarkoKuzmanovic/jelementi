On 1 August 1969, a three-metre telescope at California’s Lick Observatory sent a ruby-laser pulse toward the Apollo 11 landing site.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99) The pulse came back at the predicted time.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99) The Moon was about 384,000 kilometres away; the round trip took roughly two and a half seconds.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) In the returning signal, the observatory had found the first light reflected from the small instrument that Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin had set on the lunar surface twelve days earlier.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99)

The instrument was a panel of one hundred glass corner cubes, each about 3.8 centimetres across.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html) A corner cube is not an ordinary mirror.[[6]](https://repository.hou.usra.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5855ed82-b3c7-4ca1-a64a-a3e0e19ba271/content)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Three reflective faces meet at right angles inside it; a ray entering the cube bounces from those faces and leaves travelling almost straight back along its incoming line.[[6]](https://repository.hou.usra.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5855ed82-b3c7-4ca1-a64a-a3e0e19ba271/content)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) That makes it a retroreflector: instead of scattering light in every direction, it sends a portion back toward the telescope that launched it.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) The Apollo panel needed no battery, radio or moving part.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html)

A simple timing measurement does the rest.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) The observatory records when a pulse leaves, then when returning photons arrive.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Multiply the round-trip time by the speed of light and divide by two, and you have the distance to the reflector.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds) Getting a pulse to the Moon, then recognising its faint return against the lit lunar surface, is the hard part.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)

Apollo 11 was the first, but not the last.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html) Apollo 14 and Apollo 15 left arrays too.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html) Soviet Luna missions carried French-built arrays on the Lunokhod 1 and Lunokhod 2 rovers.[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) The Apollo 11 and 14 panels each contain one hundred corner cubes; Apollo 15’s has three hundred.[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Each Lunokhod array has fourteen larger cubes.[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) The reflectors are passive: their continuing usefulness comes from their geometry, not from electronics still working on the Moon.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)

Apollo 15’s larger panel was meant to return a stronger signal.[[6]](https://repository.hou.usra.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5855ed82-b3c7-4ca1-a64a-a3e0e19ba271/content) Its extra cubes were intended to make returns more frequent and lunar ranging possible with smaller-aperture telescopes and simpler equipment.[[6]](https://repository.hou.usra.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/5855ed82-b3c7-4ca1-a64a-a3e0e19ba271/content) The wider physical array also spreads the return pulse in time as its tilt changes.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)

Lick and McDonald Observatory in Texas made the first measurements of the Apollo 11 reflector.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.167.3917.368) Lick detected the first returns on 1 August 1969; McDonald made its first acquisition measurements on 20 August and again in September.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.167.3917.368) Over the following decades, lunar ranging also became a regular programme at the Observatoire de la Côte d’Azur near Grasse.[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html) Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico added the APOLLO system in 2006, using a 3.5-metre telescope and single-photon detectors.[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128) Its dataset through 2020 reported a median nightly range accuracy of 1.7 millimetres.[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128) Other stations have joined or tested lunar ranging as well.[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html)

A laser pulse does not return as a bright green line for anyone to admire; the signal loss is severe.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) In Murphy’s representative calculation, with a one-arcsecond beam, roughly one photon in twenty-five million launched from Earth finds the Apollo 11 array.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) On the return leg, a one-metre telescope aperture receives about one photon for every 250 million photons emerging from the reflector.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Once atmospheric, optical and detector losses are included, the typical two-way throughput is around one part in a billion billion, or 10 to the power of minus eighteen.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Lunar laser ranging therefore operates in the single-photon regime.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)

One return does not give a millimetre-level distance.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Early stations accumulated photons across many shots.[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) APOLLO’s large aperture and efficient detectors let it collect far more, then combine arrival times into a range estimate.[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128) The array’s physical size and changing lunar orientation also spread arrival times.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) A useful range comes from repeated observations fitted to a detailed model.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128)

The first measurement answered a narrow question: the reflector was where the calculations said it would be, and it worked.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.166.3901.99) Decades of measurements made the experiment more interesting.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) Lunar laser ranging measures the Earth–Moon distance increasing by about 3.8 centimetres per year.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)

The cause is tidal.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving) The Moon’s gravity raises bulges in Earth’s oceans.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving) Because Earth rotates faster than the Moon travels around it, the bulge on the side ahead of the Moon pulls the Moon forward along its orbit.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving) Earth’s rotation loses angular momentum; the Moon gains orbital energy and moves outward.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving) The reflectors do not push the Moon away; they give scientists a long, accurate ruler with which to see the effect.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)

The same data also reveal how the Moon moves and flexes.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf) Earth’s changing gravity raises and lowers the lunar surface, and the Moon’s rotation wobbles as the Earth–Moon geometry shifts.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf) Researchers fit these motions alongside the orbit.[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf) The pattern of rotation and tidal dissipation is evidence that the Moon has a fluid core.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf) A 2004 analysis by James Williams, Slava Turyshev and Dale Boggs described a fluid core with a radius about twenty per cent of the Moon’s.[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf) The inference depends on models of the lunar interior, and a solid inner core remained unresolved.[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf)

The reflector lets researchers use the Moon as a test body.[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/29/18/184004) Because laser ranging measures the Moon’s orbit precisely, researchers can look for the small changes predicted by competing gravity theories.[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/29/18/184004) One test asks whether Earth and Moon fall toward the Sun at the same rate.[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/29/18/184004) They differ in composition and gravitational self-energy; if gravity treated those contributions differently, the lunar orbit would shift in a measurable way.[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/29/18/184004) This is a strong equivalence principle test associated with the Nordtvedt effect.[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/29/18/184004)

A separate analysis asks whether Newton’s gravitational constant, G, changes over time.[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6382/aa8f7a) A changing value would alter the calculated orbit.[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6382/aa8f7a) In a 2018 study using lunar laser ranging data from 1970 through January 2015, F. Hofmann and J. Müller reported no deviations from Einstein’s theory within the estimated accuracies.[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6382/aa8f7a) Their solution gave a gravitational-constant variation of (7.1 ± 7.6) × 10⁻¹⁴ per year, an Earth–Moon equivalence-principle result of (−3 ± 5) × 10⁻¹⁴, and a Nordtvedt parameter of (−0.2 ± 1.1) × 10⁻⁴.[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6382/aa8f7a) These are the values from that analysis, not a claim that one paper ended the search.[[11]](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6382/aa8f7a)

Every new observation extends the comparison between the Moon’s measured orbit and a detailed prediction.[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128) In 1962, Faller proposed the reflector idea; advances in ground equipment have since extracted more information from the same arrays.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294)[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128) The arrays themselves now limit further gains in precision.[[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11128) New reflectors placed at different lunar locations could help separate lunar rotation, tides and orbital motion.[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf)

The Apollo 11 panel has no power source and no way to report its own condition.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[5]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/missions/satellite_missions/current_missions/ap11_reflector.html) Its reply is a handful of photons, arriving about two and a half seconds after a pulse leaves Earth.[[2]](https://www.nist.gov/history/moon-and-back-25-seconds)[[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6294) For decades, those arrival times have let researchers measure the Moon’s retreat and flexing, then test gravitational predictions against its orbit.[[1]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-11/the-apollo-experiment-that-keeps-on-giving)[[9]](https://ilrs.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/williams_cospar_2004.pdf)[[10]](https://doi.org/10.1088/0264-9381/29/18/184004)
