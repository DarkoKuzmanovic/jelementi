Belgrade Fortress stands on a ridge at the point where the Sava flows into the Danube. The ridge rises above both rivers and overlooks the plains to the north and west. Whoever held it could watch and control the flat country across the water, and people have lived on it since prehistoric times.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The rock is far older than any of them. Part of the slope between the fortress's upper and lower halves is a protected natural monument, Kalemegdan Cape (*Kalemegdanski rt*). It preserves a cross-section of the shore of the Pannonian Sea from the oldest stage of that sea's history, in sediments laid down in the Miocene, millions of years before anyone built anything here. The same pale stone is probably the reason the city is called Belgrade.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/o-kompleksu/kalemegdanski-rt/?lang=en)

First, the names. Belgrade Fortress (*Beogradska tvrđava*) is the fortification itself, divided into the Upper Town (*Gornji grad*) on top of the ridge and the Lower Town (*Donji grad*) below it. Kalemegdan is, strictly, the park around it: Great and Little Kalemegdan Park, laid out on what had been an open field in front of the fortress. Fortress and park together form a single protected whole.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The name Kalemegdan is Turkish and much younger than anything in this article. In everyday speech it usually means the park and the fortress together.

## Farmers, Celts and a shared name

Finds on the plateau of the Upper Town show that the first settlement on the ridge dates from the Neolithic, the New Stone Age.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

Celts arrived in the third century BCE. In 279 BCE a Celtic campaign against Delphi in Greece failed, and in the years that followed a Celtic tribe, the Scordisci, settled around the Danube. There they met the Illyrian Autariatae and other Thracian and Illyrian tribes.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The town's first name records that meeting. According to the fortress's own history, *Singidunum* joins a Thracian or Dacian tribal name, *Singi*, to the Celtic word *dunum*, meaning a town.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The Scordisci were good warriors and, once settled, took up farming and pottery. By the middle of the second century BCE they were minting their own coins.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

Celtic Singidunum was not on the ridge, though. Excavations put it in what is now Karaburma, another part of Belgrade.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The name only moved up to the ridge with the Romans.

## The legion's camp

The Romans built their first military camp on the ridge at the start of the first century CE, most probably between 6 and 11, after barbarian attacks there became more frequent.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

It began as an earth rampart topped with a timber palisade. A stone castrum, a fortified legionary camp, came later. It was a rectangle about 560 metres long and 350 metres wide, covering today's Upper Town and the part of Kalemegdan Park that runs to Pariska Street, and it enclosed about 20 hectares.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0/?lang=en) From the beginning of the second century, Singidunum was the base of the Fourth Flavian Legion, Legio IV Flavia, nicknamed *Felix*, "lucky", for its successes in war.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

A town grew up alongside the camp. Under Emperor Hadrian, who ruled from 117 to 138, Singidunum became a *municipium*, a town with limited self-government. Some time between 211 and 287 it was raised to a *colonia*, a settlement whose people had full Roman civic rights.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/)

Its houses looked like houses anywhere else in the Roman provinces. They were built of stone quarried at Tašmajdan or Topčider and arranged around an atrium, a central hall with a pool that collected rainwater through a square opening in the roof. Wealthier houses had a second courtyard surrounded by columns, the peristyle, with a small garden, fountains and statues, and they had their own bathrooms.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%83%D1%9B%D0%B5-%D1%83-%D1%81%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B3%D0%B8%D0%B4%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D0%BC%D1%83/?lang=en) The town had doctors, too. Forceps, scalpels, hooks and small spoons have been found, along with the seal of an eye doctor, which turned up in the Lower Town.[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%B8%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%83-%D1%81%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B3%D0%B8%D0%B4%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D0%BC%D1%83/?lang=en)

Very little of the camp can be seen today, because later builders either put their walls on top of it or destroyed it.[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0/?lang=en) The easiest piece to find is at the edge of the park. One of the castrum's gates, the *Porta Decumana*, stood on the line of today's Knez Mihailova Street. The foundations of this monumental gate, which was reinforced by two inner and two outer square towers, can be seen in the Roman Hall of the Belgrade City Library, at the entrance to Kalemegdan Park.[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0/?lang=en)

At the opposite end of the camp, a square Roman corner tower about nine metres across, only partly explored, lies under the medieval corner tower of the Upper Town now called the Despot Tower. Beside it, under the medieval rampart, about 110 metres of Roman wall survive, one or two courses of stone high.[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0/?lang=en)

Singidunum also gave Rome an emperor, briefly. Jovian, emperor in 363 and 364, is described in a late Roman digest of imperial lives, the *Epitome de Caesaribus*, as "an inhabitant of the district of Singidunum", and he is usually said to have been born there, around 331.[[7]](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/victor/victor.caes2.html)[[8]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jovian) He reigned for eight months. According to the *Epitome*, he died suddenly while hurrying back from Persia to Constantinople in a hard winter, aged nearly forty, overcome by indigestion and by the fresh plaster of a new building.[[7]](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/victor/victor.caes2.html)

## Christians in Singidunum

Christianity reached Singidunum while Christians were still being persecuted. The town remembers two local martyrs, Hermylus and Stratonicus. By 343 it was a bishopric: it appears among the episcopal cities in the acts of the church council held that year at Serdica, today's Sofia.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B7%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%98%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BA%D0%BE%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B2/?lang=en)

The best physical evidence is a stone coffin. In 1885 a Roman sarcophagus was found by chance at the corner of Kapetan Mišina and Gospodar Jovanova streets, on the Danube side of the city, where Singidunum's north-eastern cemetery had been. It is made of porous limestone and is just over two metres long. Its front panel had been framed for an inscription in a style used from the second century onward, but in the early fourth century someone carved a Christian scene into it instead.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B7%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%98%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BA%D0%BE%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B2/?lang=en)

The carving is rough and crowded. On the right, the prophet Jonah is on a ship, is thrown overboard, is swallowed by a whale and is spat out onto the shore. On the left is Christ as the Good Shepherd, with a lamb across his shoulders. Jonah's story stood for the belief in resurrection, and the coffin shows that Singidunum had an organised church in the fourth century. It belongs to the National Museum in Belgrade, and when the fortress presented it in 2013 it was kept in the Great Gunpowder Magazine in the Lower Town.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BF%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B7%D0%B5%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%98%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BA%D0%BE%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B2/?lang=en)

That building, a powder store from the great Austrian rebuilding of the fortress in the eighteenth century, opened to visitors in 2014 as a display of Roman sarcophagi, tombstones, altars and other carved stone from the first to the fourth century. Most of them were found in and around Belgrade, many in the city centre along the line of Singidunum's ancient cemetery.[[10]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B2%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B0-%D1%81%D0%B5-%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D1%83%D1%82%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD/?lang=en) More Roman stone from Singidunum stands outdoors in the Upper Town, in the Ancient Lapidarium next to the building of the city's Institute for the Protection of Monuments.[[11]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%84%D0%BE/?lang=en)

## Huns, Goths and Justinian's walls

When the Roman Empire was divided in 395, Singidunum went to the eastern half, the empire we call Byzantium.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/) The Byzantines called it Singidon. It stood at a crossroads, and during the great migrations of the following centuries many peoples passed through it or stayed.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

In 441 the Huns under Attila broke into the Balkans and destroyed a string of cities, Singidon among them. After Attila died and his state fell apart, Ostrogoths, Gepids and Sarmatians settled around the town.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The Ostrogoths took it through a teenager. Jordanes, the sixth-century historian of the Goths, says that Theodoric, son of the Ostrogothic king Thiudimer, was eighteen when he gathered nearly 6,000 men, crossed the Danube without his father's knowledge and killed Babai, king of the Sarmatians. Theodoric then seized Singidunum, which the Sarmatians had occupied, and instead of returning it to the Romans he kept it for himself.[[12]](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/iordanes1.html) That was in 471. He held the town for 17 years, until he left for Italy.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The rebuilding came under Justinian I, emperor from 527 to 565, who began restoring the town shortly before 535 as part of his defence of the border.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) Procopius, who wrote an account of Justinian's building works, tells both halves of the story. Barbarians had captured Singidunum and razed it, "leaving the place quite destitute of inhabitants". Justinian then "restored the entire city and surrounded it with a very strong fortification, and thus made it once more a famous and important city".[[13]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Procopius/Buildings/4B%2A.html)

## The white city

The Serbs enter the story through an emperor's handbook. In the tenth century, the Byzantine emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus had a handbook compiled on the empire's neighbours, known by its Latin title, *De administrando imperio*. Its account of the Serbs says they first settled in the empire with the consent of Emperor Heraclius. Later some of them decided to go home, crossed the Danube, changed their minds, and asked Heraclius for other land, sending their request through the strategos, the Byzantine military governor, of Belgrade.[[14]](https://balcanica.rs/index.php/journal/article/view/68) The fortress's history dates this to around 630.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) Constantine was writing three centuries later, and historians have argued over every part of the passage, including the governor and the name of the city.[[14]](https://balcanica.rs/index.php/journal/article/view/68)

The new name is easier to explain than to date. Seen from below, the white limestone ridge and the early Byzantine walls, built of stone of the same colour, must have stood out in the landscape. That is the usual explanation for what the Slavs called the place: *Beli grad*, the White City, which became *Beograd*.[[15]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4/?lang=en)[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/o-kompleksu/kalemegdanski-rt/?lang=en) The same history is less sure about when the Slavic town grew up on the ruins of the ancient one. There is no reliable evidence, it says, though it is assumed to have happened around the turn of the eighth and ninth centuries.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/) Excavations have uncovered the remains of a Slav settlement in the Lower Town, which most researchers date to the ninth century.[[14]](https://balcanica.rs/index.php/journal/article/view/68)

The Slavs also found something older still standing. In the same tenth-century handbook, Belgrade appears in a short list of ancient landmarks, and the only thing mentioned in it is "the pyrgos of the holy and great emperor Constantine": his tower. The historian Jovanka Kalić argues that this means Constantine the Great, who ruled from 306 to 337, and that the tower probably stood in the commanding north-western part of the Upper Town. Archaeology has not yet confirmed it.[[14]](https://balcanica.rs/index.php/journal/article/view/68)

At the start of the ninth century the map around Belgrade changed. The Avar khaganate disappeared, the Franks took control of parts of Pannonia, and the Bulgarian state expanded to the north-west. In the second and third decades of the century Belgrade came under Bulgarian rule, as a border town facing the Franks.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

## The first written Belgrade

That is why the first written "Belgrade" is in a letter to a Bulgarian ruler. On 16 April 878, Pope John VIII wrote to Boris of Bulgaria, addressing him by his Christian name, Michael.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[16]](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Epistolae_et_decreta_(Ioannes_VIII))

Most of the letter is church politics. The pope is unhappy that Boris has been following "the Greeks", meaning the church of Constantinople. He invites him to ask his elders whether the Greeks have ever been without one heresy or another, urges him to come back to Rome, and thanks him for a gift. Then, at the end, comes the passage that matters here. Translated from the Latin, it tells Boris that Sergius the eunuch, "a Slav by birth and entangled in many vices", had obtained the priesthood by deceit. He had then been found out, convicted of other misdeeds and deposed by his own bishop, and after that he had been promoted "quite unworthily" to the bishopric of Belgrade by a certain George, "who falsely claims the title of bishop". By the authority of the apostles Peter and Paul, the holy canons and the pope's own decree, Sergius was now deposed.[[16]](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Epistolae_et_decreta_(Ioannes_VIII))

The Latin word is *Belogradensem*, "of Belgrade". The fortress's official history sums the letter up as the pope writing that "Slav Sergije is at the head of the Belgrade episcopate".[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) That is true, in the way a dismissal notice confirms someone's job title.

Over the next four centuries the town passed between Bulgarians, Byzantines and Hungarians, and it was knocked down and rebuilt several times.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) Part 2 picks up the story there.

## Dates to remember

- 279 BCE: a Celtic campaign against Delphi fails; the Scordisci then settle by the Danube
- 6–11 CE: the first Roman military camp on the ridge
- early 2nd century: the Fourth Flavian Legion is based at Singidunum
- 343: Singidunum is listed as a bishopric at the Council of Serdica
- 395: the Roman Empire is divided and Singidunum goes to the East
- 441: the Huns destroy the city
- 471: Theodoric the Ostrogoth takes it
- shortly before 535: Justinian begins rebuilding it
- c. 630: Serbs at Belgrade, according to Constantine Porphyrogenitus
- 16 April 878: the first written mention of Belgrade, in Pope John VIII's letter
