The Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge crosses the Drina at Višegrad, in eastern Bosnia and Herzegovina. Its eleven stone arches carry a roadway from the town's side of the river toward the opposite bank. There, instead of continuing straight inland, the approach turns at a right angle and follows the water. The bridge and its approach solve two different problems: getting across the river and getting onto the crossing in the first place. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[2]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1260)

The crossing itself is about 179.5 metres long. Its separate approach ramp, about 120 metres long, has four smaller arches. These are not four of the eleven river arches: they belong to the road leading up to them. The distinction matters when looking at the whole structure, whose shape is less like a straight line than a long stroke with a bent end. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

It was built between 1571 and 1577 for Mehmed Paša Sokolović, the Ottoman grand vizier, to a design by the imperial architect Mimar Sinan. In the twentieth century Ivo Andrić made the bridge the organising centre of his novel *The Bridge on the Drina*. The building and the book share a setting, but they are different kinds of evidence: one is a bridge that has been repaired many times; the other is a work of fiction about people and centuries. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[4]](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1961/andric/biographical)[[6]](https://ivoandric.org.rs/english/works/novels/the-bridge-on-the-drina)

## A road meets a river

At Višegrad the Drina flows through a mountain landscape before continuing north toward the Sava and ultimately the Danube. The bridge stands just below a sharp bend. On its right bank there is a plain where the town developed; on the left, a rocky hill lies close to the river. An approach heading straight away from the water would have met that ground. The ramp instead runs along the left bank before lifting the road onto the bridge. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

The arrangement is easy to miss if the bridge is described only by its river span. ICOMOS, the international heritage advisory body that evaluated it for UNESCO, describes the ramp as a direct architectural extension of the bridge. Its four arches support the raised approach. The turn is a response to the geography of the bank, not a kink in the main run of eleven arches. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

The crossing formed part of an important route through the Ottoman Balkans. ICOMOS describes it in connection with roads linking the Danube plains, Sarajevo and the Adriatic coast, including Ragusa, now Dubrovnik. A bridge on such a route was more than a local convenience: it helped people and goods move through territory governed from Istanbul. It could also express the power of the person who paid for it. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

Sokolović held the grand vizierate for fourteen years under three sultans. A study by historian Beytullah Mısır places his childhood in the village of Sokolovići near Višegrad. Born into a Christian family, he entered Ottoman service through the devshirme system, under which boys were recruited for imperial service. He later rose through the court and administration. Mısır's account traces this rise in part through the networks Sokolović built over his career. [[3]](https://balkanjournal.org/jbs/article/download/161/88/613)

The system could lead to exceptional advancement, but that does not make the removal of a boy from his family a simple success story. Nor do we need to assign him a modern national identity to explain the bridge. ICOMOS calls the commission a tribute to his native region as well as a work of imperial communication and control. Both purposes can fit the same stone crossing. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[3]](https://balkanjournal.org/jbs/article/download/161/88/613)

## Stone, timber and a place to sit

Sinan was the Ottoman court's chief architect and engineer, responsible for a team working across the empire. ICOMOS attributes both the design and construction of the Višegrad bridge to him; it does not follow that he personally laid its stones. The evaluation describes the substantial labour and money devoted to the project, while giving far fewer particulars about the individual workers than about the finished structure. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

The masonry used dressed travertine from local quarries. Beneath it, the historic foundations included timber rafts strengthened with wooden piles and stone blocks. Those foundations carried the piers, and the piers in turn supported the arches. The bridge was not a single slab reaching the full width of the Drina, but a series of shorter spans resting on supports in the river. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

Each arch transfers the weight above it toward the supports on either side. The slightly pointed openings on the Višegrad bridge span roughly 11 to 15 metres, according to UNESCO's rounded description. ICOMOS gives the smallest more precisely as 10.70 metres. The piers are about 3.90 metres thick. Their projecting ends have different shapes on the two sides of the bridge: triangular on the upstream side and rounded downstream. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[2]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1260)

A raised road also needed a surface and edges. ICOMOS gives the overall roadway width, including its parapets, as 7.20 metres. The central part is the highest, measured in the evaluation at about 15.40 metres above the average water level. That figure is not a fixed clearance for every occasion: the Drina's level changes. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

At the central pier, the passage widens. One side has a wall bearing inscriptions celebrating the bridge and its creators; opposite it is a stone divan, a place to sit. This widening gives the structure a public space as well as a route across the water. In Andrić's novel, the central section becomes a recurring meeting place and a setting for events in the town. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[6]](https://ivoandric.org.rs/english/works/novels/the-bridge-on-the-drina)

The description in the heritage evaluation tells us what the bridge was made of and how its parts were arranged. It does not provide a complete day-by-day construction record. Scaffolding, the order in which the arches were raised, and the conversations of the builders would need their own evidence. The physical details we do have are enough to show the scale of the undertaking: stone piers anchored in a mountain river, eleven arches above them, and a long approach fitted to a difficult bank. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

## The bridge in a book

Ivo Andrić was born in 1892 in Dolac, near Travnik, and went to school in Višegrad and Sarajevo. He later served as a Yugoslav diplomat. When Germany invaded Yugoslavia in 1941, he returned from Berlin to Belgrade, where he lived during the Second World War. His Nobel Prize biography says he wrote three major works during the war, all published in 1945. One was *Na Drini ćuprija*, known in English as *The Bridge on the Drina*. [[4]](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1961/andric/biographical)[[5]](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1961/andric/facts)

The novel takes the bridge and Višegrad as its fixed setting while the people around them change. Its chronicle runs from the sixteenth century to the First World War. Rather than follow one person for a lifetime, it connects a succession of episodes through a place: the bridge is where a reader returns as one generation gives way to another. [[6]](https://ivoandric.org.rs/english/works/novels/the-bridge-on-the-drina)

Andrić does not spend equal time on every century. The account presented by the Ivo Andrić Foundation notes that the bridge's construction occupies several chapters, while other periods pass much more quickly. The later nineteenth and early twentieth centuries receive considerably more space. The pace of the book is therefore not the pace of a calendar; the author can linger over an episode or let years pass between them. [[6]](https://ivoandric.org.rs/english/works/novels/the-bridge-on-the-drina)

This structure makes room for more than the careers of rulers and architects. The foundation describes a mixed town whose stories, legends and experiences meet at the bridge. The novel contains violence and cruelty as well as ordinary encounters, as the Nobel Prize's account of Andrić's work notes. Its central structure is a setting for these lives, not proof that everyone in the town experienced its history in the same way. [[5]](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1961/andric/facts)[[6]](https://ivoandric.org.rs/english/works/novels/the-bridge-on-the-drina)

The book's historical setting can also make a fictional scene feel like a document. A scholarly study of the novel describes its deliberate mixture of historical and fictional material. That distinction is useful for the bridge's origins: a novelist can imagine motives, conversations and private grief; an engineering evaluation can document dimensions and materials. Neither should quietly be made to do the other's job. [[7]](https://doi.org/10.32600/huefd.1256055)

Andrić received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1961. The award was for his literary work, not for the bridge itself. The Nobel biography describes *The Bridge on the Drina* and *Bosnian Story* as chronicles concerned with Bosnia and its history; the prize citation recognised the force with which he portrayed human destinies drawn from the history of his country. [[4]](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1961/andric/biographical)[[5]](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1961/andric/facts)

## Damage and repair

The physical bridge had its own history after the events covered by the novel. The Drina could flood severely: in 1896 an exceptional flood submerged the bridge and swept away its parapets. ICOMOS records later work to stabilise and reinforce the piers. Repairs were not a footnote to an otherwise untouched monument; they are part of the structure's history. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[2]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1260)

During the First World War, two piers were blown up in 1914–1915. A temporary crossing used steel girders, and stone reconstruction followed in 1939–1940. In 1943, during the Second World War, five arches were destroyed. They were rebuilt in stone between 1950 and 1952, using the surviving portions as a model. The eleven-arched bridge seen afterwards retained its historic form, but not every stone in it had remained in place for four centuries. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)

The distinction between damage to the structure and harm to people also matters. ICOMOS states that the 1992–1995 war had no direct impact on the bridge itself. That statement is about the structure; it is not an account of the war's effect on Višegrad's residents. The international tribunal for the former Yugoslavia documented the killing of Muslim civilians in the Višegrad area in 1992, including killings beside the Drina. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[8]](https://www.icty.org/x/cases/milan_lukic_sredoje_lukic/acjug/en/121204_judgement.pdf)

In 2007 the World Heritage Committee inscribed the bridge on UNESCO's World Heritage List. Its assessment recognised the work's architectural and engineering qualities while warning that changes to the river's water levels, associated with nearby hydroelectric stations, threatened its foundations. The concern was about the condition assessed at the time, not a substitute for a current engineering inspection. [[2]](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1260)

The bridge's regular arches can give an impression of a single, unchanged object. Its history is more specific: a sixteenth-century design, a left-bank approach shaped by a rocky hill, damage, and successive repairs. Andrić chose that crossing as the place to which his novel returns. The reader can return to the real bridge too, and see where the road turns before it reaches the river. [[1]](https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/1260.pdf)[[6]](https://ivoandric.org.rs/english/works/novels/the-bridge-on-the-drina)
