On 15 October 1872, the American Arctic expedition ship Polaris was trapped in ice in Smith Sound, between Greenland and Ellesmere Island. A gale and moving ice separated it from a floe carrying nineteen people, including expedition crew and two Inuit families. Fourteen others remained aboard. A U.S. Navy inquiry later judged the separation accidental.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)

Charles Francis Hall, commander of the expedition, had died almost a year earlier. Sidney Budington was in command by October 1872.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)

The floe carried its passengers south toward Labrador. Lt. Commander H. C. White later sailed north aboard the Tigress to search for the Polaris; he wrote of “a drift of 1,800 miles.”[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)

## What did 1,800 miles measure?

White’s 1874 account is the source for the familiar figure. He also described the piece of ice on which the party survived as “scarcely a hundred feet square.” Those are striking numbers, but they are not the product of a modern track reconstructed from continuous location records.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)

An earlier report in Nature, summarizing the U.S. Navy’s examination of the survivors, says they were picked up about 600 miles south of where they began. That describes their change in latitude, not necessarily the full distance traveled as the ice shifted and turned. The surviving accounts do not provide a continuously measured route that lets us calculate the floe’s exact path.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.1038/008217b0)

So the 1,800 miles belongs in the story as a contemporary estimate of the drift, not as a precise surveyed distance. A winding route and a final position are different measures; the ice did not have to move 1,800 miles due south for the floe party to travel that far.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.1038/008217b0)

The Navy’s 1876 roster uses “Joe” and “Hannah” for the Inuit couple, lists their daughter Punny, and includes Hans Hendrik’s family. It counts ten expedition men, two Inuit couples and five children; one baby appears as Charles Polaris. Parks Canada identifies Joe and Hannah as Ipirvik and Taqulittuq, also recorded as Ebierbing and Tookoolito. Kaalund’s scholarly article uses Ipiirvik; English records vary in their transliteration.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)[[6]](https://www.pc.gc.ca/apps/DFHD/page_nhs_eng.aspx?id=1628)[[7]](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0018246X22000139)

Taqulittuq served as an interpreter and made Arctic clothing; Ipirvik hunted and drove dogsleds. Tyson’s printed account also credits Ipirvik and Hans Hendrik with hunting that helped feed the group.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)[[6]](https://www.pc.gc.ca/apps/DFHD/page_nhs_eng.aspx?id=1628)

## Two groups, two winters

The Polaris expedition had been sent north by the United States to explore toward the North Pole. The ship reached Smith Sound and spent the winter of 1871–72 in the Arctic. Hall died aboard it on 8 November 1871, after an illness lasting about two weeks. The expedition continued under Budington.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197004022821406)

The following autumn, the Polaris was again caught among ice and driven south. Tyson’s account describes a north-west gale and heavy ice pressure on 15 October. When the engineer reported that water was rising in the hold, Budington ordered the crew to throw supplies onto the ice. Tyson later wrote that the alarm was false: the ice had lifted the ship and shifted water already in its hold. The men worked three or four hours in the dark, trying to save stores. Then the ice broke apart; the Polaris pulled free, and they lost sight of her almost at once.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

The two groups then faced different journeys. The ship party made winter camp on Greenland’s coast, salvaging material from the Polaris after it became unusable and building boats. They set out south in the summer. On 23 June 1873, they saw the Scottish whaler Ravenscraig about ten miles away, fastened to land ice. A party from the ship walked across the ice to meet them, bringing biscuits. The Polaris crew gathered their records and personal effects, then walked back through soft snow and reached the whaler at midnight. They reached Scotland in October before returning to the United States.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)

The floe party had neither a shore camp nor a dependable route. George E. Tyson, the expedition’s assistant navigator, later described the food, shelter, hunting and movement of the ice in detail. His printed account is valuable, but it is not a journal written continuously on the floe: Tyson said his original journal was lost when the groups separated, and that he reconstructed it from memory and a few brief notes before sailing again on the Tigress.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

At first the party tried to reach land and moved between pieces of ice. Eventually they remained on a larger floe, using what supplies and shelter they could keep with them. Tyson’s account follows their rations closely: as winter wore on, the group was dividing food by ounces and relying on the chance to hunt. They spent eighty-three days without seeing the sun, according to his printed narrative.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

Tyson’s first inventory included fourteen cans of pemmican, eleven and a half bags of bread and fourteen hams for nineteen people. Later he counted nine cans and six bags left. At their current ration, he estimated the pemmican would last two months and the bread three.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

They fashioned a lamp from a pemmican can and a strip of canvas for a wick, but blubber smoke sometimes drove them from the hut. Before long, they had begun breaking up one of their two boats for fuel.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

Tyson wrote that Joe shot two seals on a hunt about five miles from the hut, but young ice carried one away. The group ate the other’s skin, hair and all. The account records the Inuit hunters’ search for seals near open water when the ice allowed it.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

## The Tigress finds them

During an attempt to reach land by boat, Tyson wrote that a craft designed for six or eight men carried twelve men, two women and five children, along with a tent and their necessary skins. They made about twenty miles west before nearly swamping and turning back.[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

The rescue came on 30 April 1873, after more than six months on the ice. The Navy’s account of the inquiry gives the dates as 15 October to 30 April and places the pickup off Labrador; Nature’s contemporary summary reports the same rescue date.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[4]](https://doi.org/10.1038/008217b0)

A steamer appeared through fog. The people on the floe raised signals and fired their weapons to attract attention. Hans launched his kayak toward the vessel. It was the Tigress, a sealer from Newfoundland, commanded by Captain Bartlett. Her crew brought all nineteen aboard and carried them to St. John’s.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[3]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexperience00blakrich)

The Tigress then changed roles. The U.S. Navy chartered the ship for a second expedition, this time to look for the Polaris and the people who had stayed with it. In August, White’s search party reached the camp on Littleton Island where the ship party had spent the previous winter. They found that the Polaris crew had already left in boats. The fourteen had been picked up by the Ravenscraig before the Tigress began her search.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)[[5]](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/t/tigress-iii.html)

That distinction is easy to lose in a short retelling: the Tigress rescued the nineteen from the floe in April, then searched for the other party later in the year. It found the camp, not the missing crew. By October, the ship’s crew had reached Scotland, and the Tigress learned they were safe.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)[[5]](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/t/tigress-iii.html)

## The pages cut from the journals

At Littleton Island, White’s party examined what remained in the camp. White reported that the log-book and journals had many pages cut out. He said that his party found no writing about Hall’s death or the separation, and that pages covering those dates had been carefully removed.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)

White did not identify who removed them. He wrote that whether the destruction was caused by white crew members or Inuit was an open question. The missing pages are evidence of an incomplete record, not evidence of what they contained or why they were removed.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)

Hall’s death has its own evidentiary trail. The Navy’s inquiry concluded that he died of apoplexy. In 1968, Chauncey C. Loomis led an expedition to exhume Hall’s body in Greenland. Loomis later recounted the expedition and Hall’s life in *Weird and Tragic Shores*.[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)[[9]](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/103519/weird-and-tragic-shores-by-chauncey-loomis/)

A 1970 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine reported markedly elevated arsenic in the parts of Hall’s hair and fingernails that had grown during the final two weeks of his life. The finding supports arsenic exposure. It does not establish who administered arsenic, how it entered Hall’s body, or whether the exposure was deliberate.[[8]](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197004022821406)

Hall’s death came before the floe drift; White found the cut pages later, but did not say what they had contained. The Navy’s report dates the separation and rescue, while White’s 1,800-mile figure remains a contemporary estimate.[[1]](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1874/december/cruise-tigress)[[2]](https://archive.org/details/narrnorthpolar00davirich)
