In the Rare Book Department of the Free Library of Philadelphia, a raven stands inside a glass case. The bird is called Grip, and once lived with Charles Dickens. After its death, Dickens had it preserved; after his own death, it passed through the hands of collectors and eventually joined the library's collections. It is an unusual object to find among books, though the company suits it.[[1]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/digital/item/cdc102140)[[3]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/blog/post/3554)

Grip has been connected with two fictional birds: the one Dickens put into *Barnaby Rudge*, and the one that answers a grieving man in Edgar Allan Poe's poem *The Raven*. The first connection is Dickens's own account. The second is a persuasive literary possibility, supported by what Poe wrote about Dickens's novel, but harder to prove as a direct line from a particular pet to a poem.

## A raven in the family

Dickens had more than one raven. In a later preface to *Barnaby Rudge*, he said the bird in the novel was a composite of two he had owned at different times. His description of the first suggests a pet with a lively interest in other people's property. It slept in the stable, sometimes on horseback, and, Dickens claimed, could take a Newfoundland dog's dinner from under its nose.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)

In the same preface, Dickens remembered the raven eating white lead left by painters and said this led to its death. But a letter written when the bird died gives a more cautious picture. In the letter, reproduced in his daughter Mamie's recollections, he said the raven had swallowed white paint the previous summer; the family wondered whether some was still affecting it. The letter records a suspicion, not a modern toxicology finding. A definite diagnosis of lead poisoning goes further than that contemporary account can establish.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)[[11]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27234/27234-h/27234-h.htm)

The letter is also a distinctly Dickensian report on a sick pet. A medical gentleman gave the raven castor oil; Dickens counted the subsequent biting of the coachman as a sign of improvement. The next morning, the bird ate some warm gruel. Around noon it walked about the coach house, barked, staggered and, Dickens wrote, cried "Halloa, old girl!" before dying. He added that the children seemed rather glad: the raven had bitten their ankles, although, in his view, only in play.[[11]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27234/27234-h/27234-h.htm)

The library's account says a visitor who looked after the ravens at the Tower of London identified Grip as female. It does not explain the basis for that identification. Dickens called the bird "he"; his pronoun, like the later identification, is not by itself a biological test. The bird's sex is therefore best left uncertain rather than presented as a discovery beyond doubt.[[3]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/blog/post/3554)[[11]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/27234/27234-h/27234-h.htm)

Dickens acquired another raven after the first died. In his later account, the newcomer dug up the cheese and halfpennies its predecessor had buried in the garden. This second bird had its own accomplishments, including the ability to imitate the language of the stable. Dickens described meeting it in the street with a crowd in attendance; it had to be brought home after taking refuge behind a pump.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)

The distinction matters because the real Grip was not simply copied, unchanged, onto the page. Dickens's preface gives him two birds' habits to draw upon. It is also a reminder that the stories he told about them were written by a man who knew how to make an audience laugh.

## Grip meets Barnaby

*Barnaby Rudge* appeared in serial form in 1841. It begins years before the Gordon Riots, the anti-Catholic disturbances that shook London in 1780, and brings its characters into the violence. Barnaby travels with a raven named Grip. Dickens presents Barnaby through a nineteenth-century idea of intellectual disability; the language used for him in the novel and in Poe's review is language we would not use as a diagnosis today.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)[[4]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/sep41dc1.htm)

The fictional Grip speaks in fragments rather than making sensible speeches. The bird's phrases and movements can interrupt a conversation, alarm a listener or supply an odd kind of company. Barnaby regards Grip as a companion with an active life of its own, and talks about the bird stealing and hiding things. Those habits are not difficult to match to Dickens's account of his pets, though matching an anecdote to a fictional scene is not the same as knowing exactly when Dickens invented it.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)

The raven gives the novel room for comedy, but the larger story contains murder, suspicion and mob violence. Its plot asks readers to notice incomplete clues and wait for an explanation. For a critic interested in how stories are constructed, that made it an inviting book to take apart.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)[[5]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/gm42dc01.htm)

Poe did just that. In May 1841, while the novel was still coming out, he reviewed the early instalments for the *Saturday Evening Post*. He tried to predict how its murder mystery would develop, and he thought the raven's croakings would be heard "frequently, appropriately, and prophetically" as the story went on. He compared the relationship between the bird and Barnaby to a musical accompaniment and its melody.[[4]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/sep41dc1.htm)

That was a prediction, not a report on the completed novel. Poe was imagining what Dickens might do with the material as much as describing what had already appeared. His early review shows him paying close attention to a talking raven before he wrote his own famous poem, but it does not say that the real Grip had inspired him.

In February 1842, writing in *Graham's Magazine* about the finished book, Poe returned to the idea. He called the raven "intensely amusing" but thought Dickens could have made it more integral to Barnaby's character. The croakings, he wrote, might have been heard prophetically through the drama. He repeated the musical comparison, this time in the language of an opportunity Dickens had missed.[[5]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/gm42dc01.htm)

Poe's complaint is more specific than saying he wanted a larger bird part. He was interested in a relationship: two distinct characters whose peculiarities could sound against each other, each affecting the way a reader understood the other. The raven in Dickens is often funny; Poe saw the possibility of making its voice contribute more strongly to the shape and atmosphere of the story.

## Another bird, another room

Dickens and Poe met in Philadelphia in 1842, during Dickens's American tour. Surviving letters show that they were in contact and that Dickens tried, without success, to interest English publishers in Poe's work. The Poe Museum notes that there is no evidence Dickens told Poe about the death of his pet during their meeting. They do not need an invented conversation to connect them: Poe had already read and reviewed the novel.[[6]](https://poemuseum.org/charles-dickens-meets-edgar-allan-poe)

In 1845, Poe's *The Raven* appeared in print. Its narrator, mourning a woman named Lenore, hears a tapping and opens his window. A raven enters and perches on a bust above the door. Asked its name, it answers "Nevermore." The narrator at first finds the solemn visitor almost comical, then keeps asking questions to which the bird gives the same reply.[[7]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/poems/ravenb.htm)

The word does not change, but the questions do. A noise that might be merely the bird's learned vocabulary comes to sound like a verdict. The narrator asks whether he will find relief from grief and whether he will see Lenore again. The answer keeps arriving, conveniently and terribly, in a single word.[[7]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/poems/ravenb.htm)

In an 1846 essay, "The Philosophy of Composition," Poe gave his own account of constructing the poem. He said he wanted a repeated word that could acquire different force as its context changed. He considered a parrot, he wrote, before choosing a raven as a speaking bird better suited to the melancholy tone. The essay begins by mentioning Dickens and *Barnaby Rudge*, but Poe does not credit Grip there as the source of the poem's bird.[[8]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/essays/philcomp.htm)

That essay is a writer's retrospective explanation of his method, not a recording of every thought that occurred while he worked. Still, its silence matters. The evidence shows that Poe knew Dickens's fictional raven and had already suggested making its voice prophetic. The Free Library calls an influence on the poem probable. It does not establish that Poe met the live bird, heard it speak, or consciously based his raven on that particular animal.[[3]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/blog/post/3554)[[4]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/sep41dc1.htm)[[5]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/gm42dc01.htm)[[8]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/essays/philcomp.htm)

The birds also do different jobs. Barnaby's Grip has a repertoire and a talent for disrupting a household. Poe's bird has one repeated word, and the speaker supplies much of its terror himself. In Poe's later explanation, the grieving man knows the answer is learned by rote, yet continues to ask questions that will make the answer hurt.[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)[[8]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/essays/philcomp.htm)

## The bird in the case

After Dickens died in 1870, the preserved raven was sold with his effects. The Free Library's collection record traces it through several owners before the collector Richard Gimbel acquired it and brought it into his Poe collection. The record says Gimbel donated Grip to the library in 1970; the library's blog gives 1971 for the bequest, so the precise transfer date depends on which record is used.[[1]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/digital/item/cdc102140)[[3]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/blog/post/3554)

There is another trace of Poe in the same department: the library also holds a manuscript of *The Raven* in his hand. A mounted bird and a poem in manuscript are very different kinds of evidence. One preserves an animal Dickens knew; the other preserves words Poe wrote. The reviews and the novel sit between them, showing where the connection is strong and where it remains an interpretation.[[9]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/digital/item/48335)[[4]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/sep41dc1.htm)[[5]](https://www.eapoe.org/works/criticsm/gm42dc01.htm)

The Rare Book Department's visitor information includes Grip on its tour at Parkway Central Library. The bird can no longer steal a dog's dinner or announce itself from a stable, but it still has a place among the books that made its name familiar.[[10]](https://libwww.freelibrary.org/programs/rarebooks/visit.cfm)[[2]](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/917/917-h/917-h.htm)
