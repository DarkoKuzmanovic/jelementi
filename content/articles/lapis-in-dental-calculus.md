She was between forty-five and sixty when she died, and nothing about her skeleton stands out. No injuries, no disease worth noting, no sign of hard labor. She was buried in an unmarked grave beside a church in Dalheim, in what is now western Germany, sometime around the year 1100. Her teeth were in poor shape: two molars lost before death, gums mildly diseased, and a heavy crust of tartar on her front teeth.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

That crust is the reason we know about her. In 2014, researchers studying plant remains in medieval dental calculus looked at her tartar under a microscope and saw blue. More than a hundred particles of a deep, vivid blue, some still embedded in fragments of the crust itself.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

Blue is a difficult color in nature. Few minerals provide it, and medieval Europe knew only a handful of blue pigments: azurite, Egyptian blue, smalt, vivianite, and one stranger among them, ultramarine.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) The name means "from beyond the sea," and it was literal. Ultramarine is ground from lapis lazuli, a rock colored blue by the mineral lazurite, whose principal historical source is the remote Sar-e-Sang district of Badakhshan, in what is now Afghanistan.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/lapis-lazuli)[[4]](https://www.gia.edu/lazurite-afghanistan-reading-list) From there it traveled thousands of miles along Eurasian trade routes, a luxury good among luxury goods, used ornamentally, religiously and medicinally since ancient times.[[4]](https://www.gia.edu/lazurite-afghanistan-reading-list) By the time it reached a European workshop it was, the researchers write, as expensive as gold, reserved along with gold and silver for the most luxurious books, and entrusted only to scribes and painters of exceptional skill.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

## How tartar becomes an archive

Teeth are not an obvious place to look for art history. Dental calculus is mineralized plaque, and as it hardens year after year it seals in whatever passes through the mouth: food, pollen, fibers, dust. It is an abundant and long-lasting reservoir of a person's life, preserving microbes and diet along with debris from craft work, including plant fibers that speak of textiles, trade and daily labor.[[2]](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.2013.0376)

That is what makes it an archive rather than a stain. The blue in this woman's tartar was not one smear from one accident. The particles lay singly, like a dispersed powder, scattered across fragments of crust that had formed on different teeth at different times. Whatever brought the pigment into her mouth happened again and again, over years, as the tartar slowly calcified around each intake.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

The identification took three kinds of looking. Under the microscope the particles averaged just under eleven micrometers across, the same size as reference pigment ground from Afghan lapis.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) Elemental analysis then ruled out every metal-based blue: the particles contained no copper, cobalt or iron as major elements, which excluded azurite, smalt and vivianite alike, and their spectrum matched lazurite instead.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) Finally a laser probe read the mineral's fingerprint directly. The Raman spectrum showed lazurite's characteristic bands, and beside the blue crystals sat a colorless mineral, iron-rich phlogopite, which accompanies lazurite in natural lapis stone. Together the two minerals gave a positive identification: lapis lazuli.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

There was one laboratory mishap on the way. The standard procedure, dissolving the tartar in dilute acid, bleached the blue away, so the team abandoned acid and broke the crust apart with ultrasound in pure water instead. The color survived.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

## Four ways blue gets into a mouth

The woman, catalogued as individual B78, was radiocarbon dated to between 997 and 1162, and her sex was confirmed by both bone structure and DNA.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) She belonged to a small religious community: a stone church dedicated to Saint Peter, first built in the ninth century, with a house of Augustinian canonesses attached, first documented in the thirteenth century, home to about fourteen women until fire destroyed it after a series of fourteenth-century battles. Westphalian archaeologists dug it in the late 1980s. Almost no books or records from the place survive.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

So how did the most expensive pigment in Europe end up in the teeth of an unremarkable woman in an unremarkable community? The authors weighed four explanations.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

The most plausible is that she painted books. Medieval illuminators are known to have licked their brushes to draw them to a fine point, a practice later artists' manuals describe outright, and each touch of the tongue could carry pigment into the mouth. That habit fits the evidence exactly: repeated small doses, spread over time, across many teeth.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) There is even a precedent from her own region and century. Between 1140 and 1168, Sindold, the keeper of books at the men's monastery of Reinhardsbrunn, wrote to the women's house at Lippoldsberg, only seventy kilometers east of Dalheim, commissioning a deluxe illuminated prayer book from a sister identified only as N, and shipped her the parchment for nearly four hundred pages plus leather, pigment and silk. A men's monastery outsourcing its finest book to a women's house says something about who had the reputation for this work.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

The second possibility is that she ground and purified the pigment rather than painting with it. Grinding lapis throws dust, and a fifteenth-century handbook warns the reader to cover the mortar so the powder does not go off in the air. The team confirmed by experiment that grinding leaves particles on the lips and in saliva.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) But there is a wrinkle: the oil-flotation method that makes ultramarine brilliantly blue is not documented in European manuals before the fifteenth century, which raises the question whether eleventh-century scribes made their own pigment at all or received it finished, imported through trading cities such as Alexandria by Italian merchants. If a woman at Dalheim prepared pigment, it was probably for her own hand or another sister's.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

The third route is medicine. Lapis had a long career as a drug, from an ancient Greek text prescribing it for scorpion bites and eye complaints to a Cairo apothecary's stock and many Islamic recipe books. It enters European medical writing only in the eleventh century, in works by Marbod of Rennes, Hildegard of Bingen and the Circa Instans.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) There is little sign anyone in eleventh-century Germany actually swallowed it, and a swallowed dose would more likely arrive as one clump than as years of scattered powder.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

The fourth idea, kissing painted holy figures in prayer books, fails on dates. That kind of emotive devotion belongs to the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, some three hundred years after this woman died, and kisses would have lifted clumps of paint with their binding medium, which is not what the tartar holds.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

Painting it is, then, with pigment preparation kept on the table. The paper's wording is careful, and this article keeps the care: the blue suggests a painter; it does not prove one.

## The women the records forgot

The reason one mouthful of pigment matters is that the women who made medieval books are mostly invisible. Before the fifteenth century scribes seldom signed their work. Fewer than fifteen percent of books in women's monastery libraries carry a female name, and before the twelfth century fewer than one percent can be attributed to women. It was long assumed monks had produced nearly everything.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

That assumption has been coming apart. In Germany and Austria, religious women worked as scribes and illuminators from as early as the late eighth century. The nuns of the double monastery at Admont copied many of the more than two hundred books surviving from its twelfth-century collection. Diemut of Wessobrunn in Bavaria is credited with more than forty books, including a richly illuminated gospel. For the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries, where records improve, scholars have identified over four thousand books by more than four hundred women, with active scriptoria at forty-eight women's houses. The eleventh century, where our woman falls, is the dark stretch: few surviving books, thin records, unsigned work.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126)

Alison Beach's study of Bavarian houses showed the scriptorium standing at the center of the medieval intellectual revival, with women working in it, alone, with other women, and alongside men.[[5]](https://www.cambridge.org/us/universitypress/subjects/literature/anglo-saxon-and-medieval-literature/women-scribes-book-production-and-monastic-reform-twelfth-century-bavaria) Cynthia Cyrus found manuscript production by women monastics across more than four hundred and fifty convents in late medieval Germany, enough to change how the whole manuscript trade is understood.[[6]](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/106071/) The big names survive too: Hildegard of Bingen, the twelfth-century abbess who wrote theology, music and medicine, including a sacred musical drama for her own convent around 1150; the workshop of Herrad of Landsberg, whose painted saints' lives of around 1175 to 1200 may be its only surviving work; and the plain fact the British Library states about nunneries, that they gave religious women education, creativity and community.[[7]](https://www.bl.uk/about/press/releases/british-library-to-open-exhibition-about-medieval-women)

The German manuscript record even offers a direct parallel. The earliest tested German book containing lapis lazuli that may be tied to a woman scribe is a copy of Hildegard's Liber Scivias from around 1200, though its paintings were colored by at least two anonymous hands.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) Our woman predates it by a century or more.

Her community left almost nothing. No books survive from Dalheim, from its shelves or from anywhere else. The monastery burned with everything in it, which is why the researchers note that her teeth may hold the only surviving evidence that anyone there ever made a book.[[1]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau7126) Today the site is a museum of monastic culture, run by the regional heritage association, on eight centuries of history in Lichtenau-Dalheim.[[8]](https://www.stiftung-kloster-dalheim.lwl.org/de/)

There is a temptation to give her a finished biography: the nun-artist of Dalheim, brush in hand. Resist it. What the evidence supports is smaller and stranger: a woman whose mouth, over years, gathered the dust of the costliest blue on earth, grain by grain, until her tartar kept what her monastery's library could not. One individual, one interpretation, honestly held. The rest of the women's work from those centuries is still out there, unsigned, waiting for someone to think of looking in unlikely archives.
