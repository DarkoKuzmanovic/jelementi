For most of its course the Danube takes its time. At Đerdap it does not. Here it squeezes between mountains on the border of Serbia and Romania, through a chain of gorges that English-speaking visitors know as the Iron Gates. The Serbian side of that passage is Đerdap National Park (*Nacionalni park Đerdap*), the largest national park in Serbia. It runs for about a hundred kilometres along the right bank of the Danube, from Golubac to Karataš near Kladovo, and it has been protected since 1974.[[1]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/about-the-park/)

For a guide, Đerdap is generous. It has a river landscape that silences a bus, a fortress straight out of a storybook, one of Europe's most important prehistoric sites, Roman engineering, and a twentieth-century dam that changed all of them. The trick is to tell it as one story: people and a river, from the Stone Age to the age of hydropower.

## A gorge in four acts

The first thing to explain is that the [Iron Gates](#glossary-iron-gates) are not one canyon. The Đerdap gorge is a chain of four narrower [gorges](#glossary-gorge) and three wider [basins](#glossary-basin) in between.[[2]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/geolosko_nasledje/djerdap-gorge-iron-gate/) The river tightens, opens out, tightens again, and a group travelling downstream can feel the rhythm.

Below Golubac the Danube enters the first narrows, the Golubac Gorge, and then opens into the Ljupkovo basin.[[3]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/geoheritage/) Next comes Gospođin vir, where steep banks hold the riverbed tight. At Greben, a spot named after a medieval monastery, the gorge ends again and the river spreads out into the Donji Milanovac basin, more than two kilometres wide.[[3]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/geoheritage/)

Then comes the climax: the [Kazan](#glossary-kazan) gorge, in two parts called Veliki Kazan and Mali Kazan, the Big and Little Cauldron. Here the Danube narrows to a few hundred metres, and limestone cliffs rise straight up out of the water.[[3]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/geoheritage/) The geopark's own description calls the Lower Gorge the place where the Danube is "the narrowest and the deepest in its flow".[[4]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/kulturno_nasledje/trajans-plaque/)

![The Danube flowing between steep, wooded cliffs in soft evening light, the gorge narrowing into the distance.](articles/national-parks-3-djerdap/photo-1-v1.jpg "The Danube between the wooded cliffs of the Iron Gates. Photo: Geologicharka, CC BY-SA 4.0")

The best place to show all this is from above. Above Mali Kazan lies Ploče, a level limestone plateau with viewpoints over the river.[[5]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/pesacke_staze/hiking-trails-and-lookouts-of-the-djerdap-national-park/) Higher still, at 626 metres above Veliki Kazan, is Mali Štrbac, which the geopark counts among the most beautiful viewpoints in the park. From there the view takes in the most dramatic part of the gorge and the Romanian bank opposite.[[5]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/pesacke_staze/hiking-trails-and-lookouts-of-the-djerdap-national-park/) Check the trails and the group's fitness first. The walks are real walks.

:::fact{title="A face on the other bank"}
From Mali Štrbac you can see the giant face of Decebalus, the last king of the Dacians, carved into the Romanian cliff. It looks ancient but is modern: it has been visible only since 2004.[[5]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/pesacke_staze/hiking-trails-and-lookouts-of-the-djerdap-national-park/)[[6]](https://thersites-journal.de/index.php/thr/article/view/101)
:::

That face deserves a sentence of its own, because someone always asks. Decebalus was the Dacian king who fought Trajan's Rome.[[6]](https://thersites-journal.de/index.php/thr/article/view/101) Across the river from it, on the Serbian side, is a genuinely ancient monument from the same war: Trajan's Plaque.[[6]](https://thersites-journal.de/index.php/thr/article/view/101) One river, two sides of the same conflict, carved nineteen centuries apart.

## Golubac: the gate to the gate

Golubac Fortress (*Golubački grad*) stands where the gorge begins, and its position explains its whole history. Whoever held it held the entrance to the Iron Gates, so Hungary and Serbia, and later the Ottoman Empire, fought over it again and again in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.[[7]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/history/)

The fortress first appears in a written source in 1335, already holding a Hungarian garrison. It is older than that, but nobody knows who built it or when.[[7]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/history/) Point the group to the way it climbs the rock: a palace and nine towers joined by walls, laid out to fit the terrain.[[8]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/fortress/) Then point to the newest addition. When gunpowder changed warfare, the Ottomans built a Cannon Tower to defend the harbour and strengthened the outer towers.[[8]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/fortress/) A medieval castle that had to learn to face artillery is a nice small lesson in how history happens.

![A tall stone tower and battlemented walls climbing a rocky slope against a blue sky, with a wooden walkway below.](articles/national-parks-3-djerdap/photo-2-v1.jpg "Golubac Fortress at the entrance to the gorge. Photo: BuhaM, CC BY-SA 4.0")

The name is where guides like to linger, and where they should be careful. The best-known legend tells of a beautiful local girl called Golubana who refused to join an Ottoman commander's harem. She was chained to a rock in the river as punishment and died there, and the fortress was named in her memory.[[7]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/history/) It is a good story, and the fortress's own history presents it as what it is: a medieval legend.[[7]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/history/) Tell it as a legend.

The fortress went through a long restoration that its operator calls the start of its "new life". Today it is visited with guided tours and exhibitions.[[9]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/eng/visits/) It was open to visitors by 2019.[[10]](https://tvrdjavagolubackigrad.rs/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Program-izmena-Programa-poslovanja-2019.pdf)

## People on the river, from Lepenski Vir to Rome

Đerdap's cultural story begins long before any fortress. On the Danube's bank near Donji Milanovac lies [Lepenski Vir](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/lepenski-vir), where archaeologists found settlements from the [Mesolithic](#glossary-mesolithic) and early Neolithic, with trapezoidal houses and monumental stone sculptures.[[11]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en) For a guide, the essentials are simple: Stone Age people built houses and carved monumental sculptures here, and the whole site was moved uphill to save it from the reservoir. The relocation was carried out in 1970 and 1971, and today the relocated settlement is the heart of the museum, with copies of the sculptures placed where the originals were found.[[11]](https://www.narodnimuzej.rs/about-museum/locations-of-the-national-museum/muzej-lepenski-vir/?lang=en) Jelementi's article on Lepenski Vir has the full story.

The Romans came for the river. Their road through the gorge was cut into the cliffs, and the most famous trace of it is Trajan's Plaque (*Trajanova tabla*, Latin *Tabula Traiana*), an inscription dedicated to the emperor Trajan.[[12]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/cultural-heritage/) The park reads it as marking the completion, in the year 100, of the last and most difficult section of the road.[[12]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/cultural-heritage/)

Two details make the plaque a favourite on boat trips. First, the big letters across the top, TABULA TRAIANA, are not Roman at all. They were added in 1891, when the Serbian government took the first steps to protect it.[[4]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/kulturno_nasledje/trajans-plaque/) Second, the plaque is not where the Romans put it. In 1969, before the reservoir rose, the whole block of rock with the inscription was cut out and lifted 20 to 25 metres higher.[[4]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/kulturno_nasledje/trajans-plaque/)

![A carved stone tablet set into a pale cliff above the water, with the words TABULA TRAIANA across the top.](articles/national-parks-3-djerdap/photo-3-v1.jpg "Trajan's Plaque, lifted above the reservoir in 1969. Photo: Sanja026, CC BY-SA 4.0")

Further downstream, at Kostol, the Romans did something bolder still. [Trajan's Bridge](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/trajans-bridge), designed by Apollodorus of Damascus, crossed the Danube between the First and Second Dacian Wars. A fort called Pontes guarded its Serbian end and Drobeta the Romanian one.[[13]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/) These forts were part of the [limes](#glossary-limes), Rome's fortified Danube frontier. At Karataš the auxiliary fort of Diana kept watch over the dangerous rapids and over the canal the Romans dug to get ships past them.[[14]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/diana/) Excavations there uncovered the ramparts with their gates and towers, and barracks inside.[[12]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/cultural-heritage/)

## The dam that moved a landscape

Everything above was shaped by one decision. In 1964 Yugoslavia and Romania began building the Đerdap I hydropower and navigation system together, and it was completed in 1972.[[15]](https://www.eps.rs/eng/Poslovanje-EE/Pages/Hidroelektrane.aspx)[[16]](https://www.eps.rs/lat/djerdap/Stranice/heps_djerdap1.aspx)

The dam gave both countries electricity and tamed the river for shipping. It also raised the water through the gorge, and a guide should give both sides of that story. On the Serbian bank alone, about 8,500 people and six settlements were moved to make room for the [reservoir](#glossary-reservoir), and Trajan's Plaque and Lepenski Vir had to be moved with them.[[16]](https://www.eps.rs/lat/djerdap/Stranice/heps_djerdap1.aspx) Downstream lay the island of Ada Kaleh, which once sat on the river frontier between the Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian empires and changed names and rulers many times. It was home to its islanders until the reservoir covered it.[[17]](https://ojs.ugal.ro/index.php/cultural-intertexts/en/article/view/8386)

![A long dam and power station spanning a wide, calm Danube, seen from a wooded hillside.](articles/national-parks-3-djerdap/photo-4-v1.jpg "The Đerdap I dam and power station. Photo: Stadtigel, CC BY-SA 4.0")

Nature paid too. The dams of Đerdap I and II blocked the fish that used to swim up the Danube from the Black Sea to spawn.[[18]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/fauna/) The park also records three plant species that disappeared locally when their habitats were flooded.[[19]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/flora/)

:::fact{title="An island under the reservoir"}
Ada Kaleh, an inhabited Danube island that once sat on the Ottoman frontier, was submerged in 1970 to make way for the Iron Gates power plant.[[17]](https://ojs.ugal.ro/index.php/cultural-intertexts/en/article/view/8386)
:::

## A refuge for old forests

The gorge also kept something safe. During the Ice Age, Đerdap's sheltered valleys became a [refugium](#glossary-refugium), and forest plants that vanished elsewhere survived here.[[19]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/flora/) UNESCO points to Turkish hazel, a [relict](#glossary-relict) of the Tertiary period, as one of the trees that still grows in the park.[[20]](https://www.unesco.org/en/iggp/djerdap-unesco-global-geopark)

The plant that surprises visitors most is common lilac, which the park calls an ornament of the entire gorge.[[19]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/flora/) If the group visits in spring, that is worth stopping for.

Among the animals the park lists are deer, lynx, wolf and jackal, golden eagles and eagle owls, white-tailed eagles and black storks.[[1]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/about-the-park/)[[18]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/fauna/) They are wild animals, not an attraction on a timetable, so promise the landscape, never a sighting.

:::fact{title="Garden lilac, gone wild"}
The lilac in your grandmother's garden grows wild at Đerdap, in dense shrubs on steep limestone slopes where trees cannot grow.[[19]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/flora/)
:::

## Three titles, one river

Đerdap carries more labels than any other Serbian park, and a guide should keep them apart. It is a national park, protected since 1974.[[1]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/about-the-park/) In 2020 it also became a UNESCO Global [Geopark](#glossary-geopark). The geopark includes the national park and parts of the Miroč and Kučaj mountains, so it is larger than the park.[[20]](https://www.unesco.org/en/iggp/djerdap-unesco-global-geopark) UNESCO's description also mentions a living tradition, [bućka](#glossary-bucka), the old way of catching catfish practised at Tekija, and the visitor centre in Donji Milanovac with exhibits on the area's geology, nature and history.[[20]](https://www.unesco.org/en/iggp/djerdap-unesco-global-geopark)

That is a good place to finish a tour. Đerdap is not a museum of nature with the people taken out. It is a river that people have fished, fortified, crossed, dammed and argued over for nine thousand years.

## Dates to remember

- 100: the park dates the completion of the Roman road section marked by Trajan's Plaque to this year
- 1335: Golubac Fortress first appears in a written source
- 1891: the words TABULA TRAIANA are added above the Roman inscription
- 1964: Yugoslavia and Romania begin building Đerdap I
- 1969: Trajan's Plaque is lifted above the future reservoir
- 1970–1971: Lepenski Vir is moved to higher ground; Ada Kaleh is submerged in 1970
- 1972: Đerdap I is completed
- 1974: Đerdap becomes a national park
- 2020: UNESCO names Đerdap a Global Geopark
