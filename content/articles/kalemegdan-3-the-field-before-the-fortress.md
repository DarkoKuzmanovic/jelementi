Most visitors walk into Belgrade Fortress from the park, through the Outer Stambol Gate (*Spoljna Stambol kapija*). The gate is hewn stone under a segmental arch, a shallow curve, and its only decoration is three small rosettes. In the words of the fortress's own description, the arch has clear features of Turkish architecture.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%99%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en) Behind the gate runs a hidden path along a wall with loopholes, once the fortress's first line of defence. Behind that lies the main moat, 20 to 25 metres wide and about five metres deep, which now holds the tennis and basketball courts of the Partizan and Crvena zvezda sports clubs.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%99%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en)

Further in, the Inner Stambol Gate (*Unutrašnja Stambol kapija*) and the Clock Tower (*Sahat kula*) both date from the eighteenth century.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en)[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%85%D0%B0%D1%82-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0/?lang=en) Between 1688 and 1791 the fortress changed hands six times.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) This part covers that century, and the century and a half of Ottoman calm before it.

The park in front of the walls gave the whole place its popular name. The Turks called the open field before the fortress Kalemegdan, from *kale*, fortress, and *megdan*, field, and they called the hill itself *Fićir-bajir*, the hill of thinking.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/)

## A city far from the front

[Part 2](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-2-the-despots-capital) ended on 29 August 1521, with Belgrade in Ottoman hands.[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%9A%D0%B8-%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4/?lang=en) For the next century and a half it was no longer a border town. The Ottoman Empire had pushed its frontier far to the north, and the city served mainly as a large military supply base, as winter quarters for campaigns in Europe, as the seat of the provincial governor, the *sancak bey*, and as a great treasury.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

Most of the new building went into a cannon foundry, a powder mill, storehouses for food, grain and military equipment, and a mint. New fortifications were not a priority: the old walls were used and repaired as needed.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf) In the Lower Town, the Church of the Assumption became the mosque of Sultan Suleiman, and around it grew *mahalas*, or quarters, with Suleiman's caravanserai, a hammam, barracks and barns.[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%9A%D0%B8-%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4/?lang=en)

The one large repair before the late seventeenth century followed an accident in 1564. Lightning struck a tower of the castle, which the Turks called *Narin*, the inner fort, and set off the gunpowder kept inside.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf) The blast destroyed part of the underground treasury and many buildings in the Upper and Lower Town, and even damaged the Danube port. Rebuilding began in 1565 and took almost ten years. Even so, the gunpowder stayed in the castle towers.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

## A vizier's fountain

Of the many Ottoman buildings in the fortress, only two have survived: a fountain from the sixteenth century and a tomb from the eighteenth.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The fountain of Sokollu Mehmed Pasha (*Česma Mehmed-paše Sokolovića*) stands in the Upper Town by the Defterdar Gate (*Defterdarova kapija*).[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf) Built in 1576–1577, it is the only endowment in Belgrade of this famous vizier.[[8]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BC%D0%B0-%D0%BC%D0%B5%D1%85%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B4-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B5-%D1%81%D0%BE%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%9B%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The castle still stood then, and the fountain sat in its moat, under the bridge that led to the castle's main gate. It was also where the water line from the springs around Belgrade ended.[[8]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BC%D0%B0-%D0%BC%D0%B5%D1%85%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B4-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B5-%D1%81%D0%BE%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%9B%D0%B0/?lang=en)

Sokollu Mehmed Pasha was born a Serb, in the village of Sokolovići in Herzegovina, and was educated at the Mileševa monastery. Taken into the janissaries at fifteen, he won the trust of Suleiman the Magnificent and became grand vizier in 1565. He also ordered the bridge over the Drina at Višegrad, the bridge of Ivo Andrić's novel *The Bridge on the Drina*.[[8]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BC%D0%B0-%D0%BC%D0%B5%D1%85%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B4-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B5-%D1%81%D0%BE%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%9B%D0%B0/?lang=en) The fountain lost its original look at the end of the seventeenth century, when the Defterdar Gate was built and the remains of the castle were cleared away. It was restored in 2017.[[8]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%87%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BC%D0%B0-%D0%BC%D0%B5%D1%85%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B4-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B5-%D1%81%D0%BE%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%9B%D0%B0/?lang=en)

## 1688: the Austrians

The calm ended after the Ottomans failed to take Vienna in 1683. They lost southern Hungary, and the Habsburg armies advanced quickly towards Belgrade.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf) The siege of 1688 was led by Maximilian Emanuel of Bavaria, and the medieval walls could not stand up to his artillery for long. After less than a month of bombardment, Habsburg troops broke in through the ruined ramparts of the Upper Town, and on 6 September 1688 Belgrade was theirs.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

Austria at once began a modern fortress, designed by the engineer Andrea Cornaro, whom the fortress's history calls Andrej Kornar.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en) This was the start of the bastion fortress. A bastion is a low, five-sided fortification that projects from the main wall, laid out so that neighbouring bastions protect each other. The straight stretch of wall between two bastions is the curtain wall.[[10]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/bastion)[[11]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/curtain-wall) A ravelin is a fortification built in front of the curtain wall and surrounded by ditches; it often guarded a gate.[[12]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/demi-lune-or-ravelin)

Leopold's Gate (*Leopoldova kapija*) stands in the oldest part of these bastion works. It was begun right after the conquest and finished a few years later, in works led by Cornaro, but its baroque façade dates from the 1720s. The letters L. P. over the arch stand for *Leopoldus Primus*, Emperor Leopold I, under whom Austria first held Belgrade.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en) The King's Gate (*Kralj kapija*), in the south-western rampart of the Upper Town, began as a passage cut after the 1688 siege.[[13]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%99-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en)

## 1690: the explosion

Nobody in the Habsburg command expected the Ottomans to win Serbia back so quickly. Even after Smederevo fell on 27 September 1690, Belgrade's commander, General Aspremont, wrote to his superiors that he was not convinced the enemy would come.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

The Ottoman siege began on 2 October and lasted a week. By 7 October the defenders had been pushed back behind the walls.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf) On the afternoon of 8 October, at about four o'clock, an Ottoman grenade fell on one of the castle towers and set off the gunpowder stored there. The castle wall and the towers facing the Sava came down with their guns and their crews, and more than a thousand Habsburg soldiers died.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

General Aspremont later reported that rubble blocked his door, so he jumped out of the window. He escaped with nothing but the clothes he wore, leaving even his wedding ring behind.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf) By that evening there were no Christians left in the fortress. Ottoman chroniclers wrote that between five and eight thousand people were killed, and as many again died in the explosion or drowned in the river trying to escape.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

The explosion completely destroyed the castle of Despot Stefan Lazarević, the subject of part two.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) It had stood between today's Victor monument (*Pobednik*) and the fountain at the Defterdar Gate, and only a small scale model now shows what it looked like.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

The work went on under new owners. According to the fortress's official history, Cornaro went over to the Ottomans, and they continued rebuilding the fortifications to his design.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The Defterdar Gate was built in the last decade of the seventeenth century, as a vaulted passage through the north-western rampart of the Upper Town with a baroque outer portal designed by Cornaro. Its name comes from *defterdar*, a Turkish word for a treasurer or finance officer.[[14]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%84%D1%82%D0%B5%D1%80%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en) Most of the King's Gate was built between 1693 and 1696.[[13]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%99-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en)

## Prince Eugene

In 1716 an Ottoman army marched north against the Habsburgs and was beaten by Prince Eugene of Savoy at Petrovaradin.[[15]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/battle-belgrade) The grand vizier, Damat Ali Pasha, was killed in that battle and buried in the Upper Town, near the mosque of Sultan Suleiman.[[16]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B4-%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE-%D1%82%D1%83%D1%80%D0%B1%D0%B5/?lang=en) Eugene then took the fortress of Temesvar and, the next summer, marched on Belgrade, which was held by 30,000 Ottoman troops. His men built pontoon bridges over the Danube to keep their lines to the north open.[[15]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/battle-belgrade)

Before they could take the city, a second Ottoman army arrived under the Grand Vizier Halil Pasha. It was twice the size of Eugene's force of 70,000, which was weakened by dysentery.[[15]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/battle-belgrade) Eugene attacked anyway. On 16 August 1717, surprise, Ottoman overconfidence and a disciplined bayonet charge broke the janissaries' lines, and Belgrade fell shortly afterwards.[[15]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/battle-belgrade)

Europe greeted the conquest of Belgrade and northern Serbia as a great victory of Christianity over Islam. Under the peace signed at Požarevac (Passarowitz) the following year, Austria kept its conquests. Belgrade became its main border fortress and a centre for spreading Catholicism to the east.[[17]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BB%D0%B0-vi/?lang=en)

## The baroque fortress

Austria now built a new fortress, with ramparts, bastions and earthen embankments, under Colonel Nicolas Doxat de Démoret.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) Doxat was a Swiss aristocrat, a soldier and engineer in Habsburg service, and the reconstruction of Belgrade, from 1723 to 1736, was the crown of his career.[[18]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332289) The fortifications of the south-eastern front were built to his design.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en) The official history says that by the end the fortress was one of the strongest in Europe.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The new main entrance to the Lower Town was the Gate of Charles VI (*Kapija Karla VI*), built between 1718 and 1720 and named after the emperor. Its façades are the only original baroque architecture preserved south of the Sava and the Danube. The outer side was a triumphal entrance with the emperor's monogram. The inner side carried the arms of Serbia as an Austrian crown land: a boar's head pierced by an arrow.[[17]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BB%D0%B0-vi/?lang=en)

Gunpowder went into the rock. The Great Gunpowder Magazine (*Veliki barutni magacin*) was dug into the Danube slope in only two years, from 1718 to 1720, as a store safe from enemy artillery. Today it holds Roman stone monuments, and sometimes concerts and plays.[[19]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D1%83%D1%82%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD/?lang=en)

The Roman Well (*Rimski bunar*) is not Roman. A document in the War Archives in Vienna records that it was finished in 1731, and the people of Belgrade gave it its ancient-sounding name in the nineteenth century. It is 3.4 metres wide and 35 metres deep, with two rows of spiral steps around its inner wall. Digging at the bottom on the eve of the Second World War showed that no tunnel connected it with the Sava, as people had believed. A tunnel had been started, but it stops after about two metres of rock.[[20]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B8%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D1%83%D0%BD%D0%B0%D1%80/?lang=en)

In the Lower Town the Austrians built an arsenal, artillery barracks, gunpowder stores and food stores with bakeries. The Ottomans used all of them for the same purposes until 1867.[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%9A%D0%B8-%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4/?lang=en)

Doxat did not see the fortress handed back. In the next Austro-Turkish war, which began in 1737, he surrendered the fortress of Niš without resistance. He was tried for it and executed in 1738.[[18]](https://www.ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1332289)

## 1739: the walls come down

That war went badly for Austria. In September 1739 it made a separate peace and gave northern Serbia, with Belgrade, back to the Ottomans.[[21]](https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Belgrade-1739) The Ottomans took the city without a fight, and one term of the treaty obliged Austria to demolish all its new fortifications.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The gate of the south-eastern front was to be completely destroyed by June 1740.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en)

Next to the Clock Tower gate, the Austrian Baroque Gate (*Barokna kapija*) was walled up and stripped of its façade. It was excavated in 1987, and since 1989 it has housed an exhibition on the fortress's history. Its scale models show the fortress in the fifteenth century and as a baroque fortress in 1736 and 1790.[[22]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%BA%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The Ottomans built on top of the demolished Austrian works. The Inner Stambol Gate stands where the Austrian gate stood, and it was the main gate towards the road to Constantinople.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en) The Austrian gate before it had passages, side rooms and casemates: vaulted chambers built to withstand artillery.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en)[[23]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/casemate)

The Clock Tower, over today's main entrance to the Upper Town, was built in the middle of the eighteenth century. Clock towers were familiar in Turkish bazaars but rare in fortresses, and this one took its style from the baroque, then the leading movement in European architecture. It is 27.5 metres tall. Plans from the 1790s describe "a clock tower with a guard under it" and "a tall tower with a dome covered with tin in the German manner". Its clock runs for about a week between windings, and the mechanism was renewed in 2002.[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%85%D0%B0%D1%82-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The tower's official page also passes on a claim by Evliya Çelebi, the seventeenth-century Ottoman travel writer, that its bell could be heard a day's journey away across the Sava.[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%85%D0%B0%D1%82-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0/?lang=en) Evliya wrote about a century before this tower was built, so whatever bell he heard, it was not this one. The Upper Town did have an older clock. In 1690 Abdullah Üsküdarî, an Ottoman official who kept a diary of the campaign, walked through the captured town and described a tower in the Upper Town with a large clock that chimed every hour, day and night.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250826055906/http://rih.iib.ac.rs/1020/1/Walking%20Through%20The%20Ravaged%20City.pdf)

The other Ottoman survivor is the six-sided türbe, or mausoleum, in the middle of the Upper Town. It was built in 1784 over the grave of Izzet Mehmed Pasha, the commander of Belgrade, who died that year. Its present name came later. After damage in the First Serbian Uprising, it was rebuilt in 1818–1819 and dedicated to Damat Ali Pasha, the grand vizier killed near Petrovaradin, whose own tomb the Austrians had destroyed along with the mosque.[[16]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B4-%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE-%D1%82%D1%83%D1%80%D0%B1%D0%B5/?lang=en)

## One more Austrian visit

Austria tried once more. Between 15 September and 8 October 1789, a Habsburg army under Field Marshal Ernst Gideon Laudon besieged the fortress and took it.[[24]](https://mmc.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/entities/event/siege-belgrade/)[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The Austrians held it until the Treaty of Sistova, concluded in August 1791, returned it to the Ottomans.[[24]](https://mmc.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/entities/event/siege-belgrade/)[[25]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Treaty-of-Sistova) Under that peace, the Janissaries were forbidden to enter the Belgrade Pashaluk, the province governed from the city.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[25]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Treaty-of-Sistova)

The ban did not hold. At the beginning of the nineteenth century, after the commander of Belgrade, Hadji Mustafa Pasha, was assassinated, the Janissaries took over the city and the surrounding villages.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) Part four picks up the story there.

## Dates to remember

- 1576–1577: the fountain of Sokollu Mehmed Pasha is built
- 6 September 1688: the Austrians take Belgrade
- 8 October 1690: an explosion destroys Despot Stefan's castle, and the Ottomans retake Belgrade
- 16 August 1717: Prince Eugene of Savoy defeats the Ottoman army at Belgrade
- 1718: the Treaty of Požarevac (Passarowitz); Belgrade stays Austrian
- 1723–1736: Nicolas Doxat de Démoret rebuilds the fortress
- 1731: the Roman Well is finished
- September 1739: the Treaty of Belgrade; the Ottomans take the city back without a fight
- 1789: Laudon takes Belgrade for Austria
- 1791: the Treaty of Sistova returns Belgrade to the Ottomans
