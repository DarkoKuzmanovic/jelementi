In 1846, staff at the British Museum glued a snail's shell to a small mounting card and added it to the collection. They did not realize there was a living animal inside.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)

It was one of two specimens brought from Egypt, both fixed in place among the museum's other shells.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) Almost four years passed without anyone discovering the surviving snail or supplying it with food or water.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)[[11]](https://archive.org/details/guidetomolluscae00brituoft)

Then, in March 1850, the naturalist William Baird noticed a thin, glassy film across the opening of one shell. It looked freshly formed.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) Something about this supposedly dead specimen had changed.

Baird detached both shells and placed them in tepid water.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) After about ten minutes, one snail began to emerge. A few minutes later, it was crawling along the basin.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)

He moved it into a tumbler, where it began climbing the side. The following day, he offered it a small piece of cabbage leaf, which it ate.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)

After four years as a museum specimen, it still had an appetite.

## An animal equipped to wait

The other snail did not revive. Baird found the animal inside its shell dead.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) The survivor had been mistaken for a dead specimen too, but had remained alive all along.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) Its endurance becomes less mysterious once we leave the museum and consider where it came from.

This was an Egyptian desert snail, a species now called *Eremina desertorum*.[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich)[[12]](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?p=taxdetails&id=1422400) In the nineteenth-century naturalist Samuel Pickworth Woodward's account, it appears as *Helix desertorum*.[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich) London was an accident in this animal's life. Dry conditions were not.

A snail seems an improbable desert resident. We tend to picture one crossing a wet path or disappearing beneath a leaf, rather than living somewhere rain may be absent for long stretches. Yet a range of land-snail species inhabit arid and seasonally dry environments, with adaptations that help them endure both heat and water shortage.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Our damp-garden picture captures only part of what a snail can be.

Moving about in those conditions can be costly. Prolonged activity in the heat increases water loss and the risk of drying out.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Searching for food is of limited use if the search dries you out before you find it. For a snail adapted to seasonal drought, withdrawing into its shell and reducing its needs can be a better way to survive.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674)

This prolonged reduction in activity and metabolism is called *aestivation*.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) It is often described as summer sleep, but the body has more to do than stop moving. The animal must conserve water and lower its energy use enough to survive a long interruption in feeding.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) A shell provides shelter; it does not supply a meal.

The season in which a snail becomes dormant depends on the conditions it needs to avoid. *Theba pisana*, another land-snail species, can aestivate through a Mediterranean summer, then become active in the cooler, wetter months.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) In Britain, the same species instead hibernates through winter and is active during the warmer part of the year.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) A summer that is tolerable in one place can be the season to wait out in another.

The Egyptian snail's survival makes sense against this background. It had arrived in London with ways of enduring dry conditions that nobody looking at the mounted shell had thought to allow for.

## Behind the glassy film

Withdrawing into a shell still leaves one problem: the opening. The thin covering Baird noticed across it is called an *epiphragm*.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) In aestivating land snails, this covering slows water loss while remaining permeable to air.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) It has to do both, because the animal inside still needs to exchange gases even while its metabolism is reduced.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) An airtight cork would solve one problem by creating another.

These coverings can contain calcium carbonate, and their composition and thickness vary.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Thicker, more solid epiphragms can improve water retention and help support longer periods of dormancy.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) The small film was therefore worth Baird's attention. It suggested that the shell still held an animal capable of making something, however little that animal otherwise seemed to be doing.

Inside the shell, a dormant snail also slows its metabolism, including the making and breaking down of proteins.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Protective mechanisms help keep the components of its cells stable during the wait.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Remaining motionless would not be enough on its own: the animal has to reduce its internal demands while keeping the body in a condition from which it can recover.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674)

Those savings are substantial, but they do not make survival free. Studies of dormant desert snails have found that they consume their own body material and lose mass over time.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Even an animal that never leaves its shell is using something up. Lower energy expenditure stretches what it has, while slower water loss helps keep it from drying out; how long this can work varies with the species and its conditions.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674)

The outside of the shell gives little indication of any of this. A snail can remain in the same place while its resources gradually diminish.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) A still shell is easy to mistake for an empty one, particularly when it is already mounted among specimens assumed to be dead. In the museum, it took a change in that small covering to prompt a closer look.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)

Coming out again has its own risks. Research on other land snails shows that as metabolism rises, increased oxygen consumption can lead to the production of reactive molecules that damage proteins and fats within cells.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) This is called *oxidative stress*.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) Recovering from dormancy involves coping with that change as well as becoming active again.

In *Otala lactea*, for example, antioxidant defenses are already activated during dormancy, before the rapid rise in oxygen use on waking.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) The protection is in place ahead of the disturbance. Baird saw his snail emerge and start crawling within minutes.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) His report describes what he watched in the basin, rather than the changes inside its cells. Studies of other species help explain the difficulties such an animal must manage, without telling us exactly what happened inside this particular shell.

The same limit applies to the familiar phrase "four years without food or water." The museum's later account says neither was supplied during its time in the case.[[11]](https://archive.org/details/guidetomolluscae00brituoft) That does not mean the animal had no water left in its body, or had ceased to use energy. Conserving both is central to aestivation.[[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6875674) We do not have measurements of this snail's losses, or a temperature and humidity record for the case. The historical account documents its endurance; modern physiology explains how snails can make their resources last.

## Four years is not necessarily one long sleep

The paper beneath the shell offered another clue. Woodward described discoloration there, which he took as evidence that the snail had emerged, tried unsuccessfully to get away, then withdrawn and closed its shell again.[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich) If his interpretation was right, Baird's bath was not its first moment of activity since arriving at the museum.

That possibility changes the picture of those four years. The shell was fixed to its mounting card, but the living animal inside might still have tried to move.[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich) An attempted escape could have gone unnoticed, leaving only a mark for someone to puzzle over later. Woodward was interpreting that mark, though, not reporting an escape he had watched.[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich) We cannot say when it happened or how long the snail remained outside its shell.

Baird's fresh-looking epiphragm also suggested recent activity.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) Neither observation tells us how often the animal might have stirred. What the records establish is that it survived the years between mounting and discovery, not that it passed them in one uninterrupted sleep.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich)

Even the day of the recovery is uncertain: Woodward gives 7 March, while Baird places his observation at about 15 March.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich) Both put it in March 1850. The mounting date and the animal's emergence in water are much firmer parts of the story than a reconstruction of everything that happened between them.

Nor can one survivor tell us how long its species would usually endure such conditions. Baird did not know how long the shells had been in the collector's possession before they reached the museum.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) The two animals' earlier histories and condition remain largely unknown. One was alive when he put the shells in water; the other was not.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)

After the recovery, the record becomes more intimate. Baird was no longer examining what he believed to be an unoccupied shell. He was looking after an animal, and began recording its meals and movements.

## Cabbage, climbing and unfinished repairs

By June 1850, the snail was living in a glass jar about eighteen inches high.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) It liked climbing near the top rather than remaining at the bottom.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) The jar offered a considerable improvement in freedom of movement over a mounting tablet, if not a return to Egypt.

Cabbage had become more than a first meal. Baird tried lettuce and other foods, but the snail preferred cabbage leaf and continued to feed readily.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) The animal that had survived for years without deliberate care now had someone trying to find out what it liked for dinner.

It also had unfinished work on its shell. Before collection, the shell had been damaged and partly repaired.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) Baird noticed that the snail had resumed adding to the opening after its revival.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) That small addition connected its life before the museum with its life in the jar. It had survived long enough to continue work interrupted years earlier.

A second living snail joined it: a dark variety of *Helix hortensis*, which Baird introduced as a companion.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) He reported that the two seemed to live "quite harmoniously together."[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)

The snail even acquired a portrait. A sketch accompanied Baird's report and appeared in Woodward's manual.[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond)[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich) Woodward described the animal as "alive and flourishing" and said it had "sat for his portrait."[[10]](https://archive.org/details/manualofmollusca01woodrich)

In March 1851, according to the later account in *Excelsior*, the snail withdrew into its shell again.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami) This time it remained there for eight months.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami)

On 9 November it emerged once more.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami) It ate cabbage and moved around its glass jar, returning to the small routines recorded after the first revival.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami)[[9]](https://archive.org/details/annalsmagazineof26lond) The account describes it as "quite lively."[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami) On 15 November it withdrew into its shell again.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami) It was never seen to emerge after that.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami)

It was found dead in 1852.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami)[[11]](https://archive.org/details/guidetomolluscae00brituoft) The accounts differ over the final dormancy and the month of that discovery, but neither says why it died.[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami)[[11]](https://archive.org/details/guidetomolluscae00brituoft)

The writer in *Excelsior* recorded the loss this way: "Such was the end of the Egyptian snail, and it was with some feeling of regret that its death was recorded."[[8]](https://archive.org/details/excelsiorhelpsto12hami)
