In the 1920s a professor in Belgrade sat down to compute the weather of the deep past. Not next week's rain, but the sunlight that fell on northern summers hundreds of thousands of years ago, and the ice sheets it built or melted. His model covered six hundred thousand years before 1800, latitude by latitude, worked out longhand from the mechanics of the Earth's orbit. When deep-sea sediment cores finally tested his numbers in 1976, they matched. He had been dead for eighteen years.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/)

## An engineer who wanted a field of his own

Milutin Milanković was born in 1879 in Dalj, then in Austria-Hungary, into a well-to-do Serbian family. At seventeen he went to Vienna to study engineering, earned his doctorate in 1904 with theoretical work on concrete, and spent a few successful years designing structures across the empire, patenting new construction methods along the way. Then, in 1909, he gave up the business and accepted the chair of applied mathematics at the University of Belgrade. He would stay there for forty-six years, until retirement, a civil engineer retrained by his own decision into a mathematician of the sky.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)

He arrived with a plan. Teaching was agreeable, but he wanted research ground he could call his own, somewhere his mathematics would be the tool that mattered. He chose meteorology, then a mostly observational science, and set himself what sounds like an absurd goal: to calculate temperatures at every point on Earth, in every season, from first principles. He put it in farming terms: he was looking for an arable field he could cultivate with his mathematical tools, and meteorology was it.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch) As early as 1912 his attention had turned to solar climates and the temperatures prevailing on the planets, the subject that would fill his war years.[[3]](https://www.egu.eu/awards-medals/portrait/milutin-milankovic/)

## A prisoner with a library card

He had barely settled in when history started interrupting. In June 1914, newly married, he was on honeymoon near Dalj when the First World War broke out. As a Serbian citizen on Austro-Hungarian soil he was rounded up, held in a camp at Osijek, moved to Karlovac, and from October 1914 confined at Nezsider with other Serbian civilians.[[3]](https://www.egu.eu/awards-medals/portrait/milutin-milankovic/)[[4]](https://web.archive.org/web/20190415114244/http://arhiva.unilib.rs/unilib/eng/about_us/exhibitions/milankovic_virtual/1914.php)

What happened next is the strangest research grant ever awarded. Through his wife's appeal to his old Vienna mentor, Emanuel Czuber, Milanković was allowed to spend the rest of his captivity in Budapest. He kept his civil rights, including the right to work. His only restrictions were that he could not leave the town and had to report to a police office once a week. The head of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences library, himself a mathematician, let him use the collection even after closing time. Milanković spent the war years studying everything written about the Earth's insolation and the distribution of sunlight across its surface, and wrote several papers plus a monograph on the problem. A friend, Ivan Djaja, translated the monograph, and it appeared in France in 1920. Back in Belgrade, Milanković was elected a full professor.[[4]](https://web.archive.org/web/20190415114244/http://arhiva.unilib.rs/unilib/eng/about_us/exhibitions/milankovic_virtual/1914.php)

## Three slow wobbles

The idea he carried out of that library is now taught to undergraduates, but it helps to take it slowly, because nothing about it is fast. The Earth does three things at once, each on its own schedule, and each changes how much sunlight each latitude gets in each season.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/)

First, the shape of the orbit breathes. Right now the Earth's path around the Sun is nearly circular: the closest approach, around the third of January, is only about three percent nearer than the farthest point, around the fourth of July, a difference of five million kilometres, worth roughly six percent more incoming sunlight in January than in July. But over a cycle of about ninety to a hundred thousand years the orbit stretches and relaxes, and when it is at its most elliptical, the sunny-side advantage at closest approach grows to twenty or thirty percent. There is also a much longer eccentricity beat of about four hundred thousand years.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/)[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)

Second, the axis nods. The tilt of the Earth's axis drifts between 22.1 and 24.5 degrees and back every forty-one thousand years. It stands at 23.5 today. More tilt means fiercer summers and deeper winters; less tilt means milder seasons both ways.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/)[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)

Third, the axis wobbles like a slowing top, tracing a circle in the sky over about twenty-six thousand years. This precession decides which hemisphere leans toward the Sun at closest approach, so it strengthens the seasons in one hemisphere while softening them in the other. Right now the northern summer happens near the farthest point of the orbit.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/)[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)

None of these changes the total sunlight much. Their trick is redistribution: moving warmth between latitudes and seasons until snow that falls in winter survives the summer. At the suggestion of the climatologist Vladimir Köppen, Milanković picked one number to watch above all: the summer sunlight at sixty-five degrees north, the latitude of the great ice sheets, where a cool summer means last winter's snow never melts and the ice starts marching south.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/)

## The curves find readers

His first full statement of the theory, the Mathematical Theory of Thermal Phenomena Caused by Solar Radiation, came out in 1920 and got meteorologists' attention, because its results roughly matched observed temperatures.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch) Between the calculations, he kept a second career as an explainer: through the 1920s he wrote a monthly letter in a Serbian magazine to a young imaginary friend, describing mental journeys into the past to visit famous scientists and talk through their ideas, especially in astronomy. The letters were collected in 1928 in book form as Through Distant Worlds and Times.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)

Working with Köppen and the geophysicist Alfred Wegener, Milanković pushed his longhand calculations hundreds of thousands of years into the past and drew the radiation curves for latitudes fifty-five, sixty and sixty-five north. The curves appeared in Köppen and Wegener's 1924 volume on the climates of the geological past. In 1927 he was invited to contribute to two major German handbooks, one on climatology and one on geophysics; his climatology introduction grew into the 1930 volume that carried the theory further, with a second set of curves for lower latitudes. Both were eventually folded into his masterwork.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)[[3]](https://www.egu.eu/awards-medals/portrait/milutin-milankovic/)[[5]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Die_Klimate_der_geologischen_Vorzeit.html?id=TT64AAAAIAAJ&hl=en)

That masterwork, the Canon of Insolation and Its Application to the Ice-Age Problem, was written in about two years starting in early 1939. The printing was finished on the second of April 1941. Four days later, on the sixth of April, Belgrade was bombed, and the print shop producing the Canon was destroyed in the devastation, so the final pages had to be printed again. The book came out in German from the Serbian Royal Academy, six hundred and twenty-six pages.[[6]](https://web.archive.org/web/20141027005441/http://legati.matf.bg.ac.rs/milankovic/paper.wafl?paper=kanon_osuncavanja_zemlje) Even then, support arrived from an unexpected quarter: in early 1944 a German meteorological journal carried a long review by Karl Wundt with colleagues, arguing the theory had been perfected past its early difficulties, and Milanković noted with satisfaction that one of its defenders was a geographer, since geographers had been his theory's most frequent opponents.[[3]](https://www.egu.eu/awards-medals/portrait/milutin-milankovic/)[[6]](https://web.archive.org/web/20141027005441/http://legati.matf.bg.ac.rs/milankovic/paper.wafl?paper=kanon_osuncavanja_zemlje) Part of that defense was an amplifier: once snow survives the summer, its bright surface reflects sunlight away, which cools the ground further, which saves more snow. A small cut in summer sunlight could compound, through the whitening ground, into an ice sheet.

He never fully left his first profession either. Between 1920 and 1941 he designed civilian and military buildings across Yugoslavia: airports, fortifications, bank buildings, the Mint, even churches. Late in life he headed the Belgrade Astronomical Observatory from 1951 and was elected to the Academy of Natural Sciences at Halle in 1955.[[7]](https://www.mi.sanu.ac.rs/History/milankovic.htm)

The same mathematics wandered further. In 1913 he calculated the mean temperature of Mars at minus seventeen degrees Celsius, a number later direct measurements confirmed. He drew up a reconciled Julian-Gregorian calendar, counted the most accurate ever proposed, which was never adopted for want of church agreement.[[7]](https://www.mi.sanu.ac.rs/History/milankovic.htm)

## The long dismissal

It did not carry the field. Through the 1950s his work was challenged and fell out of favour. Most scientists thought the temperature changes he predicted were too small to march glaciers back and forth across continents. Worse, several European deposits whose ages matched his predicted cold periods turned out not to be glacial deposits at all, which discredited the main evidence cited for him. The objections came mostly from meteorologists, who held that orbital nudges to sunlight were simply too feeble to matter.[[2]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Milutin-Milankovitch)[[3]](https://www.egu.eu/awards-medals/portrait/milutin-milankovic/)

Note what this means for the "ignored for fifty years" version of the story. Milanković was not unread: his curves went into a major 1924 book, his 1930 volume, the 1941 Canon, and a serious 1944 review. What he never got was assent. The field knew his answer and voted no.

## The cores agree

The reversal came from the bottom of the sea. In 1976 three researchers, James Hays, John Imbrie and Nicholas Shackleton, published a study of deep-sea sediment cores in the journal Science that tracked global climate across the past four hundred and fifty thousand years. The variance in the climate record clustered into three peaks, at twenty-three thousand, forty-two thousand and about a hundred thousand years, carrying roughly ten, twenty-five and fifty percent of the variance. The forty-two-thousand-year beat kept a constant phase relationship with the tilt of the axis; the twenty-three-thousand-year portion matched the precession index, with a nineteen-thousand-year overtone; the dominant hundred-thousand-year component marched in step with eccentricity. Their conclusion was blunt: changes in the Earth's orbital geometry are the fundamental cause of the succession of the Quaternary ice ages. They added two honest caveats: the hundred-thousand-year link to eccentricity probably needs some nonlinearity in the climate system to work, and their model of the future, ignoring human influence, pointed toward extensive northern glaciation in the coming millennia.[[8]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.194.4270.1121)

After that, the institutions moved. The United States National Research Council embraced the orbital-cycle model.[[1]](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/milutin-milankovitch/) The story of the vindication got its popular history in John and Katherine Palmer Imbrie's Ice Ages: Solving the Mystery.[[9]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ice_Ages.html?id=GIxRp9fRDGwC) In 1982 Columbia University hosted a symposium with the weight of a verdict, "Milanković and Climate". Craters on the Moon and on Mars now carry his name, along with a minor planet, and since 1993 the European Geophysical Society has awarded a Milanković medal for work on long-term climate.[[7]](https://www.mi.sanu.ac.rs/History/milankovic.htm)

## The house on Ljubomira Stojanovića

Milanković did not live to see any of it. He died in Belgrade on the twelfth of December 1958, vice-president of the Serbian Academy, having been its corresponding member since 1920 and full member since 1924.[[10]](https://www.sanu.ac.rs/en/member/milankovic-milutin/)

What Belgrade kept is easier to visit than an orbital element. In 1927 he built himself a house in the Profesorska kolonija, the professors' colony: a modest town villa to the colony's standard plan, signed by the architects Jovanović, Krstić and Radovanović, with plain facades and a bay window dominating the upper floor, at number nine Ljubomira Stojanovića street. The republic's heritage register lists it as a cultural monument, significant above all because a man who timed the ice ages lived and worked inside it.[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20241219223914/https://nasledje.gov.rs/index.cfm/spomenici/pregled_spomenika?spomenik_id=102129)

There is something fitting about the address. The theory said the largest events in climate come not from drama but from small, patient geometry: a stretch of the orbit here, a nod of the axis there, compounding over tens of thousands of years until continents of ice answer. Milanković worked the same way. He did the arithmetic by hand, waited out a war in a library, waited out the field's rejection, and let the numbers keep their appointment with the seafloor, eighteen years after he was gone.
