Strictly speaking, Kalemegdan is not the fortress. The Ottomans gave the name to the open field in front of the walls, from *kale*, fortress, and *megdan*, field.[[1]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/) That field is now the park. The interactive map of the public company that runs the site, JP Beogradska tvrđava, sorts the sights into three areas: Kalemegdan Park, the Upper Town (*Gornji grad*) on the ridge, and the Lower Town (*Donji grad*) by the rivers.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%84%D0%BE/?lang=en)[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%9A%D0%B8-%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4/?lang=en) This walk takes them in that order.

## Kalemegdan Park

Start at the entrance to the park, by the Belgrade City Library. From the beginning of the second century Singidunum was the base of the Fourth Flavian Legion, in a camp that covered about 20 hectares.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0/?lang=en) The camp's main entrance, the *Porta Decumana*, lay on the line of today's Knez Mihailova Street, and part of it lies under the library. The foundations of that gate, with two inner and two outer square towers, can be seen in the Roman Hall of the City Library.[[5]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B3-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%BC%D0%B0/?lang=en) [Part 1](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-1-the-white-ridge) tells the Roman story.

Inside the park stands the Monument of Gratitude to France (*Spomenik zahvalnosti Francuskoj*), by the sculptor Ivan Meštrović. It was unveiled in October 1930, with King Alexander I present, on the spot where a monument to Karađorđe had stood until Austrian shells destroyed it in 1915. The inscription on the back of the pedestal reads: "We love France as it loved us in 1914–1918".[[6]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BA-%D0%B7%D0%B0%D1%85%D0%B2%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%BD%D0%BE%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B8-%D1%84%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%BD%D1%86%D1%83%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%BE%D1%98/?lang=en) [Part 5](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-5-from-battlefield-to-park) has the full story.

The park has two named staircases. The Small Staircase (*Malo stepenište*) was built in 1903 to designs by Jelisaveta Načić, the first woman architect in Serbia.[[4]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B5-%D1%82%D0%B2%D1%80%D1%92%D0%B0%D0%B2%D0%B5/?lang=en) The fortress company describes it as the first piece of park architecture in Belgrade, and a decorative fountain near it dates from the same period. The Grand Staircase (*Veliko stepenište*), designed by Aleksandar Krstić in a Romanesque spirit, was finished in 1928 and completed the works on the Sava promenade.[[7]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%BE-%D0%B8-%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%BE-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D0%BF%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%88%D1%82%D0%B5/)

## Through the Stambol gates

The main way into the fortress runs through the Outer Stambol Gate (*Spoljna Stambol kapija*). A wooden bridge once stood in front of it; an earthen embankment replaced it at the end of the nineteenth century. The gate keeps its original shape and has lost only its wooden doors. It is plain hewn stone under a segmental arch with clear Turkish features, and its only decoration is three small rosettes. Inside are rooms for the guards. Beyond it runs a covered way, a path behind a wall with loopholes, which was the fortress's first line of defence. Behind that lies the main moat, 20 to 25 metres wide and about 5 metres deep. Today it holds the tennis and basketball courts of the Partizan and Crvena zvezda sports clubs.[[8]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%99%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The Inner Stambol Gate (*Unutrašnja Stambol kapija*) replaced an Austrian one. Under Austrian rule, from 1717 to 1736, the south-eastern front of the fortress was rebuilt to the design of Colonel Nicolas Doxat de Démoret, with a baroque gate on this spot. The Treaty of Belgrade required that gate to be destroyed, and from 1739 the Ottomans built the present one on its site. In Ottoman times it was the main gate towards the road to Constantinople, which the name Stambol recalls. After the keys were handed over in 1867, the Serbian flag flew here beside the Turkish one, and in 1876 the pole with the Turkish flag was taken down.[[9]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en) Today the gate holds a gallery, an old-crafts workshop and a souvenir shop.[[2]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%84%D0%BE/?lang=en) [Part 3](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-3-the-field-before-the-fortress) and [Part 4](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-4-the-keys-of-the-city) cover both stories.

The Military Museum (*Vojni muzej*), founded in 1878, stands on Bastion 1 of the same south-eastern front. Its building went up between 1924 and 1928 for the Military Geographical Institute, to a design by the Russian émigré architect Vasiliev, and the museum has been there since 1959.[[10]](https://www.muzej.mod.gov.rs/eng/230/o-vojnom-muzeju-230)

## The Upper Town

The main entrance to the Upper Town today is the gate under the Clock Tower (*Sahat kula*). Clock towers were a familiar sight in Turkish bazaars but rare in fortresses, so this one is unusual. It was built in the middle of the eighteenth century, with baroque features, and it is 27.5 metres high. The clock runs for about a week between windings, and its mechanism was renewed in 2002.[[11]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%85%D0%B0%D1%82-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0/?lang=en)

The fortress's own account of the tower adds a note by Evliya Çelebi, the seventeenth-century Ottoman travel writer: the tower's bell could be heard a day's journey away across the Sava. The dates do not fit. Çelebi wrote a century before this tower was built, so the bell he described cannot have hung in it.[[11]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%B0%D1%85%D0%B0%D1%82-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0/?lang=en)

In the centre of the Upper Town stands the six-sided türbe, or mausoleum, of Damat Ali Pasha (*Damad Ali-pašino turbe*), one of the few Ottoman buildings left in Belgrade. It was not built for him. It went up in 1784 over the grave of Izzet Mehmed Pasha, a commander of Belgrade who died that year. After damage in the First Serbian Uprising, it was rebuilt in 1818–1819 by Marashli Ali Pasha, the governor of Belgrade, who dedicated it to Damat Ali Pasha, the grand vizier killed near Petrovaradin in 1716. Two later commanders of Belgrade, Selim Pasha and Hasan Pasha, were buried in it in 1847 and 1850.[[12]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B4-%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D0%BF%D0%B0%D1%88%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE-%D1%82%D1%83%D1%80%D0%B1%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The Roman Well (*Rimski bunar*) is misnamed too. A document in the War Archives in Vienna records that it was finished in 1731, and Belgraders gave it its ancient-sounding name in the nineteenth century. When it was dug, it stood in a moat in front of the ruined medieval castle. It is 3.4 metres wide and 35 metres deep, with two rows of spiral steps around its inner wall, and a vaulted corridor leads from it to the King's Gate. Water fills only the bottom 21 metres, and the lowest 4 metres of that are rubble that filters it. The story of a tunnel to the Sava did not survive a dig at the bottom on the eve of the Second World War, which found only the start of a corridor, about two metres long and never finished.[[13]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B8%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BC%D1%81%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D1%83%D0%BD%D0%B0%D1%80/?lang=en)

The corridor ends at the King's Gate (*Kralj kapija*), in the south-western rampart. A first passage was cut here after the Austrian siege of 1688 and destroyed less than two years later, when the Ottomans retook the town. Most of the present gate was built between 1693 and 1696, and it got its final shape and baroque façade under Austrian rule, between 1717 and 1736. The wooden doors lined with iron plates at the inner gate date from the middle of the eighteenth century, and the wooden bridge in front of the gate was replaced by the present one in 1928.[[14]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%99-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/?lang=en)

Little is left above ground of the medieval castle on the ridge. During the Ottoman siege at the end of the seventeenth century, a gunpowder explosion almost completely destroyed its walls and towers, and clearing the ruins left a mostly flat space. A scale model by the sculptor Kolja Milunović, placed roughly in the centre of where the castle stood, shows what was there. Its core was a castellum built in the twelfth century on the orders of the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Komnenos, about 135 metres long and 60 metres wide; part of its rampart, with two towers, can still be seen on the edge of the slope. In the first decade of the fifteenth century Despot Stefan Lazarević turned it into a castle with his court. Its keep, a great tower with a drawbridge and the court's last line of defence, was called Nebojša, "fearless". When it was destroyed, the name passed to a much smaller tower on the Danube bank, the last stop of this walk.[[15]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BA%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0-%D0%B7%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%BA%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B7%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2/?lang=en) [Part 2](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-2-the-despots-capital) tells the Despot's story.

On the highest spot of the fortress, on the ridge above the confluence of the Sava and the Danube, stands the Victor (*Pobednik*), Ivan Meštrović's bronze, unveiled in 1928. Figure and Doric column together are 14 metres high, and he faces the Sava.[[16]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BA-%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BA/?lang=en) Part 5 tells how a statue meant for a fountain in the city centre ended up here.

The Despot himself stands in the Upper Town in bronze: a 3.2-metre figure in a cloak by the Belgrade sculptor Nebojša Mitrić, unveiled in 1982. Under Despot Stefan, Belgrade became the capital of Serbia for the first time, but only for 23 years, until his death in 1427.[[17]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%BA-%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D1%83-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%BD%D1%83-%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B7%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%9B%D1%83/?lang=en)

## The eastern gates

On the eastern side of the Upper Town, the Despot's Gate (*Despotova kapija*) was the fortress's main entrance in the Middle Ages. Together with the north-eastern rampart, it is the best-preserved part of the medieval Upper Town, from the first half of the fifteenth century, and the only gate kept in its original state. It had two gates, one in each of two ramparts. Above the inner gate was a defensive balcony, a machicolation, with a niche below it for an icon of the town's protectress. Beside it stands the square Dizdar Tower (*Dizdareva kula*), home of the fortress commander in the eighteenth century. Badly damaged in 1915 and partly rebuilt in 1938, it now houses the observatory of the Ruđer Bošković Astronomical Society.[[18]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%81%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%BC-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%BC/?lang=en)

The Zindan Gate (*Zindan kapija*) was added in the middle of the fifteenth century to protect the East Gate, then the main entrance to the town. It is a barbican, a small outwork in front of a gate: two massive semicircular towers with a passage between them. A moat with a wooden drawbridge lay in front of it, and its towers and the curved wall beside them had seven openings for cannon. According to the fortress's page, this is where Belgrade was first defended with artillery, and it was from these positions that the town held out against the Ottoman siege of 1456. The name came later: *zindan* is Turkish for a dungeon or prison, and in the eighteenth century the towers probably served as one.[[19]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%BF%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BA%D1%81-%D0%B7%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%BD-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B5/?lang=en)

The Leopold Gate (*Leopoldova kapija*) stands in the oldest part of Belgrade's bastioned fortifications. Work on it began right after the Austrian conquest of 1688 and was finished a few years later. Its monumental baroque façade is younger, from the 1720s. The initials over the arch, L. P., stand for *Leopoldus Primus*, the Emperor Leopold I, in whose reign Belgrade first came into Austrian hands.[[20]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BE%D0%BF%D0%BE%D0%BB%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/?lang=en)

## The Lower Town

Below the ramparts lies the Lower Town. Despot Stefan laid it out as a fortified settlement at the beginning of the fifteenth century, with four gates. Inside stood the cathedral, the Church of the Assumption of the Mother of God, with the residence of the Metropolitan of Belgrade. It was the most densely settled part of the town, with the houses of merchants and craftsmen from Serbia, from towns in Hungary and from the coast, above all from Dubrovnik.[[3]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%9A%D0%B8-%D0%B3%D1%80%D0%B0%D0%B4/?lang=en)

Two churches stand there today. Ružica Church (*Crkva Ružica*) was made in 1867 by adapting an Austrian gunpowder store from the 1730s as a military church. Its chandeliers are made of trophy weapons from the First World War.[[21]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%86%D1%80%D0%BA%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D1%80%D1%83%D0%B6%D0%B8%D1%86%D0%B0/?lang=en) St Petka's Chapel (*Kapela Svete Petke*), built in 1937 to a design by the architect Momir Korunović, stands over a spring that has been thought miraculous since the Middle Ages.[[22]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%86%D1%80%D0%BA%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D1%81%D0%B2%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B5-%D0%BF%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%BA%D0%B5/?lang=en) Part 5 covers both.

In the western part of the Lower Town, below the Victor, the Big Gunpowder Magazine (*Veliki barutni magacin*) is dug into the rock of the slope.[[23]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%84%D0%BE/)[[24]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D1%83%D1%82%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD/?lang=en) The Austrians built it in only two years, from 1718 to 1720, as a shelter for gunpowder that enemy artillery could not reach. Today its first hall holds Roman stone monuments, stelae, sarcophagi and altars brought from sites around Belgrade, Kosmaj and Kostolac, and concerts and plays are held there from time to time.[[24]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D1%83%D1%82%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD/?lang=en)

The walk ends on the Danube bank at the Nebojša Tower (*Kula Nebojša*), the largest and best-preserved tower of the fortress. It was built around 1460 to guard the entrance to the Lower Town's port. It is a cannon tower on an eight-sided base, originally about 22 metres high, and in 1521 its fall marked the collapse of Belgrade's defence. In the spring of 1798 the Greek patriot Rigas Feraios was imprisoned and killed here. Since its renovation in 2010 the tower has held an exhibition about its own history, Rigas and the Serbian uprisings.[[25]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%BE%D1%98%D1%88%D0%B0/?lang=en) [Part 4](https://jelementi.quz.ma/articles/kalemegdan-4-the-keys-of-the-city) tells those stories.

Several names on this walk mislead. Kalemegdan was the field, not the fortress. The Roman Well is Austrian, the türbe was built for a different pasha, and the Nebojša Tower took its name from a tower that no longer exists.

## Words a guide uses

The English definitions follow the illustrated glossary of the Network of Vauban's Major Sites; the Serbian terms are the ones the fortress uses on its own pages.

- rampart, *bedem*: the main wall of a fortress; strictly, a mound of earth held in place by timber or a retaining wall[[26]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/rampart)[[27]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%81%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%BC-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%BC/)
- bastion, *bastion*: a low, five-sided work projecting from the main wall[[28]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/bastion)[[23]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%84%D0%BE/)
- ravelin, *ravelin*: a work in front of the wall between two bastions, entirely surrounded by ditches and often guarding a gate[[29]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/demi-lune-or-ravelin)[[23]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%84%D0%BE/)
- ditch or moat, *rov*: a trench below ground level, dug as an obstacle[[30]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/ditch)[[31]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%99%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/)
- covered way, *skriveni put*: a walkway along the outer edge of the ditch, open but sheltered by a parapet[[32]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/covert-way)[[31]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%99%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/)
- loophole, *puškarnica*: any opening made for firing from a fortification[[33]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/loophole-or-murder-hole)[[31]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%99%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0/)
- casemate, *kazamat*: a vaulted chamber proof against artillery[[34]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/casemate)[[35]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D1%83%D0%BD%D1%83%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B0%D1%88%D1%9A%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%B1%D0%BE%D0%BB-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-2/)
- counterguard, *kontragarda*: a low outwork protecting the faces of a bastion[[36]](https://sites-vauban.org/en/resources/glossary/counterguard-or-coverface)[[37]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%B8-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D0%BF%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%88%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D1%81%D0%B8%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B7/)
- barbican, *barbakan*: a small outwork in front of a gate, like the Zindan Gate[[19]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%BF%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BA%D1%81-%D0%B7%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%BD-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B5/?lang=en)[[38]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%BF%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BA%D1%81-%D0%B7%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%BD-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B5/)
- machicolation, *mašikula*: a defensive balcony above a gate, like the one on the Despot's Gate[[18]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%81%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%BC-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%BC/?lang=en)[[27]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0-%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%98%D0%B0-%D1%81%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%B4%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%BC-%D0%BA%D1%83%D0%BB%D0%BE%D0%BC/)
- keep, *donžon kula*: the strongest tower of a castle and its last line of defence, like the Despot's original Nebojša[[15]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BA%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0-%D0%B7%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%BA%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B7%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2/?lang=en)[[39]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%BA%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0-%D0%B7%D0%B0%D0%BC%D0%BA%D0%B0-%D0%B4%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%BE%D1%82%D0%B0-%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B5%D1%84%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B7%D0%B0%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%B2/)
- powder magazine, *barutni magacin*: a store for gunpowder, built to be safe from enemy artillery[[24]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D1%83%D1%82%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD/?lang=en)[[40]](https://www.beogradskatvrdjava.co.rs/%D0%B2%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B8-%D0%B1%D0%B0%D1%80%D1%83%D1%82%D0%BD%D0%B8-%D0%BC%D0%B0%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%86%D0%B8%D0%BD/)

## Dates to remember

- early 2nd century: the Fourth Flavian Legion's camp, about 20 hectares
- 12th century: the Byzantine castellum on the ridge
- first half of the 15th century: the Despot's Gate and the north-eastern rampart
- middle of the 15th century: the Zindan Gate
- around 1460: the Nebojša Tower
- 1688: work begins on the Leopold Gate
- 1693–1696: most of the King's Gate is built
- 1718–1720: the Big Gunpowder Magazine
- 1731: the Roman Well is finished
- from 1739: the Inner Stambol Gate
- middle of the 18th century: the Clock Tower
- 1784: the türbe, dedicated to Damat Ali Pasha in 1818–1819
- 1867: Ružica Church, in an Austrian powder magazine
- 1903: the Small Staircase
- 1928: the Victor and the Grand Staircase
- 1930: the Monument of Gratitude to France
- 1937: St Petka's Chapel
- 1982: the Despot Stefan monument
