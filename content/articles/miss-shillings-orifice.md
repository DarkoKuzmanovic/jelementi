In the summer of 1940, a British fighter pilot chasing a German Messerschmitt over southern England had one manoeuvre he could not copy. If the German pilot shoved his stick forward and pitched steeply into a dive, the pursuing Spitfire or Hurricane would follow nose-down, and then its Rolls-Royce Merlin engine would cough, falter and sometimes stop altogether.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) The German simply flew away while the British pilot wrestled with a silent propeller and tried to restart his engine before he ran out of sky.

The difference was not courage or skill. It was plumbing. The German Daimler-Benz engines used direct fuel injection, which kept fuel at constant pressure whatever the aircraft did.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) The Merlin still breathed through a carburettor of the kind you might find, in principle, on a car. Push the nose hard down and the fuel inside it went where physics, not the engine, told it to go.

British pilots worked out a countermeasure: a half roll before following into the dive, so the aircraft only ever felt positive gravity.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) It worked, but it cost time, and in a dogfight time was the whole contest. What they needed was an engine that would keep running whatever direction they pointed it. What they got, in the meantime, was a washer.

## The engine that could not stand on its head

The early Merlin came with a dual-choke updraught SU carburettor, with a float chamber that metered fuel the way a lavatory cistern meters water.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) A float riding on the fuel opened and closed a needle valve, letting in just enough to keep the level steady. It was a neat arrangement for level flight. It assumed that down was down.

In a steep dive the aircraft accelerates earthward faster than free fall, and everything loose inside it behaves as if gravity has reversed. Fuel is forced toward the top of the float chamber instead of down toward the engine, so the engine is starved and loses power.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) If the dive continues, the picture gets worse: fuel collecting in the chamber pushes the float down to the floor, the float holds the needle valve open, and the carburettor floods, drowning the supercharger in an over-rich mixture.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) The resulting rich mixture cut-out could shut the engine down completely.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)

Pilots gave the two stages names. A brief stutter from fuel starvation was a weak cut. The full flooding shutdown was a rich cut. Either way, at the moment of greatest urgency, the finest fighter engine in Britain went quiet.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)

The Germans had solved this class of problem years earlier. The Daimler-Benz DB 601 inverted V12 in the Messerschmitt Bf 109 had used fuel injection since 1937, so its pilots could pitch forward and open the throttle with impunity.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) Complaints from British pilots about cut-outs in dives and brief inverted flight piled up through the Battle of France and the Battle of Britain, and the search for a fix became concentrated.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) Rolls-Royce produced an improved carburettor, but it failed in testing.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) The answer came instead from the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough, from an engineer who spent her weekends racing motorcycles at over a hundred miles an hour.

## The engineer on the Norton

Beatrice Shilling was born on 8 March 1909 at Waterlooville in Hampshire, the daughter of a master butcher.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[3]](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/blog/ladies-of-the-raf/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) As a child she spent her pocket money on hand tools and won a prize in a national Meccano contest.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) At fourteen she bought a second-hand motorcycle, taught herself to take its two-stroke engine apart and put it back together, and decided she wanted to be an engineer.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) She later described a childhood of Meccano, home-built wireless sets and penknives bought with saved pennies.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)

Engineering in the 1920s was not a profession that welcomed girls, in its trade unions or its institutions.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/) Shilling found a way in through Margaret Partridge, an electrical engineer running her own company, who took her on for a three-year apprenticeship and then encouraged her to study for a degree.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/) With an interest-free loan arranged through the Women's Engineering Society, Shilling enrolled at the Victoria University of Manchester in 1929 as one of only two women in the first year women took the course.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) She graduated with honours in electrical engineering in 1932 alongside Sheila McGuffie and stayed on for a master's in mechanical engineering.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[5]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Beatrice_Shilling)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling)

Work was scarce in the Depression. She spent time as a research assistant, publishing on internal combustion engines, before joining the Royal Aircraft Establishment in 1936.[[5]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Beatrice_Shilling)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) Her first post was writing handbooks in the technical publications department. Within months she had talked her way into the engine department, working on carburettors, and by November 1939 she was the technical officer in charge of carburettor research and development.[[5]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Beatrice_Shilling)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) Factory workers respected her because she could do the work with her hands, including brazing a copper joint like a professional fitter.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling)

Meanwhile she raced. From 1934 she rode a Norton M30 500cc machine at Brooklands, the rough concrete bowl in Surrey, servicing it herself.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[5]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Beatrice_Shilling) On 24 August 1934 she lapped the circuit at an average of 101.85 miles an hour, becoming only the second woman to break a hundred there and earning the British Motorcycle Racing Club's Gold Star.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) The first had been Florence Blenkiron, earlier the same year.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) In 1938 Shilling married George Naylor, a mathematician from the RAE's test department who also raced bikes. Tradition at Farnborough has it that she refused to marry him until he had earned his own Brooklands Gold Star.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)

When war came, racing stopped and her detuned Norton became everyday transport.[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/) By the summer of 1940, the carburettor problem had become her problem.

## A brass thimble with a hole

Shilling's insight was characteristically practical. If the carburettor flooded because too much fuel could rush in at the wrong moment, then limit how much fuel could rush in at all. She designed a small brass restrictor, first shaped like a thimble and later simplified to a flat washer, with a hole of precisely calculated diameter: large enough to let through the full fuel flow the engine needed at maximum power, small enough to stop the surge that drowned it.[[4]](https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/beatrice-shilling--engineer-and-battle-of-britain-heroine/)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) It came in two versions, calibrated for different manifold pressures, twelve and fifteen pounds per square inch.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) Its official name was the R.A.E. restrictor.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)

The beauty of it was operational. The disc could be fitted into the fuel line without removing the carburettor, which meant aircraft did not have to be taken out of service.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) A modification that required every fighter in the country to visit a workshop would have been nearly useless in 1940. One that a small team could fit on the airfield, squadron by squadron, was worth more than a perfect device that arrived next year.

Early in 1941 Shilling led exactly such a team around RAF fighter stations, front-line units first, fitting the restrictor to Merlin after Merlin.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) She travelled by her preferred method, the Norton, and her arrival at an airfield with a bag of tools and a brusque manner became, in her biographer's words, something of a legend.[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/) By March 1941 the device was installed throughout RAF Fighter Command.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)

The pilots gave it the name it has carried ever since. Sir Stanley Hooker, the Rolls-Royce engineer who led supercharger development, christened it Miss Shilling's orifice, or the Tilly orifice for short.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/) Shilling had married but kept her maiden name for professional purposes, so the "Miss" was a courtesy of the airfield rather than a statement of fact.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) Her biographer Matthew Freudenberg reckons the name probably amused her, recognising familiarity rather than disrespect.[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/)

Together with adjustments to the needle valve, the restrictor let pilots snap into quick negative-gravity manoeuvres without losing power.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) The RAF Museum puts it strongly: the little device saved a huge number of pilots' lives.[[2]](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/blog/women-and-girls-in-science/)[[3]](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/blog/ladies-of-the-raf/) That is a qualitative judgment rather than a countable one, since nobody can say which pilots would otherwise have died, but the logic behind it is plain. Every engine failure in combat risked a pilot as well as an aircraft, and the restrictor removed a failure that struck at the worst possible moment.

## A stopgap, not a cure

Shilling never claimed the washer was the whole answer, and it was not. It minimised cut-outs to an acceptable level rather than eliminating them.[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/) A pilot could now bunt into a dive after an enemy, but he could not fly upside down for any length of time. Sustained inverted flight still defeated the float carburettor, and the device remained what its makers always said it was: a stopgap.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice)

The permanent cure arrived in 1943 with the Bendix pressure carburettor, which did away with the float chamber's vulnerability.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) Shilling's team at the RAE kept working on the problem in the meantime, including development of the RAE Hobson injection carburettor, though that design never entered production.[[4]](https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/beatrice-shilling--engineer-and-battle-of-britain-heroine/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) The orifice stayed standard equipment until the new carburettors were ready for service.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)

It is worth keeping the proportions straight. The Battle of Britain was won with the restrictor barely fitted or not yet fitted at all, since installation across Fighter Command was completed in March 1941, after the battle's official end.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Shilling%27s_orifice) What the device did was restore to British fighters in 1941 and after a capability the Germans had enjoyed all along: the freedom to follow an enemy down. Years later Keith Maddock, a wartime engineering chief, told the BBC that Beatrice Shilling had helped Britain win the war and that of it there was no doubt.[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/) That is the testimony of one grateful engineer, not a measured share of victory, and the evidence supports something narrower but still remarkable: at minimal cost and without grounding a single aircraft, she gave Fighter Command back a manoeuvre.

## After the war

The honours came slowly, as they often did for women doing war work. In the 1949 honours she was appointed OBE for her wartime work, by then a principal scientific officer.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[5]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Beatrice_Shilling) After the war the RAE moved toward jets and rockets, and Shilling with it: ramjets, guided weapons, heat tests on scale models of the tanks for what became the Blue Streak missile, studies of wet runways and braking, even a bobsled for the RAF Olympic team.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling)[[8]](https://www.damninteresting.com/how-miss-shillings-orifice-helped-win-the-war/) She never reached the top posts at Farnborough. Senior rank there went to men, though colleagues also remembered that she dressed for the workshop rather than the committee room and made no secret of her contempt for bureaucracy and underserving superiors.[[1]](https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/beatrice-tilly-shilling-celebrated-aeronautical-and-motorcycle-engineer/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling)

She kept racing, switching to cars after the war with George, scoring podium finishes at Goodwood in an Austin-Healey Sprite and campaigning an Elva single-seater until accidents ended that chapter.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) In 1967 she was called in to help the American driver Dan Gurney cure an overheating problem on his Eagle Formula 1 car.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) She retired from the RAE in 1969 after thirty-three years and died on 18 November 1990.[[2]](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/blog/women-and-girls-in-science/)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling)

The memorials accumulated afterward: an honorary doctorate from Surrey, a pub in Farnborough called the Tilly Shilling, her racing trophies in the Brooklands Museum, university buildings named for her at Royal Holloway and Coventry, and a biography in the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography published for the Women's Engineering Society centenary.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling) Her university offers a Beatrice Shilling scholarship.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Shilling)

None of that was the point for her. The point was visible on any fighter station in the spring of 1941: a small brass disc, a hole of exactly the right size, and engines that kept running when their pilots needed them most.
