On the wall of Djehutihotep's tomb chapel at Dayr al-Barsha, in Middle Egypt, a seated stone figure travels on a sledge. Ropes run from the load to rows of men. On the front of the sledge, a man tips a vessel toward the ground. Elsewhere in the scene, others carry water and timber. The painting dates from Egypt's Middle Kingdom, around 1880 BC, and the statue represents Djehutihotep himself, a regional governor.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

The statue was no garden ornament. An inscription describes it as thirteen cubits high, roughly 6.8 metres. Its stone came from Hatnub, a quarry known for the banded stone often called Egyptian alabaster; the material is also known as travertine. The hauling scene depicts 172 men. That is a count of figures in a composition, not a surviving payroll: we cannot assume the painting records the exact number present throughout the journey.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.2474/trol.11.466)

A person pouring water immediately ahead of a heavy load is a detail any modern experimentalist might want to investigate. In 2014, physicist Abdoulaye Fall and colleagues published the results of pulling a small sled across sand with varying amounts of water. The experiment found that dampening some sands could substantially reduce the force needed. It did not reconstruct the governor's journey, or establish that every pyramid block travelled over wet sand.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

## A wall, a statue and a difficult road

Djehutihotep governed the Hare nome, an administrative region in Middle Egypt, during the Twelfth Dynasty. The chapel above his burial chamber contains a long account of the statue's transport as well as the picture. Franck Monnier's study provides a reconstruction and translation, drawing on early records and later photographs. Much of the decoration has suffered since the nineteenth century, and the accompanying text is now almost entirely lost from the wall. Early drawings and photographs therefore matter when scholars study it.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

The image is crowded. The statue is lashed to a sledge with upturned runners. Teams haul it with ropes; a figure on the statue appears to beat time. At the front, the anonymous water-pourer attends to the path directly before the sledge. Below are water carriers and men with a large timber. The inscription identifies the latter as wood for the transport road, but it does not tell us precisely how the pieces were arranged.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

The text describes a difficult route over hard, stony ground and the recruitment of people to prepare a road. It also celebrates the enterprise in the language of a governor's monument: people rejoice, strength is praised and the ruler's favour is acknowledged. It is a record with a purpose, not an engineer's logbook. The depicted count and the account of cooperation are evidence to work with, but neither supplies a complete timetable, a measured slope or a record of every labourer.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

Where the colossus ended up is debated. Monnier discusses a destination called Tjerti on the eastern side of the Nile; earlier interpretations placed the statue elsewhere. Even its weight has to be estimated from its dimensions and material rather than read off a scale. In a 2016 analysis, Simon Ayrinhac estimated about 70 tonnes for the statue, with an uncertainty of roughly five tonnes. The sledge would add weight of its own.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.2474/trol.11.466)

The practical problem was consequently larger than the picture of a few people tugging on a rope might suggest. A team had to keep its footing and coordinate its effort. The load had to be secured; changes in terrain and incline would alter the force required. Getting a stationary sledge moving could be harder than keeping it moving. Even if the route and the workforce were known exactly, the material underneath the runners would remain an important part of the calculation.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.2474/trol.11.466)

## A small sled and three kinds of sand

The 2014 researchers did not attempt to reproduce a seventy-tonne statue. Their sled was a rounded piece of PVC, 11 by 7.5 centimetres, weighted and pulled by a horizontal cord. Sandpaper covered its underside for the main measurements; the researchers also tested a smooth bottom and found qualitatively similar, though less reproducible, results. They dried the sand, mixed in measured amounts of water and compacted it before pulling the sled. A force sensor recorded what happened as it moved.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

On dry sand, a heap built up in front of the sled. The researchers observed a high initial peak in force before the sled reached a steadier sliding state. With water added, the mound became smaller and the peak fell. That observation gives the experiment its most approachable image: the sled did not merely rub against a surface, it also had to push sand out of its way.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

The team measured three sands with different distributions of grain sizes. In all three, adding water initially lowered the sliding resistance, but the size of the improvement varied considerably. For the most uniform sand, the reported reduction in the dynamic friction coefficient was about 10 percent; for Iranian sand it was 26 percent; for a standard sand with a wider range of grain sizes it was 40 percent. These numbers describe the laboratory materials and setup, not three measurements of an Egyptian road.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

For that standard sand, the paper reports reductions of up to 40 percent in the force needed to keep the loaded sled moving at a steady speed, and up to 70 percent in the force needed to start it. Those are two different measurements. The paper also describes the best sliding result as close to a factor of two. The popular shorthand that water simply "halved the force" misses both the variation between sands and the difference between starting and sliding.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

Nor was more water always better. In the Iranian-sand measurements, resistance rose again above a water content of about 5 percent. The optimum is a feature of a given material and test; it is not a recipe saying that any ancient route should contain exactly that proportion of water. The team's procedure mixed water throughout prepared sand, while the painting shows a person pouring from a vessel ahead of a sledge. The two are related situations, not identical ones.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

## Why damp sand behaves differently

A handful of very dry sand falls apart because its grains can move past one another readily. Add a little water and small liquid bridges can form where grains meet. The curved water surfaces produce forces that draw the grains together. The resulting damp sand can hold a shape, which is why a sandcastle builder knows that bone-dry sand makes an uncooperative building material.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

In the sled experiment, the researchers measured the sand's shear stiffness as well as the force of sliding. They found an inverse relationship across their measurements: as the sand became stiffer, the measured sliding resistance fell. Their interpretation was that a stiffer bed resisted piling up in front of the sled. The little water bridges helped the grains act together, making the bed more resistant to deformation under the moving load.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

The water has to occupy the right sort of spaces. The paper describes imaging of liquid among model grains: at low liquid content, separate bridges formed at grain contacts; at higher contents, bridges and filled pores coexisted, and still more liquid produced larger connected pockets. As the bridges merge and the sand approaches saturation, the effect on stiffness changes. In the authors' measurements, sufficiently wet sand became less stiff again and the sled's resistance rose.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)

This is a different proposition from coating an ordinary solid surface with a lubricant. There may be friction where the sled meets the grains, but the granular bed can also deform and accumulate. Adding water changes the bed itself. A lab result about that changing bed gives a plausible reason to wet sand, while leaving open what surface lay under the ancient sledge.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.2474/trol.11.466)

## What the water-pourer can tell us

The painting predates the experiment by nearly four millennia, but it was not an instruction manual waiting to be decoded by physicists. The researchers noted that some earlier interpretations treated the pouring as ceremonial. Their result makes a practical function plausible: under suitable conditions, a little water could make a sandy path easier to slide across. It cannot establish the intentions of the person depicted, the precise composition of the route or whether ritual and practical meanings excluded one another.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

The timber in the same scene complicates a simple wet-sand answer. Monnier reviews several proposed uses for the wood, including elements of a prepared track, but notes that the details are insufficient to settle its exact role. Water applied to a wooden track would raise a different friction problem from water mixed into sand. The painting records both water and wood; it does not label the surface under every stage of the trip.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)

Ayrinhac's later engineering analysis favoured a prepared, lubricated surface and considered direct hauling over sand unlikely under his assumptions about the load and available traction. He also stressed that friction values and human performance were uncertain and that experiments at realistic scale were needed. His calculations are a challenge to a simple sandy-road reconstruction, not a direct measurement of Djehutihotep's route. Fall's team, for its part, argued that wet sand could make such hauling feasible. The two studies do not settle the matter in the same way.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.2474/trol.11.466)

Scale matters. A small plastic sled pulled at controlled speed through a carefully prepared bed is useful for identifying a physical effect. A stone colossus on a wooden sledge, pulled by a large group over changing ground, introduces pressures, footing, rope angles, inclines and track construction that the small test cannot completely reproduce. Ayrinhac's estimate of the contact pressure also differs substantially from Fall's historical estimate. Those estimates depend on uncertain assumptions about the real load and contact area, so neither turns the illustration into a measured engineering diagram.[[1]](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2365399/160672_Sliding_Friction_on_Wet_and_Dry_Sand.pdf)[[3]](https://doi.org/10.2474/trol.11.466)

The statue scene is sometimes recruited to explain the building of the pyramids. It offers evidence that Egyptians used a sledge, ropes and organised manpower to move a very large object, and it invites useful experiments about friction. It is not a picture of a pyramid block, however, and the painting belongs to the Middle Kingdom, centuries after the Old Kingdom pyramids at Giza.[[2]](https://web.ujaen.es/investiga/egiptologia/journalarchitecture/downloads/JAEA4/JAEA4_Monnier2.pdf)[[4]](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/egypt-in-the-old-kingdom-ca-2649-2150-b-c) Methods could vary with material, terrain and the task at hand. A statue's journey cannot answer every question about a pyramid construction site.

The little figure at the front of the sledge remains worth looking at closely. He is one part of a scene that also includes a monumental load, ropes, water carriers and timber for a road. Fall and colleagues showed that wetting sand can make a substantial difference to a sled. The tomb preserves the older, messier question: what, exactly, lay beneath this one?
