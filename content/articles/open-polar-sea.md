On a map of the Arctic in the middle of the nineteenth century, the blank space at the top of the world could look less like a warning than an invitation. Explorers had crossed parts of the surrounding ice, seen open leads between floes, watched birds fly north and heard reports of water where a ship could not go. Perhaps, they reasoned, the pack formed a belt around a vast open ocean. Beyond the belt, warm currents and milder air might make a route to the North Pole possible.

The imagined sea acquired a name: the Open Polar Sea. It was never a single theory with one author or one fixed map. Some proposed a navigable channel, some a warmer basin, and some a broad, ice-free waterway near the pole. By the 1850s, however, several influential geographers and explorers were making the same inference from fragments of evidence. They did not simply invent a blue patch on a map. They built an argument from observations that were real, but incomplete, and from physical explanations that seemed to fit them. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007087421000522)

## A sea assembled from clues

The idea was older than the expeditions that made it famous. In 1774, the British antiquarian and Royal Society fellow Daines Barrington presented a paper on reaching the North Pole. He gathered accounts from whalers and other navigators who had reached high northern latitudes, arguing that past failures did not prove the pole could not be approached. His case helped prompt the British expedition led by Constantine Phipps the following year. Barrington knew that ice could block a route; he also believed that reports of open water and shifting ice left room for a way through. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.3931/e-rara-11343)

Those reports were not necessarily lies or fantasies. Sea ice moves, breaks, and opens leads. A ship near the edge of a floe might meet navigable water, while a point farther away remained sealed by pack. In Arctic air, temperature layers can also bend light and make distant ice, land, or water appear displaced or stretched. A glimpse of open water could be accurate at the horizon and still tell little about what lay beyond it. [[14]](https://doi.org/10.2312/polarforschung.49.2.173)

In the nineteenth century, oceanographer Matthew Fontaine Maury supplied the theory with a new vocabulary. As head of the United States Navy's Depot of Charts and Instruments and superintendent of the Naval Observatory, Maury helped organize ships' logs into charts of winds and currents. In his 1855 book *The Physical Geography of the Sea*, he argued that currents, migrating whales, and a hypothesized flow of warmer water beneath the surface all pointed toward a comparatively mild region in the Arctic Ocean. The evidence did not demonstrate an ice-free sea at the pole. Maury treated it as mutually supporting signs of a system whose most important part remained unseen. [[3]](https://celebrating200years.noaa.gov/rarebooks/sea/welcome.html)

The missing British explorer Sir John Franklin made the possibility more urgent. Franklin's ships had disappeared in 1845 while searching for a Northwest Passage. If they had made their way through the ice, supporters wondered, perhaps they had reached the open sea and were still alive somewhere beyond the known coast. In the United States, merchant Henry Grinnell financed search expeditions. Elisha Kent Kane, a naval surgeon who had served on an earlier search, used the Franklin mystery and Maury's oceanography to argue for another voyage north. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007087421000522)

Across the Atlantic, August Petermann was making a related case with maps. The Prussian cartographer had trained under Heinrich Berghaus and studied reports from earlier voyages, but had never been to the Arctic. In 1852 he published a polar chart that combined currents, temperature patterns, ice reports, and speculative geography. Its shaded bands and arrows made a proposed route look like a coherent physical system. Petermann argued for a winter departure, reasoning that seasonal changes in currents and river ice might clear a passage toward the central basin. He sent his proposal through a network of naval officers, learned societies, patrons, and diplomats. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007087421000522)[[4]](https://collections.lib.uwm.edu/digital/collection/agdm/id/355/)

Petermann's map was not a private doodle. Kane used a map projected from Petermann, Berghaus, and British Hydrographic Office materials to illustrate his own 1853 paper on access to an open polar sea. Copies and arguments travelled between London, New York, and scientific societies on both sides of the Atlantic. In Gotha, where Petermann later worked with the publishing house Justus Perthes, his geographical journal *Petermanns Geographische Mitteilungen* became a major venue for maps and reports. The map lent the theory authority; the theory, in turn, gave the map a destination. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007087421000522)[[4]](https://collections.lib.uwm.edu/digital/collection/agdm/id/355/)[[5]](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/august-petermann/)

## What Morton saw

Kane's Second Grinnell Expedition left the United States in 1853 aboard the *Advance*. Its public purpose was to search for Franklin. Its planned route also aimed north through Smith Sound, between Greenland and Ellesmere Island, toward the sea that Kane and his supporters expected beyond the ice. The ship became trapped near the Greenland coast and spent two winters in the Arctic. The crew made observations, mapped coastlines, hunted for food, and travelled by sledge across the sea ice. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007087421000522)[[6]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexploratio01kane)

One of those journeys produced the sighting that Kane would later present as the theory's strongest field evidence. In June 1854, William Morton and the Greenlandic hunter Hans Hendrik travelled north from the expedition's winter quarters. From high ground near the head of Kennedy Channel, Morton reported seeing an expanse of water, apparently open to the north. Kane named the headland Cape Constitution and published the report in *Arctic Explorations*, his account of the expedition. [[6]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexploratio01kane)

The report mattered because it seemed to bring the map into view. Kane had argued that an open sea should lie beyond the land and ice; now a member of his party said he had seen water from a point farther north than earlier American explorers had reached. Kane treated the sighting as confirmation. But the observation could not show how far the water extended. Morton might have seen a local lead or a distant water surface; an Arctic mirage could also have distorted the horizon. The later geographer John Kirtland Wright called Morton mistaken, while noting how much weight the report carried in reviving the theory. The expedition had evidence of open water in the region, not proof of a navigable sea around the pole. [[6]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexploratio01kane)[[7]](https://doi.org/10.2307/211752)

Kane's book preserves the distinction between what the expedition saw and what it concluded. It is a vivid account of a ship bound by ice and men travelling over it, but Kane also used the narrative to defend a proposal he had spent years promoting. The same passage could serve as a field report and as an argument for the next expedition. That did not make it fraudulent. It did mean that a striking sighting was doing more work than it could bear. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007087421000522)[[6]](https://archive.org/details/arcticexploratio01kane)

Kane's party eventually abandoned the *Advance* in 1855 and made its way south by boat and sledge. Franklin was not found. The expedition did, however, add coastlines, observations, and testimony to the body of Arctic knowledge. Isaac Israel Hayes, who had served as a surgeon on Kane's expedition, returned in 1860 with the schooner *United States*. His voyage of 1860–61 again travelled by Smith Sound in search of a route toward the pole. Hayes hoped to push a ship to about eighty degrees north, then carry a boat over the ice to the open sea he expected beyond. The Smithsonian supplied scientific instruments and equipment; Hayes's expedition also produced observations later published with Smithsonian support. [[8]](https://www.loc.gov/item/17020375/)[[9]](https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/siris_sic_13229)

Charles Francis Hall's *Polaris* expedition of 1871–73 pursued the same northward ambition, but did not reach the pole. [[10]](https://www.si.edu/object/instructions-issued-polaris-expedition:siris_sic_78)

## The ice would not make way

Petermann's vision outlasted the expeditions that had first promoted it. In 1879 the *Jeannette*, commanded by George Washington De Long, entered the Arctic from the Bering Strait. Petermann's geographical ideas had helped shape the plan. The ship did not sail freely toward a polar sea. Pack ice trapped it, carried it for nearly two years, then crushed its hull in June 1881. The crew escaped over the ice in boats and sledges; many died on the journey south. The voyage mapped unknown territory and gathered scientific observations, but its route offered no evidence for a warm, open ocean at the pole. [[11]](https://www.loc.gov/collections/meeting-of-frontiers/articles-and-essays/mutual-perceptions/the-jeannette/)

The *Jeannette* did leave behind a clue of another kind. Three years after the ship sank north of Siberia, some of its wreckage was found on the opposite side of the Arctic, off Greenland. The debris had crossed the ocean with the moving ice. Fridtjof Nansen saw in that drift not a clear sea waiting beyond the pack, but a current that could carry a vessel across the basin if the ship could survive the pressure. His answer was the *Fram*, built with a rounded hull designed to rise rather than be crushed when ice closed around it. [[12]](https://www2.whoi.edu/site/beaufortgyre/history/nansen-and-the-drift-of-the-fram-1893-1896/)

Nansen and the *Fram* entered the pack north of Siberia in 1893. The ship drifted with the ice for nearly three years. It did not reach the pole: Nansen and Hjalmar Johansen left it for a sledge journey and turned back before they got there. But the *Fram*'s measured drift and the expedition's oceanographic work established a deep central Arctic basin crossed by moving sea ice. The north was not a permanent open sea ringed by an impassable belt. It was an ocean whose ice itself moved across the map. [[12]](https://www2.whoi.edu/site/beaufortgyre/history/nansen-and-the-drift-of-the-fram-1893-1896/)[[13]](https://archive.org/details/farthestnorthbei01nans)

This did not mean there was never open water in the Arctic. Leads and polynyas form among the ice, and seasonal openings can be large. Nor did the explorers imagine every detail from nothing: currents do flow, whales migrate, and the pack does open. Their mistake was to treat local openings, distant glimpses, and a plausible account of heat and currents as evidence for a stable, navigable sea at the top of the world. [[3]](https://celebrating200years.noaa.gov/rarebooks/sea/welcome.html)[[7]](https://doi.org/10.2307/211752)[[13]](https://archive.org/details/farthestnorthbei01nans)

The explorers were not foolish to expect an opening: sea ice does break, currents do move, and reports described water at high latitudes. Their mistake was to let separate clues reinforce one another until a map looked like confirmation. Kane had field experience; Petermann had cartographic skill; Maury had built influential current charts. A sighting from a headland could support a local opening, but not a route across the central Arctic. Nansen's drift supplied the stronger test: the basin was deep, and ice crossed it.
