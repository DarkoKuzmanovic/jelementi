Stand on the Serbian bank at Kostol, a few kilometres below Kladovo, and look across the Danube on a low-water day. Near both shores you can make out heavy stonework in the shallows: the abutments of the longest bridge the ancient world ever built. It carried the army of the emperor Trajan into the conquest of Dacia, and it was designed by the emperor's own architect and engineer.[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Apollodorus-of-Damascus)[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/)

The man was Apollodorus of Damascus. Born in Damascus, Greek by culture, he was Trajan's principal engineer, the designer of the forum, the markets, the column and the basilica that still carry Trajan's name in Rome.[[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Apollodorus-of-Damascus) His Danube bridge was the project that made his reputation as a military engineer first: a permanent crossing over one of Europe's widest rivers, built between the First and Second Dacian Wars so the legions would never again depend on boats and winter ice.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-9270.2008.00216.x)[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/)

The site was chosen with care. Below the Iron Gates, where the river breaks out of the gorge, the Danube spreads wide and comparatively calm. At the chosen point it was about eight hundred metres across, and the Romans bridged nearly eleven hundred and thirty-five metres of it, causeways included, on twenty piers of brick and stone.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trajans-Bridge)[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/) On those piers rested the part you would actually have walked on: a timber superstructure of arched wooden trusses, span after span, carrying a roadway high above the current.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trajans-Bridge)[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/)

We know what it looked like from two Roman sources that rarely agree with each other. One is the historian Cassius Dio, writing a century later. He could not admire the bridge enough: twenty piers of squared stone, he says, a hundred and fifty feet high above their foundations and sixty wide, standing a hundred and seventy feet apart and joined by arches, every one of them set in a river "so deep, in water so full of eddies, and on a bottom so muddy" that diverting the stream was out of the question.[[2]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/68*.html) Those are Dio's figures, in Roman feet, and scholarship treats them as admiration rather than survey data.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-9270.2008.00216.x) What the archaeology keeps is simpler, and it is also what amazed Dio most: the piers were stone, the spans were timber, and planting twenty stone piers in a muddy, racing river with no way to turn the water aside is the part of the work he keeps returning to with disbelief.

The other witness is carved in marble. On Trajan's Column in Rome, scene ninety-nine shows the emperor in travelling clothes, attendants around him, performing a sacrifice at a garlanded altar in front of the newly built bridge over the Danube.[[8]](https://www.trajans-column.org/?imagemap_area=untitled-image-map-area-40) Behind him stands a bearded figure some scholars take to be Apollodorus himself, watching his own work being consecrated.[[8]](https://www.trajans-column.org/?imagemap_area=untitled-image-map-area-40) The carving settles one argument Dio leaves open: the arches on the Column are unmistakably timber, great curved wooden trusses between the stone piers, exactly as the modern consensus reconstructs them.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trajans-Bridge)[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/) The bridge also appeared on Trajan's coins, a boast in pocket change.[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/)

Dio tells us why Trajan wanted it, and his reason is winter. The Danube freezes. Trajan feared that one frozen winter would bring war across the ice onto the Roman side before his troops could get at the enemy, so he built a crossing that worked in every season.[[2]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/68*.html) It did its job. In 105 Trajan crossed his new bridge at the head of the army, fought the hard, slow campaign Dio describes, and took Dacia. Decebalus killed himself, his treasure was fished out of a diverted riverbed, and Dacia became a Roman province.[[2]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/68*.html)

Then comes the oddest passage in Dio's account. The bridge, he says, "is of no use to us; for merely the piers are standing, affording no means of crossing, as if they had been erected for the sole purpose of demonstrating that there is nothing which human ingenuity cannot accomplish."[[2]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/68*.html) The deck was gone already in Dio's lifetime. He blames Hadrian, Trajan's successor, who feared the crossing worked both ways: once barbarians overpowered the bridge guard, they could march straight into Moesia. So Hadrian removed the superstructure.[[2]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/68*.html)

That may not be the whole story. Serbian archaeology notes that the Hadrian story looks like a temporary measure, with the bridge restored soon after, and that the final destruction remains an open question.[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/) Britannica gives the demolition to a later emperor still, Aurelian, when Rome withdrew from Dacia.[[3]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trajans-Bridge) What is certain is the shape Dio saw: piers without a deck, a monument to capability rather than a crossing. After Dacia was abandoned the bridge was most likely disabled the same way, by lifting the wooden roadway off, and the piers stood in the river for centuries. Some were later broken up because they blocked shipping; today only the remains near the two banks are visible above water, though underwater survey has traced all the piers still lying on the riverbed.[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/)

The bridge was never a solitary structure. It came with a pair of forts, Drobeta on the Romanian bank and Pontes on the Serbian one, built at the same time to guard the two ends.[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/) Pontes, the fort on the Serbian bank, was a square auxiliary fort about a hundred and twelve by a hundred and twenty metres. Its Trajanic layout survived every later rebuilding: repairs under Marcus Aurelius, a major reconstruction under Septimius Severus, then abandonment when the bridge lost its purpose, a revival under Justinian, and silence after the Avar invasion of the sixth century.[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/) A bronze head of Trajan's father found there is now one of the finest Roman portraits dug up in Moesia.[[5]](https://www.danubelimes-serbia.rs/en/archaeological-sites/ponteskostol/)

And the bridge was the second half of a larger machine. Before the legions could cross the Danube, they had to sail up it, and the Iron Gates gorge nearly stopped ships altogether. So Trajan first finished a road through the gorge itself, cut into the cliffs above the water and carried on beams over it. The memorial of that work is still in the gorge: the Tabula Traiana, a tablet carved into the rock face a metre and a half above the Roman road, recording that in the year 100 the final and most difficult section was complete.[[6]](https://npdjerdap.rs/en/cultural-heritage/) The inscription explains the method in the emperor's own boastful shorthand: cliffs cut through, beams raised, road built.[[7]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/kulturno_nasledje/trajans-plaque/) When the Djerdap dam raised the river in the twentieth century, Yugoslavia refused to drown it. In 1969 engineers cut a three-hundred-ton block of cliff, tablet and a strip of the road with it, and jacked it twenty to twenty-five metres up the rock, where it sits today above the waterline, the giant added letters TABULA TRAIANA still legible from a boat.[[7]](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/kulturno_nasledje/trajans-plaque/)

The bridge got the same century's attention, minus the lifting. In the summer of 2003, nineteen centuries after construction began, Serbian heritage archaeologists started the first underwater survey of the remains: multibeam sonar over the whole bridge zone, geophysical mapping, photogrammetry in difficult water, all non-destructive.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-9270.2008.00216.x) The survey established which piers survive and in what condition, and made the case that they need long-term protection rather than souvenir diving.[[1]](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1095-9270.2008.00216.x)

That is roughly where things stand. A road tablet lifted up a cliff. A square fort keeping quiet watch above Kostol. Twenty pier foundations in the mud, traced by sonar, a few stones showing near the banks. And on a column in Rome, an emperor sacrificing in front of a wooden-arched bridge that no longer exists, with the bearded figure of possibly its architect standing behind his shoulder.[[8]](https://www.trajans-column.org/?imagemap_area=untitled-image-map-area-40)[[2]](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/68*.html)
