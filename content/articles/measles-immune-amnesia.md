Measles begins in the respiratory tract, then spreads through the body. Fever, cough, runny nose and the familiar rash are the visible part. After the rash fades, the body may look as though it has put the illness behind it. Some of its defenses, however, may have lost records of earlier encounters with other infections. Researchers call this effect “immune amnesia.” The name is vivid, but the change is selective: measles does not erase every part of the immune system or every antibody in the blood. [[4]](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles)[[5]](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/about/questions.html)

An immune system’s memory is not a single file stored in one place. B cells that have encountered a pathogen can become memory B cells, ready to respond quickly if it returns. Some become antibody-producing plasma cells. Other immune cells, including memory T cells, recognize infected cells and help organize the response. Antibodies are the proteins that bind particular features of microbes. If memory cells or antibody-producing cells disappear, the body may retain fewer of the molecular clues that helped it recognize familiar threats. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aay6125)

## A virus with a route into memory

Measles virus uses a receptor called CD150, also known as SLAMF1, to enter many immune cells. Memory B cells and memory T cells can carry this receptor, as can other lymphocytes and immune cells. The virus reaches lymphoid tissue and infects susceptible cells there. Human-cell experiments found that measles can infect both naive and memory B cells; memory T cells are also among the susceptible targets. CD150 is not a marker found only on memory cells, but its presence helps explain why measles can reach the immune system’s working archive. [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1038/35022579)[[7]](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.00131-18)

During acute measles, the number of circulating lymphocytes can fall and later return toward normal. A normal count does not mean that the same mix of cells has returned. Infected cells may be depleted; as the population rebuilds, new cells can replace some of the lost ones without restoring all the old, pathogen-specific lineages. That distinction helps explain why someone can recover from measles, develop a strong measles-specific response, and still have weaker defenses against infections encountered before it. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aay6125)

The first clear measurement of this effect came from an outbreak in the Netherlands. Researchers had blood samples from children in a community with low measles vaccination rates, collected before and after laboratory-confirmed measles. They studied seventy-seven unvaccinated children, with a mean age of nine. The average interval between samples was about ten weeks, and the second sample was taken, on average, seven weeks after infection. The unusual advantage was the first blood draw: it gave the researchers a picture of each child’s antibody repertoire before measles changed it. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

## Reading antibodies with VirScan

The team used VirScan, a blood test that surveys antibodies against thousands of pathogen targets. They built a library of bacteriophages, harmless viruses that can display short pieces of proteins from other organisms. The expanded library represented the proteomes of most known human pathogenic viruses, about four hundred species and strains, along with many bacterial proteins. Each phage displayed a short peptide, an epitope that an antibody might recognize. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

Researchers mixed the phage library with plasma from the children. Antibodies attached to phages displaying a matching peptide; the bound phages were pulled out and their genetic material sequenced. The sequence identified which peptide had been recognized. Counting distinct matches gave a measure of repertoire diversity, while the amount of binding to each peptide provided a relative signal for antibody abundance. It was a way to ask, across a very large catalogue, “Which molecular signatures can this blood still recognize?” [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

VirScan has useful limits. It mainly detects antibodies that bind short, continuous stretches of protein, not every folded or three-dimensional feature of a pathogen. It measures recognition in plasma, not whether each detected antibody would prevent disease in a person. Some antibodies can bind a target without neutralizing it; some protection also depends on immune cells and other defenses the assay does not measure. The result is a map of part of immune memory, not a score for the whole immune system. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

The map changed after measles. The paper’s abstract summarizes the result as elimination of “11 to 73% of the antibody repertoire across individuals.” More specifically, the children with severe measles lost a median of forty percent of their pre-existing pathogen-specific repertoire, with individual losses from eleven to sixty-two percent. Those with mild measles lost a median of thirty-three percent, with a range from twelve to seventy-three percent. The wide span matters: the effect varied substantially from child to child. The study also found an average reduction of about twenty percent in overall repertoire diversity. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

This was not a simple fall in all antibody classes. Total IgG, IgA and IgM levels did not show a corresponding change. Instead, the pattern suggested that the antibody repertoire had been reshaped: some pathogen-specific recognitions weakened or vanished while measles-specific recognition appeared. Controls without measles generally retained about ninety percent of their measured repertoires over similar or longer intervals. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

The researchers also tested infants before and after their first MMR vaccination. They did not see the same loss of pre-existing antibody recognition in that group. This comparison is important, but it is not a direct trial of every vaccine schedule or every person. It shows that the changes measured after natural measles were not reproduced in the study’s vaccinated infants. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

## Cell memories, not just antibody signals

A second 2019 study looked more directly at B cells. Petrova and colleagues sequenced the genes that encode B-cell receptors in paired blood samples taken before and after measles. The sequences let them track clones: families of B cells descended from a cell that had already recognized a particular target. After infection, previously expanded memory B-cell clones were depleted. The naive B-cell pool also returned in an altered, less mature pattern. Cell counts could recover while the receptor repertoire remained different. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aay6125)

The group’s animal experiments added a functional test. In ferrets infected with a measles-like morbillivirus, vaccine-acquired antibodies against influenza fell, and the animals mounted a weaker recall response when later challenged with influenza. They also developed more severe disease after that challenge. Ferrets are not people, and the virus was a surrogate, but this experiment connected an altered B-cell repertoire to a measurable loss of protection in an animal model. [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aay6125)

Mina and colleagues tested the antibody pattern in another controlled setting. They measured plasma from four rhesus macaques before and five months after experimental measles infection. The overall diversity of recognized epitopes fell by an average of twenty-six percent, with a range of twenty-one to thirty-five percent. Each macaque lost, on average, forty to sixty percent of its pre-existing repertoire, and that loss was still present five months later. Those figures use different denominators: total detected diversity includes new measles targets, while the forty-to-sixty-percent figure compares only targets recognized before infection. A small group of macaques cannot establish the human effect size, but the experiment showed that the pattern was not confined to children sampled during one outbreak. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

A separate line of evidence suggested that the effects may persist beyond recovery. In 2015, Mina and colleagues examined historical measles and childhood infectious-disease mortality records from England and Wales, the United States, and Denmark. They found that deaths from infections other than measles tracked measles incidence with a delay. In England and Wales, their model fit best when measles-related immune effects were spread over roughly twenty-eight months. The authors described an epidemiological “shadow” lasting about two to three years. These were population-level correlations, consistent with prolonged immune damage, not a direct measurement of how long every child’s immune memory takes to rebuild. [[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa3662)

## What the evidence can and cannot say

The human antibody study was longitudinal, but its main cohort was seventy-seven unvaccinated children from one low-vaccination Dutch community. It compared each child before and after infection, and used other control groups; it did not randomly assign measles exposure. Its short follow-up captures early changes, not the full recovery path. The macaque experiment supplies controlled evidence, while the human B-cell and ferret work offers a second line of support. Together they make a strong case that measles can damage immune memory, but they do not make every detail of the human mechanism or long-term clinical risk settled. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aay6125)

Nor should “11 to 73 percent” be read as the percentage of a person’s total immunity erased. It refers to the range of pre-existing pathogen-specific antibody repertoire losses measured by VirScan in those children. The test sampled particular peptide targets; it did not assess every pathogen, every antibody, every T-cell response, or each child’s actual risk of becoming ill. The researchers also detected some antibodies returning after later natural exposure, showing that the measured repertoire can change over time. The study did not establish how reliably or quickly each missing response is restored. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)

The 2015 mortality analysis adds a different kind of evidence. If measles were followed only by short-lived immune suppression, later deaths from unrelated infections should not track earlier measles waves so closely. The lagged association fits the immune-memory hypothesis. Still, correlations in national time series cannot, on their own, prove that antibody loss caused each later death; changes in health care, other infections and population conditions can complicate comparisons. The cell and animal studies help with mechanism, but no single paper answers every question. [[3]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa3662)

## Prevention without the disease

The practical distinction is simple: measles infection exposes immune cells to the wild virus and can damage previously acquired memory; vaccination trains the immune response against measles without the same disease. MMR uses weakened viruses to stimulate protective responses. In Mina’s study, the measured antibody-repertoire loss was not seen after vaccination, while natural infection and experimental infection produced it. Preventing measles therefore avoids the infection-associated route to immune amnesia, in addition to preventing measles itself. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)[[5]](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/about/questions.html)

The World Health Organization recommends two measles-containing vaccine doses and describes vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles. The CDC reports that two MMR doses are about ninety-seven percent effective at preventing measles after exposure. No vaccine protects every person in every circumstance, but the evidence does not support treating measles infection as a safer way to acquire immunity. [[4]](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles)[[5]](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/about/questions.html)

Measles gives the immune system a lasting memory of measles. The cost can be a thinner record of infections that came first. Blood tests made that loss visible; cell sequencing and animal experiments traced some of the changes behind it. The loss is partial, and its consequences vary. Preventing measles protects against the disease and avoids the infection-associated damage to older immune memory. [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6485)[[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aay6125)[[4]](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles)
