A European eel can spend years in a river, lake or coastal lagoon, then leave for the open Atlantic. Its destination lies thousands of kilometres away, in a part of the ocean where the adults that might explain the journey have never been caught spawning. For a long time, the evidence came from the young.

The eel question was old even when Schmidt took it to sea. Aristotle wrote that eels had no reproductive organs and seemed to arise spontaneously from mud and damp ground. It was a plausible answer to a stubborn absence: people caught eels in rivers, but did not see them mate or lay eggs. The animals appeared to come from nowhere, which is one way of saying that the important part of their life happened somewhere people were not looking. [[1]](https://www.loebclassics.com/view/aristotle-history_animals/1965/pb_LCL438.287.xml?readMode=recto)

By the nineteenth century, naturalists were catching small, glassy, leaf-shaped fish called leptocephali. They looked nothing like ordinary eel-shaped young, and had been described as separate species. In 1896, zoologist Giovanni Battista Grassi, working with Salvatore Calandruccio, showed that one of these forms, *Leptocephalus brevirostris*, was the larva of the common eel. The larva shrank and changed shape as it became an elver. A creature that had seemed a different fish turned out to be an early chapter in the eel's life. [[2]](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspl.1896.0045)

That discovery made a much larger search possible. If the larvae could be identified, their sizes and locations might point back toward where they began. Johannes Schmidt took up the work in 1904 aboard the Danish research steamer *Thor*. His first Atlantic clue was a larva caught west of the Faroe Islands. He then worked across the Atlantic, using research cruises and, when a specialised ship could not make the crossing, nets carried by trading vessels. Later surveys aboard the schooner *Dana* added more stations. The method was patient and expansive: collect larvae, identify the species, measure them, and plot where the smallest ones occurred. [[3]](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.1923.0004)

The pattern ran in the direction expected if the larvae drifted east toward Europe as they grew. The smallest European eel larvae were concentrated in the western Atlantic, while larger ones appeared farther east. In his 1923 paper, based on a work read to the Royal Society in 1922, Schmidt placed the breeding grounds in a restricted western Atlantic area north-east and north of the West Indies. This is the result usually shortened to “the Sargasso Sea.” The argument was powerful, but it was an inference from larvae, not an observation of adult eels spawning. Schmidt himself said the area was where the youngest larvae had been found. [[3]](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.1923.0004)

The larvae are remarkable passengers. They begin as small, transparent leptocephali and travel across the Atlantic, growing before metamorphosing into glass eels near European coasts. Their adult lives unfold in fresh or brackish water, but the final migration runs the other way: silver eels leave continental waters and head back toward the oceanic breeding region. The full route from Europe has been estimated at roughly five thousand to ten thousand kilometres. [[4]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/)

That round trip is difficult to watch. The open ocean is vast; eels travel below the surface and may cross depths that challenge a tag's pressure limits. A pop-up satellite archival tag records conditions while attached, then detaches and surfaces to transmit stored data. It is a compromise: the tag must be small enough to attach to an eel, stay attached, survive the journey, and eventually reach the surface. Tags can detach early, stop reporting, exceed their depth range, or be carried by a predator. A tag's final position therefore needs to be interpreted alongside its temperature, depth and movement records. Earlier tracking could show eels leaving European waters and moving in the right direction, but had not recorded adults reaching the presumed breeding area. [[4]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/)

In 2018 and 2019, Rosalind Wright and colleagues tagged twenty-six female silver eels from the Azores, islands much closer to the Sargasso Sea than continental Europe. Twenty-one tags recorded substantial data before detaching, between forty and three hundred sixty-six days after release. The paths pointed southwest. Five tags surfaced inside the Sargasso Sea's boundaries; one surfaced within the area where the smallest larvae had been recorded. That was the first direct evidence of adult European eels reaching the presumed breeding region. [[4]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/)

The distinction in that sentence matters. The researchers tracked adults to the area; they did not watch them spawn. None of the tagged eels moved fast enough to arrive within the same year's observed spawning period. They may take a slower migration and arrive for a later season, but that remains a hypothesis. No wild European eel eggs or spawning adults have yet been sampled there. The tags closed a gap in the route, not the whole mystery. [[4]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/)

The study also began from the Azores, not from a river in northern Europe. Its authors reasoned that European eels growing in the archipelago would use the same final route as eels arriving from the continent, but the tag tracks do not by themselves establish every part of the continental migration. Nor do they settle the exact boundaries or timing of spawning. Tracking tells us where an instrument travelled with an eel; it cannot turn a presumed breeding ground into a witnessed spawning event. [[4]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/)

That caution sits beside a less comfortable fact: the European eel is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List, with a decreasing population trend. The IUCN assessment describes declines in recruitment and identifies threats across the eel's complicated life cycle, including barriers, fishing, habitat loss, pollution and changes in ocean conditions. The exact contribution of each threat varies and remains difficult to separate. [[5]](https://doi.org/10.2305/iucn.uk.2020-2.rlts.t60344a152845178.en)

For a fish whose life crosses rivers, coastlines and an ocean basin, even the most basic question—where does it reproduce?—has demanded a century of nets, measurements and patient tracking. Schmidt narrowed the search by following the youngest larvae backward. Wright's team then showed adult eels arriving in the region those larvae had pointed to. Somewhere beyond the tag's last transmission, the final part is still out of sight.
